# John 5 · The Son Who Gives Life (KJV)
> Unit john-05 · John 5:1–47 · loop john-kjv-study v2.0 · DRAFT for human doctrine review

## 🧭 At a Glance
### 🧵 Golden Thread
The Son works as the Father works, gives life to whom he will, and will judge all, so whoever hears his word and believes has passed from death to life.

### 🌗 Life & Light Line
John 5 answers the death of a man who lay helpless for thirty-eight years with 'no man' to help him, and the deeper death of hearers who searched the Scriptures and would not come to Christ, with the Son who 'quickeneth whom he will' (John 5:21), so that whoever hears his word and believes 'is passed from death unto life' (John 5:24). It exposes the darkness of a religion that could see a sabbath rule broken and not see a man made whole, and of hearts that love honour from one another, and it gives the light of the Scriptures opened, which 'testify of me' (John 5:39).

- Name the five witnesses Jesus calls in John 5:31–47 (John the Baptist, his works, the Father, the Scriptures, and Moses), and explain why each one points to him.

## 🏛️ Core Foundations
### ✝️ Christological Root
**Gospel Foundation:** The chapter rests on what Jesus did at the pool and what he said when he was charged for it. He healed a man who had been ill for thirty-eight years with a word: "Jesus saith unto him, Rise, take up thy bed, and walk." (John 5:8) When he was accused of breaking the sabbath he answered: "But Jesus answered them, My Father worketh hitherto, and I work." (John 5:17) And he explained what that work is: "For as the Father raiseth up the dead, and quickeneth them; even so the Son quickeneth whom he will." (John 5:21) Then comes the promise that stands at the centre of the chapter: "Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life, and shall not come into condemnation; but is passed from death unto life." (John 5:24)

**The Bridge:** The healing at Bethesda is a sign, and Jesus himself interprets it. The word that made a helpless man stand is the same word that makes dead souls live now (John 5:25) and will call the dead out of their graves at the last (John 5:28–29). The doctrines grounded here are the full deity of the Son, equal with the Father in work, life, judgment, and honour (John 5:17–23, 26), and the gift of eternal life, received now by hearing his word and believing (John 5:24). His opponents understood the claim exactly: he was "making himself equal with God" (John 5:18).

**Canonical Echoes:** Jesus (John 5:21) → Promise (Deuteronomy 32:39; Daniel 12:2) → Doctrine (Ephesians 2:5) → Life (1 John 3:14)

**What Gets Lost:** Taught without Christ at the centre, John 5 becomes a lesson in sabbath etiquette (the Pharisees were too strict), a story about taking responsibility for your own healing ('get up and walk'), or a debate about angels and pools. Each of those misses the point that Jesus himself draws out. The man did not get up because he finally tried harder; he had 'no man' (John 5:7), and he rose at a word. The chapter is not about the pool or the rules; it is about the Son who does what only God does. Remove Christ and John 5 becomes advice for the helpless, which is no help at all; keep him at the centre and it becomes life for the dead.

### ⚔️ Spiritual Warfare
**The Lie:** You can have God without the Son. You can read the Bible, keep the rules, honour the Father, and be counted religious, all without coming to Jesus for life. Its second form is quieter: faith can wait until you have secured the good opinion of the people around you.

**The Truth:** God will not be honoured apart from his Son: "That all men should honour the Son, even as they honour the Father. He that honoureth not the Son honoureth not the Father which hath sent him." (John 5:23) And the Scriptures themselves send us to him: "they are they which testify of me" (John 5:39).

**The Battleground:** Two places. The first is the mind that knows Scripture well and uses it as a place to rest rather than a road to Christ: 'in them ye think ye have eternal life' (John 5:39). The second is the heart that lives on approval, where faith is choked because we 'receive honour one of another' (John 5:44).

**The Weapon:** The word of the Son, heard and believed. The man at the pool rose at his word (John 5:8); the spiritually dead 'shall hear the voice of the Son of God' and live (John 5:25). The believer fights with the Scriptures read as God meant them to be read, as witnesses to Jesus (John 5:39, 46), and with the honour that comes from God only (John 5:44).

### 🌱 Life Pathway
**The Theft:** In John 5 the thief steals by long waiting and by isolation. A man lies by a pool for thirty-eight years; his hope has shrunk to a race he can never win, and his complaint is the loneliest sentence in the chapter: "Sir, I have no man, when the water is troubled, to put me into the pool" (John 5:7). The thief also steals through religion. The authorities meet a man walking for the first time in decades and see only a bed carried on the wrong day (John 5:10). And he steals from the Bible readers themselves, who search the Scriptures for life and 'will not come to me, that ye might have life' (John 5:40).

**The Source:** The Son of God, who has 'life in himself' (John 5:26) and 'quickeneth whom he will' (John 5:21). He came "that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly" (John 10:10). His means in this chapter are his spoken word ('Rise, take up thy bed, and walk', John 5:8), the voice by which the dead hear and live (John 5:25), and the Scriptures that testify of him (John 5:39).

**The Channel:** The man had 'no man'. A believer can be that man, not by carrying people to a pool but by bringing them where they can hear the Son's voice, as four friends once carried a paralysed man to Jesus: "And they come unto him, bringing one sick of the palsy, which was borne of four." (Mark 2:3) John the Baptist shows the other half of the channel: he was 'a burning and a shining light' (John 5:35), and he bore witness to the truth (John 5:33). He was not the life; he pointed to it.

**The Abundance Guard:** Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, not prosperity, ease, or freedom from suffering. The healed man was not promised an easy life; he was warned to 'sin no more' (John 5:14), and the healing led at once to trouble with the authorities. The life Christ guarantees is 'everlasting life' (John 5:24), resurrection life (John 5:29), and he gave the man's legs back as a sign of that, not as a promise that every illness will be lifted now.

### 💡 Light Pathway
**The Darkness:** John 5 exposes a religious darkness. The authorities had the Scriptures, the sabbath, and Moses, and yet they had 'not his word abiding' in them (John 5:38) and did not have 'the love of God' in them (John 5:42). They could see a rule broken and not see a man made whole (John 5:10). Their deepest blindness was the love of human honour: "How can ye believe, which receive honour one of another, and seek not the honour that cometh from God only?" (John 5:44)

**The Source:** Christ himself, the one to whom Moses, the prophets, John, the works, and the Father all bear witness (John 5:33–39, 46). He is "the light of the world" (John 8:12). "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." (2 Corinthians 4:6)

**The Lamp:** Jesus calls John the Baptist a lamp: 'a burning and a shining light' (John 5:35), where the word is *luchnos* (G3088, John 5:35), a lamp. A lamp is lit from another and shines for another. The lamp in this chapter is the Scriptures opened so that they show Christ ('he wrote of me', John 5:46), the truth told about ourselves, the honour we crave named and confessed, and the unfruitful works of darkness reproved (Ephesians 5:11), beginning with our own, with good works that glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16).

**The Love Guard:** Light without love is glare. The Pharisees in this chapter used the law as a spotlight on a healed man's bed and missed the man. Jesus' words to his opponents are severe, but they are spoken 'that ye might be saved' (John 5:34). We turn the lamp first on our own love of honour and our own unbelief, and when we speak of anyone else's darkness we do it, as he did, to bring them to life, never to win the argument or shame them in public.

### 🏺 Setting the Scene
John 5 opens with Jesus going up to Jerusalem for 'a feast of the Jews' (John 5:1). John does not say which feast, and readers have suggested Passover, Pentecost, Tabernacles, Trumpets, and Purim; none can be proved, and it does not change the story. Jerusalem at a feast was crowded with pilgrims. Near the sheep gate in the north wall (the gate Nehemiah's builders repaired, Nehemiah 3:1), there was a pool surrounded by colonnaded porches, where the sick gathered. Archaeologists working near the Church of St Anne in the old city have uncovered two large pools with the remains of colonnades, which many identify as Bethesda: a colonnade on each of the four sides and a fifth across the wall dividing the two pools would give 'five porches' (verify the details of the excavation). The name is spelled differently in different manuscripts (Bethesda, Bethzatha, Bethsaida); the KJV reads Bethesda.

The sabbath was God's gift to Israel, a day of rest from work (Exodus 20:10) and a sign of his covenant (Exodus 31:13). The prophets rebuked Israel for breaking it, and Jeremiah told the people to 'bear no burden on the sabbath day' (Jeremiah 17:21). By Jesus' day the teachers of the law had worked out in detail what counted as work, so that the command could be kept exactly. The Mishnah, written down about AD 200 but drawing on older teaching, lists thirty-nine main kinds of forbidden work, the last being carrying an object from one domain into another (Mishnah *Shabbat* 7.2, verify). A man carrying his bed through the streets would have looked to them like an open breach.

In John's Gospel, 'the Jews' often means the religious authorities in Jerusalem who opposed Jesus, not the Jewish people as a whole; Jesus, his disciples, the healed man, and John himself were Jews. John 5 is the point in the Gospel where their opposition turns to open persecution and the first mention of a plan to kill him (John 5:16, 18). It is also the first long speech in which Jesus defends his identity, and he does it like a man in a courtroom: he states his claim, and then calls his witnesses.

#### John 5:1–9 · At the Pool of Bethesda
**1** After this there was a feast of the Jews; and Jesus went up to Jerusalem.

**2** Now there is at Jerusalem by the sheep market a pool, which is called in the Hebrew tongue Bethesda, having five porches.

**3** In these lay a great multitude of impotent folk, of blind, halt, withered, waiting for the moving of the water.

**4** For an angel went down at a certain season into the pool, and troubled the water: whosoever then first after the troubling of the water stepped in was made whole of whatsoever disease he had.

**5** And a certain man was there, which had an infirmity thirty and eight years.

**6** When Jesus saw him lie, and knew that he had been now a long time in that case, he saith unto him, Wilt thou be made whole?

**7** The impotent man answered him, Sir, I have no man, when the water is troubled, to put me into the pool: but while I am coming, another steppeth down before me.

**8** Jesus saith unto him, Rise, take up thy bed, and walk.

**9** And immediately the man was made whole, and took up his bed, and walked: and on the same day was the sabbath.

**10** The Jews therefore said unto him that was cured, It is the sabbath day: it is not lawful for thee to carry thy bed.

**11** He answered them, He that made me whole, the same said unto me, Take up thy bed, and walk.

**12** Then asked they him, What man is that which said unto thee, Take up thy bed, and walk?

**View A:** 'Search the scriptures' (John 5:39) is a command, as the KJV prints it. Jesus sends his opponents back to the Bible they claim to trust, because it testifies of him. Scripture elsewhere commands the same thing: "Seek ye out of the book of the LORD, and read" (Isaiah 34:16), and the Bereans were commended because they "searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so" (Acts 17:11).

**View B:** The words are a statement, and many modern translations render them *Ye search the scriptures*. The Greek form can be read either way. On this reading Jesus is not telling them to start searching; he is saying that they already search diligently, and yet miss the point: "And ye will not come to me, that ye might have life." (John 5:40) Paul says the same of his own people: "For I bear them record that they have a zeal of God, but not according to knowledge." (Romans 10:2)

**Q12.** Which view does the text best support, and why? Before you answer, read John 5:39–40 together, and ask whether the words that follow 'Search the scriptures' sound more like instructions or like a description of what the hearers were already doing.

### 👣 Case Study · Scripture in Action
Saul of Tarsus was exactly the kind of man Jesus addressed in John 5. He searched the Scriptures as few men of his generation did, and he was proud of it: he "profited in the Jews' religion above many my equals in mine own nation, being more exceedingly zealous of the traditions of my fathers." (Galatians 1:14) Like the authorities who 'did the Jews persecute Jesus' after the healing at Bethesda (John 5:16), Saul turned his zeal against the followers of Jesus. On the road to Damascus the Son of God spoke to him, and the dead man heard his voice: "And he fell to the earth, and heard a voice saying unto him, Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?" (Acts 9:4) Afterward the same Scriptures he had searched became the book he used to show others Christ: in Thessalonica he "reasoned with them out of the scriptures" (Acts 17:2), proving that Jesus is the Christ. And the honour he had gathered from men he now counted worthless: "But what things were gain to me, those I counted loss for Christ." (Philippians 3:7)

**Q13.** What changed in Saul, and what did not change? He still searched the Scriptures; what was different about why and how he read them, and where do you see the same danger or the same change in your own Bible reading (John 5:39–40, 44)?

### 🏙️ Modern Example
Walter had taught the men's Sunday school class at his church for twenty-two years. He had read the Bible through more times than he could count and could quote whole chapters. He was respected, and he liked being respected. When his wife died, the respect was no help. He kept teaching, but at home he found that the verses he knew so well felt like a locked room. One Tuesday a younger man in his class, a new believer, asked him to read John 5 with him. When they reached verse 39, the young man said, 'That is you, Walter, isn't it? You know where all the verses are.' Walter was stung, and then he could not sleep. He saw that for years he had loved the Bible's honour, and the class's honour, more than he had come to Christ himself for life. The next Sunday he told the class so, briefly and plainly. Then he asked them to pray for him, and he began to read the Gospels again, asking of every page one question: *what does this show me of Jesus, and will I come to him?*

**Q14.** Where could you be searching the Scriptures, or doing something good and religious, without actually coming to Christ for life (John 5:39–40)? What would it look like to come to him this week?

### 📜 Voices from Church History
In the fourth century the church faced a teacher named Arius, who said that the Son of God was the first and highest of God's creatures, but not God in the same sense as the Father: there was a time, his followers said, when the Son was not. The bishops who gathered at Nicaea in 325, and again at Constantinople in 381, answered with the creed that most churches still confess, the Nicene Creed. Summarized in plain words (not quoted): it confesses one God the Father Almighty, maker of all things; and one Lord Jesus Christ, the only Son of God, begotten of the Father before all ages, true God from true God, not made, and of one being with the Father, through whom all things were made; who came down for our salvation, was made man, was crucified, rose again, and will come again to judge the living and the dead. The defenders of that creed, above all Athanasius, turned again and again to texts like John 5: the Son does whatever the Father does (John 5:19), has life in himself as the Father does (John 5:26), and must be honoured as the Father is (John 5:23). A creature could claim none of these things.

**Q15.** Why did the church insist that the Son is fully God and not the highest creature? Using John 5:18–26, explain what would be lost for your salvation if Jesus were less than God.

### ⛪ Doctrine in My Own Words
- **Theology Proper:** The Father never stops working: he upholds all things, gives life, and judges, and he works through his Son (John 5:17, 21).
- **Theology Proper:** God himself testifies to his Son through John, through the works, through his own voice, and through the Scriptures (John 5:32–39).
- **Christology:** Jesus is equal with God: he does all the Father does and must be honoured as the Father is honoured (John 5:18–23).
- **Christology:** The Son has life in himself, as the Father does, and gives life to whom he will (John 5:21, 26).
- **Anthropology & Sin:** Apart from Christ people are spiritually dead, and they will not come to him, because they love honour from one another more than the honour that comes from God (John 5:25, 40, 44).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** Whoever hears Christ's word and believes the Father who sent him has everlasting life now, will not come into condemnation, and has passed from death to life (John 5:24).
- **Eschatology:** The hour has already come when the spiritually dead hear the Son and live, and an hour is coming when all in the graves will hear him and rise, to life or to judgment, with the Son as Judge (John 5:25–29).
- **Christian Walk:** The one Christ has made whole is called to sin no more and to seek the honour that comes from God only (John 5:14, 44).

### 👑 Christocentric & Humility · Level 4
Every problem in John 5 is answered in Jesus. A man has lain helpless for thirty-eight years, and a word from Jesus makes him whole. He has 'no man' to help him, and the Son of God comes to him and asks, 'Wilt thou be made whole?' He does not know who healed him, and Jesus goes and finds him. The authorities see only a broken rule, and Jesus shows them the Father at work on the sabbath. People are dead toward God, and the Son quickens whom he will. All must face judgment, and the Judge is the one who gives life to those who hear him. The Scriptures seemed to his opponents a place to find life, and Jesus shows that they are witnesses pointing to him. Moses, the hope of his hearers, becomes their accuser, and the one Moses wrote of stands in front of them offering life. Every truth in the chapter is found in him too: the work of God, the life of God, the judgment of God, the honour due to God, and the meaning of the Scriptures all meet in the Son.

The chapter humbles us from several sides. It humbles the helpless, because the man did nothing to earn his healing and could not even name his healer. It humbles the religious, because the people who knew the Bible best did not come to the one it was written about. It humbles the admired, because Jesus puts his finger on the love of human honour as the thing that makes faith impossible. And it makes Christ glorious, because the one who 'can do nothing of himself' (John 5:19) in perfect submission to his Father is the same one who does 'what things soever' the Father does, and before whom every person will stand.

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy
### 📌 Core Concept
In Jerusalem there was a pool where many sick people lay, hoping to be healed. One man had been sick for thirty-eight years, longer than most of his neighbours had been alive. He could not get into the water by himself, and nobody helped him. Jesus came to him and asked, 'Do you want to be made well?' Then Jesus told him, 'Get up, pick up your bed, and walk', and the man did, straight away. But it was the day of rest, and some religious leaders were angry that he was carrying his bed. They were even angrier when Jesus said that God was his Father and that he was working just as his Father works. They understood that Jesus was saying he was equal with God. Jesus did not take it back. He said that he gives life to dead people, that one day he will judge everyone, and that anyone who listens to him and believes God has life for ever. He also said that the whole Bible points to him, and that many people who read it still would not come to him.

### 📖 Key Verses
"Jesus saith unto him, Rise, take up thy bed, and walk." (John 5:8) Jesus' word was strong enough to make a man walk who had not walked in thirty-eight years.

"For as the Father raiseth up the dead, and quickeneth them; even so the Son quickeneth whom he will." (John 5:21) Jesus can give life, just as God the Father can, because he is God the Son.

"Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life, and shall not come into condemnation; but is passed from death unto life." (John 5:24) If you listen to Jesus and trust God, you have a life with God that starts now and never ends.

"Search the scriptures; for in them ye think ye have eternal life: and they are they which testify of me." (John 5:39) The whole Bible is about Jesus, so when we read it we should look for him.

### 🪞 Analogy
Imagine a boy whose kite is stuck high in a tree. He jumps, he throws sticks, he waits for the wind to blow it down, but nothing works, day after day. Then his father comes, reaches up, and hands it to him. The boy did not get his kite back because he finally jumped high enough; he got it back because someone stronger came. The man at the pool was like that. He was waiting for the water to move and for somebody to help him in, and it never worked. Then Jesus came, and he did not need the pool at all. He just spoke, and the man was well. That is how Jesus gives life to us too: not because we finally become strong enough, but because he is strong enough.

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium
### 📜 Walkthrough
#### John 5:1–4 · A Feast, a Pool, and a Multitude
**Says:** "After this there was a feast of the Jews; and Jesus went up to Jerusalem." (John 5:1) "Now there is at Jerusalem by the sheep market a pool, which is called in the Hebrew tongue Bethesda, having five porches." (John 5:2) "In these lay a great multitude of impotent folk, of blind, halt, withered, waiting for the moving of the water." (John 5:3) "For an angel went down at a certain season into the pool, and troubled the water: whosoever then first after the troubling of the water stepped in was made whole of whatsoever disease he had." (John 5:4) *Impotent* means weak, disabled, without strength; *halt* means lame; *withered* means shrivelled or paralysed; *troubled* means stirred.

**Means:** John does not name the feast. That is unusual in this Gospel, which names the Passovers (John 2:13; John 6:4; John 11:55), Tabernacles (John 7:2), and Dedication (John 10:22). Readers have suggested nearly every feast of the year; John's silence suggests that the feast is only the occasion, and that the day that matters is the sabbath (John 5:9). Jesus goes up to Jerusalem as a faithful Jew.

The pool lay 'by the sheep market'. The Greek has the adjective *probatikē*, from *probatikos* (G4262, John 5:2), 'of sheep', with a noun understood. The STEPBible lexicon (Tyndale House, Cambridge, whose Greek entries draw largely on Abbott-Smith) supplies 'gate', the sheep gate of Nehemiah's day: "they builded the sheep gate" (Nehemiah 3:1). The KJV supplies 'market'. Either way it was near the temple, on the north side of the city, where sheep for sacrifice were brought in. The pool is a *kolumbēthra* (G2861, John 5:2), a pool for swimming or bathing; the word occurs 5 times in the New Testament, all in John, here and at Siloam (John 9:7, 11). It had five *stoas* (G4745, John 5:2), porches or colonnades, roofed walkways where people could lie in the shade. The name Bethesda is often explained as 'house of mercy'; the STEPBible lexicon says its derivation is uncertain, and the manuscripts spell it in more than one way.

'A great multitude of impotent folk': the porches were full. John lists them: the blind, the lame, the withered. Isaiah had promised a day when "the lame man leap as an hart" (Isaiah 35:6), and here the lame lie waiting.

The KJV continues with the end of verse 3 and verse 4, which explain what they were waiting for: an angel stirred the water from time to time, and the first to step in was healed. These words are not in several of the oldest manuscripts, and many modern editions print them as a footnote. This study teaches the text the KJV prints and explains the question at Tier 3 (see the Original Language Deep Dive and the Council's second question). For the reader here, two things are worth noticing. First, verse 7 assumes the troubling of the water, whichever way the question is settled: the sick man believes that healing comes to the first one in. Second, the arrangement is cruel. It rewards the least disabled of the disabled. A man who cannot move is the one man it can never help.

**Asks:** What 'pools' do people wait beside today, hoping that one day they will be first in: a program, a treatment, a lucky break, a religious ritual? Notice the multitude, as Jesus did. And ask whether you are waiting for something to move, when Christ himself has come near.

#### John 5:5–9 · Wilt Thou Be Made Whole?
**Says:** "And a certain man was there, which had an infirmity thirty and eight years." (John 5:5) "When Jesus saw him lie, and knew that he had been now a long time in that case, he saith unto him, Wilt thou be made whole?" (John 5:6) "The impotent man answered him, Sir, I have no man, when the water is troubled, to put me into the pool: but while I am coming, another steppeth down before me." (John 5:7) "Jesus saith unto him, Rise, take up thy bed, and walk." (John 5:8) "And immediately the man was made whole, and took up his bed, and walked: and on the same day was the sabbath." (John 5:9) *Infirmity* means sickness or weakness; *in that case* means in that condition; *made whole* means healed, made healthy.

**Means:** Out of the multitude, Jesus goes to one man. His *astheneia* (G769, John 5:5), weakness or sickness, had lasted thirty-eight years. John does not say what it was, only that he could not get into the pool quickly. Some readers note that Israel wandered in the wilderness for thirty-eight years after Kadesh-barnea, 'until all the generation of the men of war were wasted out' (Deuteronomy 2:14), and see a picture of Israel's long unbelief; John makes nothing of the number, and it may simply show how hopeless the case was. It was longer than many people then lived.

Jesus 'saw him lie, and knew'. John does not say how he knew; in this Gospel Jesus knows what is in people (John 2:25; John 4:18). Then he asks what seems an obvious question: 'Wilt thou be made whole?' The verb is *theleis* (G2309, John 5:6, V-PAI-2S), 'do you want, do you will?', and 'whole' is *hugiēs* (G5199, John 5:6), healthy, sound. The word runs through this story: *hugiēs* occurs 7 times in John, six of them in this chapter and once when Jesus refers back to this healing (John 7:23). The question is not cruel. After thirty-eight years a man can stop hoping, or build his life around his illness; Jesus draws out the longing and fixes it on himself.

The man does not say yes. He explains why it cannot happen: 'Sir, I have no man'. He calls Jesus *kurie* (G2962, John 5:7), the polite 'Sir'; he has no idea who is speaking. His whole hope is tied to the water and to help he does not have. He is not asking Jesus for healing; he may be hoping, at most, that this stranger will help him into the pool next time.

Jesus does not help him into the pool. He bypasses it entirely with three commands: *rise, take up, walk*. The bed is a *krabbatos* (G2895, John 5:8), which the STEPBible lexicon explains as 'a camp bed, pallet', a light mat that a man could roll up and carry. 'And immediately the man was made whole': no process, no pool, no angel, no race. The word that commands is the word that enables. The man who could not move takes up his mat and walks.

Only then does John add, almost as an afterthought, the detail that drives the rest of the chapter: 'and on the same day was the sabbath.' This is the third of the signs that the church has traditionally counted in John, after the water made wine and the nobleman's son (John 2:11; John 4:54). John himself numbers only the first two. Like the second, this healing is done by a word alone.

**Asks:** Where have you said, in effect, 'I have no man'? Christ does not need the pool, the right conditions, or the right helpers. Bring him your longest-standing need honestly, and let him ask you, 'Wilt thou be made whole?'

#### John 5:10–13 · It Is Not Lawful
**Says:** "The Jews therefore said unto him that was cured, It is the sabbath day: it is not lawful for thee to carry thy bed." (John 5:10) "He answered them, He that made me whole, the same said unto me, Take up thy bed, and walk." (John 5:11) "Then asked they him, What man is that which said unto thee, Take up thy bed, and walk?" (John 5:12) "And he that was healed wist not who it was: for Jesus had conveyed himself away, a multitude being in that place." (John 5:13) *Wist* means knew; *conveyed himself away* means slipped away quietly.

**Means:** The first people the healed man meets are 'the Jews', the religious authorities, and their first words are not 'Praise God' but 'It is not lawful'. They are thinking of the sabbath command, and perhaps of Jeremiah: "Take heed to yourselves, and bear no burden on the sabbath day, nor bring it in by the gates of Jerusalem" (Jeremiah 17:21). Jeremiah was speaking of trade and ordinary labour carried on as if the sabbath did not exist (Jeremiah 17:22–27). The teachers of Jesus' day had turned such commands into detailed rules, and carrying an object from one place to another was on the list of forbidden work (see Setting the Scene). A man carrying a mat the day he was healed from thirty-eight years of illness did not count as an exception.

The man's defence is simple: the one who made me whole told me to. Whoever has the power to heal, he reasons, has the authority to command. The authorities ask the obvious next question, and the way they frame it is telling: not 'who healed you?' but 'What man is that which said unto thee'? They are interested in the command, not the healing.

He does not know. John uses the old English *wist*, the past tense of *wit*, to know. Jesus had 'conveyed himself away', *exeneusen* (G1593, John 5:13, V-AAI-3S); the STEPBible lexicon gives the senses 'to bend the head aside', 'to shun, avoid', and 'to withdraw', listing this verse under the last. In the crowd he slipped away. The healing was not a performance.

**Asks:** When you see God do something good in someone's life, is your first instinct to rejoice, or to check whether they did it properly? Ask the Lord to give you eyes for the man made whole before the rule broken.

#### John 5:14–16 · Sin No More
**Says:** "Afterward Jesus findeth him in the temple, and said unto him, Behold, thou art made whole: sin no more, lest a worse thing come unto thee." (John 5:14) "The man departed, and told the Jews that it was Jesus, which had made him whole." (John 5:15) "And therefore did the Jews persecute Jesus, and sought to slay him, because he had done these things on the sabbath day." (John 5:16) *Findeth* means finds; *slay* means kill.

**Means:** The healed man goes to the temple, *hierō* (G2411, John 5:14), perhaps to give thanks. Jesus 'findeth him': the Shepherd seeks out the one he has healed. Twice more in John Jesus finds people in this way (John 1:43; John 9:35), and the second is a close parallel to this chapter.

'Behold, thou art made whole': the healing is real and complete. Then a warning: 'sin no more', *hamartane* (G264, John 5:14, V-PAM-2S), a present imperative with a word meaning 'no longer': stop sinning, do not go on in it. 'Lest a worse thing come unto thee': *cheiron* (G5501, John 5:14), worse. Does Jesus mean that the man's illness was caused by a particular sin? The text does not say so. Scripture does teach that some illness comes as the Lord's discipline for sin: "For this cause many are weak and sickly among you, and many sleep." (1 Corinthians 11:30) But Jesus flatly denies that all suffering is punishment for a particular sin: "Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him." (John 9:3) Here Jesus speaks to one man whose heart he knows, and he points beyond the illness to something worse than thirty-eight years of weakness. The rest of the chapter names it: 'the resurrection of damnation' (John 5:29). The same words, 'sin no more', close the story of the woman taken in adultery (John 8:11); in both, mercy comes first and the call to holiness follows.

Then the man 'departed, and told the Jews that it was Jesus'. The verb is *anēngeilen* (G312, John 5:15, V-AAI-3S), he reported, announced. Readers take this two ways. Some see an informer, contrasting him with the blind man of John 9, who stood up to the same authorities and was cast out (John 9:34). Others see a man doing what he was asked, or even bearing witness to his healer, since he names Jesus as the one 'which had made him whole', not as the one who told him to carry his bed. John does not tell us his motive. He does tell us the result: 'therefore did the Jews persecute Jesus'. The verb *ediōkon* (G1377, John 5:16, V-IAI-3P) is an imperfect: they began, and kept on, persecuting him. The KJV adds 'and sought to slay him', words found in the Textus Receptus. Jesus 'had done these things', and the imperfect of the underlying verb may suggest a pattern of sabbath healings, not one act (compare John 7:21–23).

**Asks:** Has Christ made you whole in some way? Receive his mercy first, then hear his call: sin no more. Do not treat grace as permission. And ask what you do with his name when the pressure comes: do you name him as the one who made you whole?

#### John 5:17–18 · My Father Worketh Hitherto
**Says:** "But Jesus answered them, My Father worketh hitherto, and I work." (John 5:17) "Therefore the Jews sought the more to kill him, because he not only had broken the sabbath, but said also that God was his Father, making himself equal with God." (John 5:18) *Hitherto* means until now.

**Means:** Jesus' answer is seven words in English and one of the boldest sentences in the Gospels. God rested on the seventh day of creation: "And on the seventh day God ended his work which he had made; and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had made." (Genesis 2:2) But God's rest was the rest of a finished creation, not the idleness of a God who stopped working. He still upholds all things, sends rain, causes children to be born, gives life, and judges, every day of the week, sabbaths included. Jewish teachers of the period discussed this very point; Philo of Alexandria and later rabbinic writings (verify the particular passages) held that God never ceases to work, and that on the sabbath he continues in particular to give life and to judge. Jesus claims that the Father's work continues 'hitherto', until now, *ergazetai* (G2038, John 5:17, V-PNI-3S), a present, 'is working', and then adds 'and I work'. Whatever the Father may do on the sabbath, the Son may do.

Notice 'My Father', not 'our Father'. The authorities heard it exactly: he 'said also that God was his Father', *patera idion*, with *idion* (G2398, John 5:18), 'his own', his own Father in a unique sense. From that they drew the conclusion: 'making himself equal with God', *ison* (G2470, John 5:18). The STEPBible lexicon defines *isos* as 'equal, the same in size, number, quality', and lists this verse beside Philippians 2:6, where Paul says that Christ "thought it not robbery to be equal with God" (Philippians 2:6).

John tells us two charges: 'he not only had broken the sabbath', where the verb is *eluen* (G3089, John 5:18, V-IAI-3S), an imperfect of 'loose', he was loosening the sabbath, and 'but said also that God was his Father'. Is John saying, as narrator, that Jesus broke the sabbath, or is he reporting how the authorities saw it? The Council's first question takes this up; the short answer is that John gives their reason for wanting to kill him, and the rest of the Gospel shows that Jesus kept his Father's law and did not keep their rules (John 7:22–24). What John certainly does not do is correct their understanding of the second charge. They understood him, and the next thirteen verses confirm it.

**Asks:** Jesus did not say 'I am a good man doing good things'. He said that his Father is working until now, and that he works too (John 5:17). Face the claim squarely. Either he is equal with God, or he is not worth following. Do you honour him as the one who does the Father's work?

#### John 5:19–20 · The Son Can Do Nothing of Himself
**Says:** "Then answered Jesus and said unto them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, The Son can do nothing of himself, but what he seeth the Father do: for what things soever he doeth, these also doeth the Son likewise." (John 5:19) "For the Father loveth the Son, and sheweth him all things that himself doeth: and he will shew him greater works than these, that ye may marvel." (John 5:20) *Soever* means whatever; *sheweth* means shows; *marvel* means wonder, be amazed.

**Means:** 'Verily, verily' introduces a solemn statement; it comes 3 times in this chapter (John 5:19, 24, 25). Jesus does not back away from the charge of equality. He explains it.

First, 'The Son can do nothing of himself'. This sounds like a denial of power, but it is a claim of perfect unity. The Son never acts independently of the Father, as a rival god would; he does 'what he seeth the Father do'. There is one will and one work. Second, 'what things soever he doeth, these also doeth the Son likewise'. No prophet or angel could say that. Moses did some things God commanded; the Son does everything the Father does, in the same way. The limitation in the first half of the verse is a limitation no mere creature could claim, because it is balanced by a scope no mere creature could claim.

The reason is love: 'For the Father loveth the Son', *philei* (G5368, John 5:20, V-PAI-3S), and hides nothing from him: he 'sheweth him all things'. The Son's knowledge of the Father is total, because the Father's love for the Son is total (compare John 3:35, where the verb is *agapaō*). And there is more to come: 'greater works than these', greater than healing a lame man. The next verses name them: raising the dead and judging all people. 'That ye may marvel': the authorities who marvelled with hostility will see things that leave them speechless, whether they believe or not.

**Asks:** The Son's glory is not independence but perfect union with his Father. Ask where you prize independence from God, and pray to live, in your small measure, as the Son did: seeing what the Father does and doing it.

#### John 5:21–23 · Life, Judgment, and Honour
**Says:** "For as the Father raiseth up the dead, and quickeneth them; even so the Son quickeneth whom he will." (John 5:21) "For the Father judgeth no man, but hath committed all judgment unto the Son:" (John 5:22) "That all men should honour the Son, even as they honour the Father. He that honoureth not the Son honoureth not the Father which hath sent him." (John 5:23) *Quickeneth* means makes alive; *committed* means entrusted, handed over.

**Means:** Here are the 'greater works'. The first is giving life. The Old Testament reserves this for God alone: "I kill, and I make alive; I wound, and I heal" (Deuteronomy 32:39). When a king of Israel was asked to heal a leper, he tore his clothes and cried, "Am I God, to kill and to make alive" (2 Kings 5:7)? Jesus claims it without hesitation: 'even so the Son quickeneth whom he will'. The verb is *zōopoiei* (G2227, John 5:21, V-PAI-3S); the STEPBible lexicon defines *zōopoieō* as 'to make alive, cause to live, quicken', and lists Romans 4:17, where God 'quickeneth the dead'. And the Son gives life 'whom he will', as sovereignly as the Father. The man at the pool is a small picture: of the multitude lying there, Jesus chose one.

The second is judgment. 'The Father judgeth no man, but hath committed all judgment unto the Son': *krisin* (G2920, John 5:22), judgment, decision; the STEPBible lexicon lists this chapter under 'the Divine judgment'. The Father has not stepped back from judgment in the sense that he no longer cares; he exercises it through the Son. The judge of all the earth will have a human face.

The purpose is honour: 'That all men should honour the Son, even as they honour the Father'. The verb is *timōsin* (G5091, John 5:23), from *timaō*, 'to honour'. God says through Isaiah, "I am the LORD: that is my name: and my glory will I not give to another" (Isaiah 42:8). If the Father commands that the Son receive the same honour he receives, the Son is not 'another'. The second half closes the door on every religion that claims to honour God while setting Jesus aside: 'He that honoureth not the Son honoureth not the Father which hath sent him.'

**Asks:** Do you honour the Son as you honour the Father, in worship, trust, and obedience? Or do you treat him as a helpful teacher on the way to God? Jesus leaves no middle ground. And if he quickens whom he will, pray for the dead around you with hope.

#### John 5:24 · Passed from Death unto Life
**Says:** "Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life, and shall not come into condemnation; but is passed from death unto life." (John 5:24) *Condemnation* here means judgment leading to condemnation.

**Means:** This single verse is the heart of the chapter and its memory verse. It has two conditions and three results.

The conditions: 'He that heareth my word', *akouōn* (G191, John 5:24), a present participle, the one who hears; 'and believeth on him that sent me', *pisteuōn* (G4100, John 5:24), the one who believes. Hearing here means more than sound reaching the ear; it means receiving the word, as the sheep hear the shepherd's voice (John 10:27). And faith in the Son is faith in the Father who sent him; the two cannot be separated (John 5:23).

The results: first, he 'hath everlasting life', *echei* (G2192, John 5:24, V-PAI-3S), present tense. Everlasting life is not only a future reward; it begins now, the moment a person believes. Second, he 'shall not come into condemnation', where the word is *krisin* (G2920, John 5:24) again, the same word as 'judgment' in verse 22. The one who has committed all judgment to the Son promises that the believer will not come into it. Paul says the same: "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). Third, he 'is passed from death unto life', *metabebēken* (G3327, John 5:24, V-RAI-3S), a perfect tense: he has crossed over, and remains on the other side. The STEPBible lexicon defines *metabainō* as 'to pass over from one place to another' and lists this verse and 1 John 3:14 under its figurative use, 'from death into life'. John's own letter repeats it almost word for word: "We know that we have passed from death unto life, because we love the brethren." (1 John 3:14)

Notice what the verse does not say. It does not say 'he that tries hard', or 'he that finally reaches the water first'. The man at the pool is the picture: he could not get to the source of healing, so the healer came to him and spoke.

**Asks:** Have you heard his word and believed? Then take him at his word: you have everlasting life now, you will not come into condemnation, and you have already crossed from death to life. Let that settle your fear, and let it send you to others who are still on the other side.

#### John 5:25–27 · The Hour Is Coming, and Now Is
**Says:** "Verily, verily, I say unto you, The hour is coming, and now is, when the dead shall hear the voice of the Son of God: and they that hear shall live." (John 5:25) "For as the Father hath life in himself; so hath he given to the Son to have life in himself;" (John 5:26) "And hath given him authority to execute judgment also, because he is the Son of man." (John 5:27)

**Means:** 'The hour is coming, and now is': the same phrase Jesus used at the well of the new worship (John 4:23). The 'hour' that 'now is' refers to the present, while Jesus is speaking. Who are 'the dead' who hear his voice now? The context points to the spiritually dead: people alive in body and dead toward God, whom Paul describes the same way: "And you hath he quickened, who were dead in trespasses and sins" (Ephesians 2:1). When they hear the voice of the Son of God, they live, *zēsontai* (G2198, John 5:25, V-FDI-3P). The raising of Lazarus will show it in the body (John 11:43–44); here Jesus speaks first of the soul. In John 5:28 he will speak of the bodily resurrection at the end.

Then the ground of it all: 'as the Father hath life in himself; so hath he given to the Son to have life in himself'. The Father's life is not borrowed; he is the fountain of life (Psalm 36:9). The Son has the same kind of life, 'in himself', and not merely as a gift received and passed on like a prophet's message. Yet it is 'given' by the Father. How can the Son both have life in himself and have been given it? The church has answered: the Father eternally gives the Son to have the very life he has, not as a creature receives life at a moment in time, but as the eternal Son is eternally from the Father (see the Debate Corner). Either way, only one who has life in himself can give life to the dead.

'And hath given him authority', *exousian* (G1849, John 5:27), the right and power, 'to execute judgment also, because he is the Son of man'. In Greek the phrase here is *huios* (G5207, John 5:27) *anthrōpou* (G444, John 5:27) without the article, a form found nowhere else in the Gospels' uses of the title (verify). It points straight back to Daniel's vision: "I saw in the night visions, and, behold, one like the Son of man came with the clouds of heaven, and came to the Ancient of days, and they brought him near before him." (Daniel 7:13) To him was given "dominion, and glory, and a kingdom" (Daniel 7:14). The one who will judge humanity is himself human; he has lived our life. That is part of why the judgment is just.

**Asks:** The Son has life in himself and gives it by his voice. Do not look for life anywhere else. If you are alive toward God, it is because you heard his voice; pray for someone who has not yet heard it.

#### John 5:28–30 · All That Are in the Graves
**Says:** "Marvel not at this: for the hour is coming, in the which all that are in the graves shall hear his voice," (John 5:28) "And shall come forth; they that have done good, unto the resurrection of life; and they that have done evil, unto the resurrection of damnation." (John 5:29) "I can of mine own self do nothing: as I hear, I judge: and my judgment is just; because I seek not mine own will, but the will of the Father which hath sent me." (John 5:30) *Damnation* here means condemnation, judgment.

**Means:** 'Marvel not at this': if giving spiritual life now amazes you, wait. Now the words 'and now is' drop out. This hour 'is coming' only. The dead in this verse are not described as spiritually dead but as 'all that are in the graves', *mnēmeiois* (G3419, John 5:28), tombs. All of them will hear his voice, and all will 'come forth'. This is the bodily resurrection of every human being at the end of the age, which Daniel had foreseen: "And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt." (Daniel 12:2) Paul preached the same: "there shall be a resurrection of the dead, both of the just and unjust" (Acts 24:15).

There are two outcomes: 'the resurrection of life', and 'the resurrection of damnation', *anastasin* (G386, John 5:29) and *kriseōs* (G2920, John 5:29), a resurrection that leads to judgment. The dividing line is 'they that have done good', *agatha* (G18, John 5:29), and 'they that have done evil', *phaula* (G5337, John 5:29), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'worthless, bad', always with a moral sense in the New Testament, and notes as the opposite of *agathos* in this verse. Does this mean salvation by works, against John 5:24? No; the Council's third question takes it up. In short, in John the work God requires is to believe on the one he sent (John 6:29), and those who have passed from death to life show it by what they do; those who have 'done evil' are those who 'loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil' (John 3:19).

Then Jesus returns to where he began: 'I can of mine own self do nothing'. His judgment is 'just', *dikaia* (G1342, John 5:30), not because he stands apart from the Father, but because he seeks only the Father's will. The one who will judge us all is the most perfectly obedient human being who ever lived.

**Asks:** Every person you know will one day hear his voice and come out of the grave. Live, and speak, in the light of that day. And let the Judge's own submission to his Father humble you: if he sought not his own will, how much more should we?

#### John 5:31–35 · John, a Burning and a Shining Light
**Says:** "If I bear witness of myself, my witness is not true." (John 5:31) "There is another that beareth witness of me; and I know that the witness which he witnesseth of me is true." (John 5:32) "Ye sent unto John, and he bare witness unto the truth." (John 5:33) "But I receive not testimony from man: but these things I say, that ye might be saved." (John 5:34) "He was a burning and a shining light: and ye were willing for a season to rejoice in his light." (John 5:35) *Bare* means bore; *for a season* means for a while.

**Means:** Now Jesus calls his witnesses, as if in court. The law required more than one: "at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established" (Deuteronomy 19:15). The word 'witness' in its forms comes 9 times in this chapter, and the Greek verb *martureō* 7 times; the noun is *marturia* (G3141, John 5:31), testimony, evidence. 'If I bear witness of myself, my witness is not true' means, in legal terms, that his unsupported word would not be accepted as valid evidence. Later Jesus says, 'Though I bear record of myself, yet my record is true' (John 8:14): in fact his word is always true, but here he meets them on their own legal ground. He does not ask them to take his word alone.

'There is another that beareth witness of me': the next verses show that this is the Father (John 5:37), not John, whom Jesus names next and then sets aside as merely human testimony. The authorities had sent a delegation to John (John 1:19), 'and he bare witness unto the truth': "And I saw, and bare record that this is the Son of God." (John 1:34) Jesus does not need human testimony, 'but these things I say, that ye might be saved'. Even while exposing them, he is seeking their salvation.

'He was a burning and a shining light': the word is *luchnos* (G3088, John 5:35), a lamp. The STEPBible lexicon defines it as 'a lamp (portable, and usually set on a stand)' and lists this verse as a metaphor 'of John the Baptist'. John had written in his prologue that the Baptist 'was not that Light' (John 1:8); he was a lamp, 'burning', *kaiomenos* (G2545, John 5:35, V-PPP-NSM), kindled by another, and 'shining', *phainōn* (G5316, John 5:35, V-PAP-NSM). A lamp gives light by being consumed. The authorities 'were willing for a season to rejoice in his light': they enjoyed the excitement for a while (the Greek word behind 'season' is *hōran* (G5610, John 5:35), an hour, a short time), but they did not follow where the lamp pointed.

**Asks:** Are you a lamp? A lamp is lit from another flame and burns for the sake of others. Point people to Christ, not to yourself. And ask whether you, like John's hearers, have enjoyed a preacher or a season of excitement without coming to the one he pointed to.

#### John 5:36–38 · The Works and the Father
**Says:** "But I have greater witness than that of John: for the works which the Father hath given me to finish, the same works that I do, bear witness of me, that the Father hath sent me." (John 5:36) "And the Father himself, which hath sent me, hath borne witness of me. Ye have neither heard his voice at any time, nor seen his shape." (John 5:37) "And ye have not his word abiding in you: for whom he hath sent, him ye believe not." (John 5:38) *Shape* means form, appearance.

**Means:** The second witness is 'greater' than John's: the works themselves. They are 'the works which the Father hath given me to finish', *teleiōsō* (G5048, John 5:36, V-AAS-1S), to complete, the same verb Jesus used at the well: 'to finish his work' (John 4:34). His works, from the healing at Bethesda to the cross where he will say 'It is finished' (John 19:30), show that the Father sent him. Nicodemus had seen this much: "no man can do these miracles that thou doest, except God be with him" (John 3:2). Later Jesus will appeal to the works again: "If I do not the works of my Father, believe me not." (John 10:37)

The third witness is the Father himself, 'which hath sent me, hath borne witness of me'. How? Some point to the voice at Jesus' baptism, which John's Gospel does not narrate, though the Baptist refers to what he saw (John 1:32–34). Others take it as the Father's witness in the Scriptures, which Jesus names next. Many take it as the Father's inward testimony in the hearts of those who believe: 'If we receive the witness of men, the witness of God is greater' (1 John 5:9). The three are not exclusive. What the authorities lack is plain: 'Ye have neither heard his voice at any time, nor seen his shape', *eidos* (G1491, John 5:37), form. At Sinai Israel 'heard the voice of the words, but saw no similitude' (Deuteronomy 4:12). These men have neither. And they do not have 'his word abiding in you', *menonta* (G3306, John 5:38, V-PAP-ASM), remaining, dwelling; the verb is John's great word for 'abide' (John 15:4). The proof is that they do not believe the one the Father sent.

**Asks:** The Father has spoken about his Son. Is his word abiding in you, or only passing through? Read the works of Jesus in John slowly, and ask what they tell you about who sent him.

#### John 5:39–40 · They Testify of Me
**Says:** "Search the scriptures; for in them ye think ye have eternal life: and they are they which testify of me." (John 5:39) "And ye will not come to me, that ye might have life." (John 5:40)

**Means:** The fourth witness is the Scriptures, *graphas* (G1124, John 5:39), the sacred writings of the Old Testament. The verb is *eraunate* (G2045, John 5:39, V-PAI-2P). The STEPBible lexicon defines *ereunaō* as 'to search, examine', and lists this verse. The form can be either an imperative, 'Search!', as the KJV reads it, or an indicative, 'You search', as many modern translations read it; the Greek data used by this study tags it as an indicative, but the spelling is the same for both (see the View A / View B panel). Both readings are true to the rest of Scripture: God does command us to search the Scriptures, and these men did search them with great diligence.

'For in them ye think ye have eternal life': they treated the study of the law as the way to life. Many teachers of that time said as much (verify particular sayings, often cited from the Mishnah's tractate *Avot*). Jesus does not despise the Scriptures; he says they are 'they which testify of me'. Their whole purpose is to lead to him: "And beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself." (Luke 24:27)

'And ye will not come to me, that ye might have life.' This is the saddest sentence in the chapter. The problem is not lack of information but lack of will: 'ye will not', *thelete* (G2309, John 5:40), the same verb Jesus used at the pool, 'Wilt thou be made whole?' (John 5:6). The helpless man was asked whether he willed to be healed; the learned men are told that they will not come. The contrast is deliberate. Life is found not in the book alone, but in coming to the person the book is about.

**Asks:** When you read the Bible, are you coming to Christ, or collecting information about him? Before your next reading, pray that you would meet him in it and come to him for life.

#### John 5:41–44 · The Honour That Cometh from God
**Says:** "I receive not honour from men." (John 5:41) "But I know you, that ye have not the love of God in you." (John 5:42) "I am come in my Father's name, and ye receive me not: if another shall come in his own name, him ye will receive." (John 5:43) "How can ye believe, which receive honour one of another, and seek not the honour that cometh from God only?" (John 5:44)

**Means:** 'Honour' here is *doxan* (G1391, John 5:41), usually translated 'glory'. The STEPBible lexicon notes that in the New Testament it always means good opinion, and so 'reputation, praise, honour, glory', and lists John 5:41 and John 5:44 under seeking and receiving it. Jesus does not need human applause. But he knows them: 'ye have not the love of God in you', *agapēn* (G26, John 5:42). Whether this means love for God, or God's love at work in them, the result is the same: they are religious without love for the God they serve.

The proof is their response. Jesus came 'in my Father's name', as his authorised representative, and they rejected him. 'If another shall come in his own name, him ye will receive'. False messiahs did come and gain followings; the best known, Simon bar Kosiba, called Bar Kokhba, led a revolt about a century later and was hailed by some as Messiah (verify). But the principle is wider than any one man: people who reject God's messenger are easily taken in by self-appointed ones.

Then the diagnosis: 'How can ye believe, which receive honour one of another, and seek not the honour that cometh from God only?' The phrase 'God only' is *monou theou*, with *monou* (G3441, John 5:44), 'the only God'. Faith is impossible for a heart that lives on human approval, because faith means receiving honour from God alone as a gift, and giving all glory to him. John later says of some leaders who believed but would not confess him: "For they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God." (John 12:43)

**Asks:** Whose honour do you live for? The love of human approval is not a small weakness; Jesus says it can make faith impossible. Ask the Lord to show you where you seek it, and to make his 'well done' enough.

#### John 5:45–47 · He Wrote of Me
**Says:** "Do not think that I will accuse you to the Father: there is one that accuseth you, even Moses, in whom ye trust." (John 5:45) "For had ye believed Moses, ye would have believed me: for he wrote of me." (John 5:46) "But if ye believe not his writings, how shall ye believe my words?" (John 5:47)

**Means:** The fifth witness is Moses, and he turns out to be a witness for the prosecution. The trial has reversed. The authorities put Jesus on trial for breaking Moses' law; Jesus tells them that Moses himself stands before the Father to accuse them. 'In whom ye trust', *ēlpikate* (G1679, John 5:45, V-RAI-2P), a perfect: on whom you have set your hope. The one they counted on to defend them is the one who will testify against them, *katēgorōn* (G2723, John 5:45, V-PAP-NSM), the ordinary word for a prosecutor.

Why? 'For he wrote of me.' Moses wrote of the seed of the woman who would bruise the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15), of the Passover lamb, of the serpent lifted up (Numbers 21:9, which Jesus has already applied to himself in John 3:14), and above all of the Prophet to come: "The LORD thy God will raise up unto thee a Prophet from the midst of thee, of thy brethren, like unto me; unto him ye shall hearken" (Deuteronomy 18:15). Moses also warned of what would happen to anyone who would not hear him: "whosoever will not hearken unto my words which he shall speak in my name, I will require it of him" (Deuteronomy 18:19).

The last verse sets 'his writings', *grammasin* (G1121, John 5:47), against 'my words', *rhēmasin* (G4487, John 5:47). If they do not believe what Moses wrote, which they claim to revere, how will they believe what Jesus says? The chapter ends with a question, and no answer from the hearers. It is left for the reader to answer.

**Asks:** Do you believe what Moses wrote? Then you will believe Jesus, because Moses wrote of him. Read the Old Testament as Jesus taught his disciples to read it, looking for him, and let its witness bring you to his words.

### 🧨 Cultural Landmines and Misconceptions
**'The Jews' as a people.** John 5:16 and 5:18 say that 'the Jews' persecuted Jesus and sought to kill him. Such verses have been used to justify contempt and violence toward Jewish people. In John the phrase often means the religious authorities in Jerusalem who opposed Jesus; the healed man, the disciples, Jesus himself, and John are all Jews, and Jesus calls Moses and the Scriptures his witnesses. The sin John 5 exposes (religion without love, and the love of human honour) is found in every people and every church.

**The sabbath as mere legalism.** Modern readers can assume that Jesus opposed the sabbath, or that it was a burden God should never have given. But the sabbath was God's gift, 'made for man' (Mark 2:27), and Jesus never called it a bad law. He opposed rules that turned a day of rest and mercy against the man made whole, and he claimed the right, as the Son, to do his Father's work on it: "For the Son of man is Lord even of the sabbath day." (Matthew 12:8)

**Healing waters.** Pools, springs, and shrines believed to heal have drawn pilgrims in every age, including our own. Whatever one concludes about John 5:4, the chapter does not commend the pool. The healing comes by Jesus' word, and the pool is the thing he bypasses. The lesson is not 'find the right water' but 'hear the Son'.

**'God helps those who help themselves.'** Many people think this saying is in the Bible. It is not, and John 5 shows the opposite. The man at the pool could not help himself, and his only hope, the water, rewarded the strongest. Jesus came to the one who could not get there first.

**Sickness and sin.** Some people assume that every long illness is punishment for a particular sin; others, reacting, deny that sin and suffering are ever connected. Jesus' warning in John 5:14 shows that sin is serious and that there is something worse than illness; his words in John 9:3 show that suffering is not always caused by a person's own sin. Hold both, and do not presume to know why a particular person is suffering.

**Jesus as a mild teacher.** A common picture of Jesus is a gentle moralist who never offended anyone. In John 5 he claims to be equal with God, the giver of life, and the judge of all humanity, and he tells respected religious leaders that Moses will accuse them. The one who gently asked a helpless man 'Wilt thou be made whole?' is the same one who will call all the dead from their graves.

## 🔴 Tier 3 — Expert
### 🏗️ Theological Framework
John 5 is one of the richest chapters in the New Testament for the doctrine of God and of Christ. Read through the Reformed conviction that Scripture interprets Scripture, it shapes at least five areas of systematic theology.

*The Trinity: equality and order.* The chapter holds together two things that every orthodox doctrine of the Trinity must keep. The Son is equal with the Father: he does 'what things soever' the Father does (John 5:19), gives life as the Father does (John 5:21), is honoured as the Father is (John 5:23), and has life in himself as the Father does (John 5:26). Yet the Son is not the Father: he is sent (John 5:23, 24, 30, 36–38), he 'can do nothing of himself' (John 5:19), and he receives from the Father (John 5:22, 26, 27). The church has named this the unity of the divine essence and the distinction of the persons, with an order among them (the Father unbegotten, the Son eternally begotten, the Spirit proceeding) that implies no inequality of being. Augustine, and the Western tradition after him, drew from John 5:19 the principle that the external works of the Trinity are undivided: the Father does not do some things and the Son others, but whatever the Father does, the Son does 'likewise' (verify Augustine's treatment in *On the Trinity* and in *Tractates on John*, 18–23). The Westminster Confession (chapter 2, section 3, verify) summarizes the doctrine in these terms (paraphrased): in the unity of the Godhead there are three persons of one substance, power, and eternity; the Father is of none, the Son is eternally begotten of the Father, and the Spirit eternally proceeds from the Father and the Son.

*Christ's two natures and his office.* John 5 names Jesus both 'the Son of God' (John 5:25) and 'the Son of man' (John 5:27). He gives life as the Son who has life in himself; he judges as the Son of man to whom authority has been given. The Reformed tradition distinguishes what belongs to Christ by nature, as the eternal Son, from what he receives by appointment, as the Mediator, God and man in one person. Both are present in John 5:26–27, and the Debate Corner considers how they relate.

*Regeneration and effectual calling.* 'The dead shall hear the voice of the Son of God: and they that hear shall live' (John 5:25), and 'the Son quickeneth whom he will' (John 5:21). The Reformed churches have read these words, with Ephesians 2:1–5, as teaching that the spiritually dead are made alive by the sovereign call of Christ, through his word and by his Spirit (John 6:63), not by any power of their own to respond first. The Westminster Confession (chapter 10, verify) calls this effectual calling. The man at the pool, unable to move until the word came, is a living picture of it.

*Justification now.* John 5:24 is one of the plainest statements in the Gospels of a present justification: the one who believes 'shall not come into condemnation', because the verdict of the last day has been brought forward. The believer 'hath everlasting life' now, in the present tense, and 'is passed', in the perfect tense. Romans 8:1 is the apostle's commentary.

*Inaugurated and final eschatology.* John 5 holds together 'the hour is coming, and now is' (John 5:25) and 'the hour is coming' (John 5:28). Eternal life and resurrection have begun already in those who hear Christ's voice; the bodily resurrection of all, just and unjust, and the final judgment are still to come. Some scholars (Rudolf Bultmann, for example, verify) have treated John 5:28–29 as a later addition that brought a 'realized' Gospel back into line with traditional expectation; but the two belong together in the chapter's logic, and the same pairing appears in John 6:40 and John 11:24–26. The Reformed confessions teach both a present spiritual resurrection in regeneration and a future bodily resurrection of all the dead (Westminster Confession, chapters 32–33, verify).

*Scripture and Christ.* 'They are they which testify of me' (John 5:39), and 'he wrote of me' (John 5:46). The Reformed hermeneutic of reading all Scripture in the light of Christ, and Christ in the light of all Scripture, has its warrant here and in Luke 24:27, 44. The Scriptures are the Father's witness; to read them without coming to the Son is to misread them.

### 📚 Scholarly Insights
- **Augustine**, *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (Tractates 17–23, verify): reads the pool allegorically: the five porches as the five books of Moses, which expose the sick but cannot heal them, and the troubled water as the Lord's passion (verify the particulars). He also finds in the thirty-eight years a number short of forty, which he treats as the number of fulfilment (verify). The allegories are not to be followed, but his great theme, that the law shows the disease and only Christ heals it, is sound. His treatment of John 5:19–30 is a major source for the Western doctrine that the works of the Trinity are undivided.
- **John Chrysostom**, *Homilies on the Gospel of John* (Homilies 36–40, verify): admires the man's patience through thirty-eight years and his lack of bitterness toward those who stepped down before him, and presses the lesson that we who have Christ should not be less patient in our troubles (verify). He argues from John 5:18–19 against the Arians that the Son's equality with the Father is plain (verify).
- **Athanasius**, *Orations against the Arians* (verify the book): uses texts from John 5, including 'what things soever he doeth, these also doeth the Son likewise' and the honour due to the Son, to argue that the Son shares the Father's nature and is no creature.
- **Hilary of Poitiers**, *On the Trinity* (book 7, verify): works through John 5:17–23 at length to show that the Son's 'can do nothing of himself' expresses his unity with the Father, not his inferiority.
- **John Calvin**, *Commentary on the Gospel According to John*: on John 5:17 sees God's providence as ceaseless, so that the sabbath rest was never divine idleness (verify). On John 5:26 he argues that Christ speaks of himself as manifested in the flesh, the Mediator in whom the life of God is given to us, rather than of his eternal relation to the Father (verify; see the Debate Corner).
- **J. C. Ryle**, *Expository Thoughts on the Gospels* (John): draws from the thirty-eight years that Christ's mercy reaches the longest and most hopeless cases, and from John 5:23 the solemn warning that no one honours the Father who does not honour the Son (verify).
- **B. F. Westcott**, *The Gospel According to St. John* (1880): discusses the name of the pool and the textual evidence for John 5:3–4, and notes that the verb in John 5:39 may be read as an indicative (verify).
- **C. H. Dodd**, *The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel* (1953): drew attention to the Jewish discussion of God's continued work on the sabbath, in giving life and in judging, and argued that John 5:17–29 claims those two works for the Son (verify).
- **D. A. Carson**, *The Gospel According to John* (Pillar New Testament Commentary, 1991): judges John 5:3b–4 not to be original, while noting that John 5:7 assumes the belief it describes; takes 'search' as indicative; and reads John 5:26 as grounding the Son's life-giving in his eternal relation to the Father (verify each).
- **Raymond E. Brown**, *The Gospel According to John* (Anchor Bible, 1966): surveys the excavations at the double pool near St Anne's and the possible reference to the site in the Copper Scroll from Qumran (verify), and treats John 5 as the first of a series of discourses set at the Jewish feasts.
- **Francis Turretin**, *Institutes of Elenctic Theology* (third topic, verify): defends the eternal generation of the Son against Socinian objections, and appeals to John 5:26 as teaching the communication of the divine essence from the Father to the Son.

### ⚔️ Debate Corner
**The question:** What does Jesus mean when he says 'so hath he given to the Son to have life in himself' (John 5:26)?

**Position 1 · The Son received life as a creature does, so he is less than the Father.** In the fourth century Arius and his followers read the verse as proof that the Son is not self-existent: whatever is given was once not possessed, so the Son is the first and greatest of the Father's works, not God in the same sense. Jehovah's Witnesses today read it similarly, and add John 14:28 ('my Father is greater than I'). They are right that the verse speaks of a real giving, and that the Son is not the Father.

**Position 2 · Eternal generation: the Father eternally gives the Son the very life he has.** The Nicene tradition, East and West, and the Reformed orthodox after it, read the verse as describing the eternal relation of Father and Son. The Father has life 'in himself', underived. He has given the Son to have life 'in himself', the same kind of life, not a lesser or borrowed life. The giving is not an event in time, as though the Son once lacked life, but the eternal communication of the whole divine life from Father to Son, which is what the church means by eternal generation. Augustine (Tractate 19, verify) and Turretin (verify) argue this way; many modern Reformed and evangelical scholars follow them.

**Position 3 · A mediatorial reading: the life is given to the incarnate Son for our sake.** John Calvin, reading the verse in context with John 5:27 ('because he is the Son of man'), held that Jesus speaks here of himself as he was manifested in the flesh: the Father has so given life to the Mediator that he may give it to us (verify). Some modern evangelical writers who question the language of eternal generation read the verse similarly, as referring to the Son's role in the work of salvation rather than to his eternal being (verify). Holders of this view affirm the Son's full deity; they simply think the verse speaks of his office.

**Most consistent with Reformed hermeneutics.** Scripture interpreting Scripture rules out Position 1. The same chapter says the Son is equal with God (John 5:18), does all that the Father does (John 5:19), and must receive the Father's honour (John 5:23), which God gives to no other (Isaiah 42:8). The Gospel opens by calling the Word God and the maker of all things (John 1:1–3), and it closes with Thomas's 'My Lord and my God' (John 20:28). A creature cannot have life 'in himself' in the sense the Father has it. Between Positions 2 and 3, the eternal generation reading best honours the words. The phrase 'in himself' is used identically of Father and Son, and describes the kind of life each has, not only a role; and verse 26 is the ground ('for') of verses 21 and 25, where the Son gives life 'whom he will', as sovereignly as the Father. Position 3 has a real insight, because verse 27 moves explicitly to what is given to the Son 'because he is the Son of man', his authority to judge as Mediator. The best reading holds both: verse 26 speaks of what the Son eternally has from the Father, and verse 27 of what he exercises as the incarnate Son of man. This is also the reading of the Westminster Confession's summary that the Son is eternally begotten of the Father (chapter 2, section 3, verify). The Reformed position here is not a later speculation added to the text; it is the attempt to hold together everything John 5 says about the Son.

**Pastoral Warning:** Debates about eternal generation can seem remote from the pew, and they can be conducted with more heat than love. But their effect on a church is real. A congregation that thinks of Jesus as less than fully God will drift toward treating him as a helper, an example, or a mediator between us and a more distant God, and it will lose the assurance that in Christ we meet God himself and receive life from its source. A congregation that affirms his deity only as a doctrine will lose his humility: the Son who says 'I can of mine own self do nothing' (John 5:30). Teach this passage so that people worship the Son as they worship the Father, and learn from him the joy of living from another's life. And do not use the debate to question the salvation of brothers and sisters who hold Position 3 while confessing the Son's full deity.

### 🔤 Original Language Deep Dive
Every Greek definition below comes from one source: the brief lexicon published by STEPBible.org (Tyndale House, Cambridge), whose Greek entries are drawn largely from G. Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament*. It is called 'the STEPBible lexicon' below.

**Equal with God.** *Ison* (G2470, John 5:18, A-ASM), the accusative of *isos*. The STEPBible lexicon defines it as 'equal, the same in size, number, quality, etc.', and gives the construction with a verb of making, 'to make oneself equal to God', listing this verse. It also lists Philippians 2:6, where the neuter plural is used of Christ's equality with God. The word occurs 8 times in the New Testament, including the false witnesses whose testimony 'agreed not together' at Jesus' trial (Mark 14:56), literally testimonies that were not equal. The claim in John 5:18 is not a mere similarity but equality.

**His own Father.** *Idion* (G2398, John 5:18, A-ASM). The STEPBible lexicon's first sense is 'one's own', of what is private and personal, and it lists John 5:43 ('in his own name') for the same word. The authorities heard Jesus claim God as his Father in a sense that was his alone, not the shared 'our Father' of Israel's prayers.

**Loosing the sabbath.** *Eluen* (G3089, John 5:18, V-IAI-3S), the imperfect of *luō*. The STEPBible lexicon's senses include 'to loose, unbind, release' and 'to resolve a whole into its parts, loosen, dissolve, break up, destroy', and it lists John 2:19 ('Destroy this temple') under the second. The imperfect presents the action as ongoing, 'he was breaking' or 'kept on breaking', which fits the sense that Jesus made a practice of sabbath healing (compare John 7:21–23). It is the same verb used for breaking the law of Moses in John 7:23 (verify the Greek there) and the Scripture in John 10:35 (verify).

**Working.** *Ergazetai* (G2038, John 5:17, V-PNI-3S) and *ergazomai* (G2038, John 5:17, V-PNI-1S), the present forms of *ergazomai*, 'to work, labour', which the STEPBible lexicon lists at this verse and at John 9:4. The noun *erga* (G2041, John 5:20, N-APN), 'works', is listed by the lexicon among the works of Christ 'especially in John' (John 5:20, 36; John 10:38; John 14:11–12). The chapter moves from the Father's working (verse 17) to the greater works the Son will do (verse 20) to the works that witness (verse 36).

**Making alive.** *Zōopoiei* (G2227, John 5:21, V-PAI-3S). The STEPBible lexicon notes that in classical Greek *zōopoieō* meant 'to produce alive', and that in the Greek Old Testament and the New Testament it means 'to make alive, cause to live, quicken'. It lists John 5:21, John 6:63 (the Spirit that quickeneth), Romans 4:17 (God who quickeneth the dead), Romans 8:11, and 1 Corinthians 15:45 (the last Adam 'a quickening spirit'). In the Greek Old Testament it renders forms of the Hebrew verb *chayah* (H2421), 'to live', in its causative sense; one example the lexicon lists is 2 Kings 5:7, where the king cries 'Am I God, to kill and to make alive'. John 5:21 puts the Son in the place of the God of that verse.

**Crossing over.** *Metabebēken* (G3327, John 5:24, V-RAI-3S), the perfect of *metabainō*. The STEPBible lexicon's basic sense is 'to pass over from one place to another', and it gives a figurative use, from death into life, listing John 5:24 and 1 John 3:14. It also lists John 13:1, where Jesus knows his hour has come to 'depart out of this world'. John uses the verb 3 times in his Gospel. The perfect tense describes a completed crossing with a continuing result: the believer has passed over and stays passed over. Beside it stand the present *echei* (G2192, John 5:24, V-PAI-3S), 'hath', and the present *erchetai* (G2064, John 5:24, V-PNI-3S), 'cometh', negated: he 'shall not come' into judgment.

**Judgment.** *Krisis* is used in four senses in this chapter's few verses, all in the STEPBible lexicon's entry: 'all judgment' committed to the Son, *krisin* (G2920, John 5:22, N-ASF); the believer not coming into 'condemnation', *krisin* (G2920, John 5:24, N-ASF); the Son's authority 'to execute judgment' (John 5:27); the 'resurrection of damnation', *kriseōs* (G2920, John 5:29, N-GSF); and 'my judgment is just' (John 5:30). The KJV's variety (judgment, condemnation, damnation) reflects context, not different Greek words. 'Damnation' in 1611 meant a judicial sentence of condemnation.

**Witness.** The noun *marturia*, here *marturian* (G3141, John 5:36, N-ASF), 'witness, testimony, evidence', is used 13 times in John, and the STEPBible lexicon lists John 5:32 and John 5:36 under 'witness concerning Christ and divine things'. The verb *martureō* dominates John 5:31–39. The courtroom language is deliberate: the Gospel is a trial in which Jesus is accused, and in which, in the end, the world is judged (John 16:8–11).

**Search.** *Eraunate* (G2045, John 5:39, V-PAI-2P). The STEPBible lexicon lists *eraunaō* as a later spelling of *ereunaō*, 'to search, examine', with this verse. The second person plural present has the same form in the indicative ('ye search') and the imperative ('search!'). The Greek data used in this study tags it as an indicative. The context favours the indicative, since 'in them ye think ye have eternal life' describes what the hearers already do, but the imperative is grammatically possible, and many older interpreters, including the KJV translators, took it that way. Either reading leads to the same point: the Scriptures bear witness to Christ, and searching them is fruitless if it does not lead to him (John 5:40).

**Honour.** *Doxan* (G1391, John 5:44, N-ASF). The STEPBible lexicon traces the word from 'expectation, judgment, opinion' in classical Greek to 'good opinion', 'reputation, praise, honour, glory' in the New Testament, and lists John 5:41 and John 5:44 under receiving and seeking it. The same noun is the 'glory' that the Word revealed (John 1:14) and that he had with the Father (John 17:5). The men who seek *doxa* from one another cannot see the *doxa* of God standing in front of them. A different word, *timōsin* (G5091, John 5:23, V-PAS-3P), from *timaō*, 'to honour', is used in John 5:23 for the honour due to the Son.

**The lamp.** *Luchnos* (G3088, John 5:35, N-NSM), 'a lamp (portable, and usually set on a stand)', which the STEPBible lexicon lists at this verse as a metaphor for John the Baptist, beside 2 Peter 1:19, where prophecy is 'a light that shineth in a dark place'. John is never called *phōs*, the light (John 1:8); here Jesus says the hearers rejoiced 'in his light', *phōti* (G5457, John 5:35, N-DSN), the light a lamp gives.

**Textual notes.** Three places in this chapter have textual differences. (1) *John 5:3b–4.* The KJV, following the Textus Receptus and the majority of later Greek manuscripts, prints the end of verse 3 ('waiting for the moving of the water') and all of verse 4 (the angel troubling the pool). Several of the earliest witnesses lack them, including the papyri P66 and P75 and the codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus; some manuscripts have one part but not the other, and some mark the passage with signs of doubt (verify these details). Scholars who think the words were added point to their absence in the earliest witnesses, to variation in their wording, and to vocabulary unusual in John: *kinēsin* (G2796, John 5:3), *nosēmati* (G3553, John 5:4), and *dēpote* (G1221, John 5:4) occur nowhere else in the New Testament. Those who defend them point out that verse 7 assumes the troubling of the water, that Tertullian around AD 200 already refers to an angel stirring the pool (*On Baptism*, chapter 5, verify), and that a scribe might have dropped the angel as seeming superstitious. This study teaches the text the KJV prints. Either way, the teaching of the passage stands: the healing came by Jesus' word, not by the water. (2) *John 5:2.* The name appears as Bethesda in the Textus Receptus and in other forms (Bethzatha, Bethsaida) in other manuscripts; the STEPBible lexicon notes the variants and calls the derivation uncertain. (3) *John 5:16.* The words 'and sought to slay him' are in the Textus Receptus but absent from many early manuscripts; John 5:18 ('sought the more to kill him') makes the same point either way. Many modern editions differ from the KJV at these places. The differences should be taught calmly, without scorn for either side; none of them touches any doctrine.

### 🚷 Preaching Mistakes to Avoid
- **Information without life:** a sermon rich in the archaeology of the double pool, the manuscripts of verse 4, and the Greek of *metabainō* that never calls the dead in the room to hear the voice of the Son and live.
- **Heat without light:** a sermon that thunders against the Pharisees' legalism, or against modern 'Pharisees', and never shows the Son who gives life, or asks the hearers whether they love the honour of men.
- **'Get up and walk' as a self-help message:** preaching John 5:8 as a call to stop making excuses and take responsibility for your own healing reverses the passage. The man could not get up until the word that commanded also enabled him.
- **Allegory in place of exposition:** making the five porches the five books of Moses, the thirty-eight years a code, and the angel a symbol of baptism, and missing what Jesus himself says the sign means in John 5:19–29.
- **Using John 5:14 to blame the sick:** telling suffering people that their illness is caused by their sin. Jesus spoke to one man whose heart he knew; he denied that rule in John 9:3.
- **Preaching John 5:29 as salvation by works, or ignoring it:** the verse must be read with John 5:24, not against it and not instead of it.
- **Softening the claim:** presenting Jesus in John 5 as a great teacher, when he claims the Father's works, life, judgment, and honour.

## 🟣 Tier 4 — Christocentric, Humility, Life & Light
### 👑 Every Answer in Jesus
John 5 demolishes every ground of self-glory. Physical strength is no help: the pool rewarded the quickest, and the man who needed healing most could never win. Religious zeal is no help: the authorities guarded the sabbath and sought to kill the Lord of the sabbath. Bible knowledge is no help on its own: they searched the Scriptures and would not come. Reputation is no help: it is precisely their love of honour from one another that made faith impossible. Even Moses, their great hope, turns out to be their accuser. Everyone in the chapter is shown to be helpless, blind, or dead, and the only one who gives anything is Jesus.

And every good thing the chapter holds is found in him. Healing is found in his word, not in the pool (John 5:8). The Father's work is found in him, because 'my Father worketh hitherto, and I work' (John 5:17). Life is found in him, because he has life in himself and quickens whom he will (John 5:21, 26). Judgment is in his hands, and therefore so is acquittal (John 5:22, 24). The honour due to God is his (John 5:23). The meaning of the Scriptures is found in him, because they testify of him and Moses wrote of him (John 5:39, 46). This is what Paul means when he says of Christ, "In whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." (Colossians 2:3) The chapter humbles us by showing that we come to him as the man at the pool came, with nothing but a complaint and an empty mat, and it makes Christ glorious by showing that he is the Son who gives life with a word.

## 📚 Doctrine
### Theology Proper · The Father Who Works and Has Life in Himself
**Proposition:** God the Father is the living God who has life in himself, underived and inexhaustible, and he never ceases to work. His rest on the seventh day was the rest of a finished creation, not the withdrawal of a God who stopped acting; he continually upholds, governs, gives life, and judges, and he does all this through his Son.

**Textual Anchor:** "But Jesus answered them, My Father worketh hitherto, and I work." (John 5:17), with *ergazetai* (G2038, John 5:17, V-PNI-3S), a present, 'is working'; and "For as the Father hath life in himself; so hath he given to the Son to have life in himself;" (John 5:26).

**Cross-References:** "For with thee is the fountain of life: in thy light shall we see light." (Psalm 36:9); "Behold, he that keepeth Israel shall neither slumber nor sleep." (Psalm 121:4); "upholding all things by the word of his power" (Hebrews 1:3)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Deism: God made the world and left it to run by itself.* Many who believe in a creator imagine him as a watchmaker who wound the world up and stepped back; they intend to honour the order of nature. Jesus says the Father 'worketh hitherto' (John 5:17): the world is upheld moment by moment. (2) *God's sabbath rest means divine inactivity, so any work on the sabbath is ungodly.* Some of Jesus' opponents reasoned that the sabbath forbade even acts of healing. Jesus answers that God's own rest has never been idleness, and that doing his Father's work of mercy is in keeping with it. (3) *Pantheism: God is simply the life of the world.* Some religious and philosophical traditions identify God with nature's life-force. John 5:26 says the Father has life 'in himself', distinct from the creation to which he gives life.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Confidence in providence: the Father is at work in our circumstances today, including our long waits. Worship of a God who is the source of all life. And rest that trusts his work rather than our own anxious labour.

### Theology Proper · God's Witness to His Son
**Proposition:** God has not left his Son without witness. He testifies to Jesus through the prophet he sent before him, through the works he gave him to finish, through his own voice and inward testimony, and through the Scriptures, which from Moses onward speak of Christ. To reject that witness is to reject the Father's word.

**Textual Anchor:** "And the Father himself, which hath sent me, hath borne witness of me." (John 5:37); "they are they which testify of me" (John 5:39); "for he wrote of me" (John 5:46). The noun is *marturia* (G3141, John 5:36), testimony.

**Cross-References:** "If we receive the witness of men, the witness of God is greater: for this is the witness of God which he hath testified of his Son." (1 John 5:9); "that all things must be fulfilled, which were written in the law of Moses, and in the prophets, and in the psalms, concerning me" (Luke 24:44); "And he said unto him, If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead." (Luke 16:31)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The Old Testament does not really speak of Jesus; Christians read him into it.* Many modern scholars hold that the Hebrew Scriptures, read in their own setting, have no Messiah Jesus in view; they want to respect the original meaning. Jesus says Moses 'wrote of me' (John 5:46), and he taught his disciples to find him 'in all the scriptures' (Luke 24:27), including in promises like Deuteronomy 18:15. (2) *The Bible is mainly a book of rules or wisdom for good living.* Moralistic reading, in churches and outside them, treats Scripture as advice. Jesus says the Scriptures are witnesses to him, and that searching them without coming to him leaves a person without life (John 5:39–40). (3) *Faith needs fresh signs, not Scripture.* Some say they would believe if God did something spectacular. Jesus says Moses' writings are enough to lead to him, and that those who reject them will not believe his words (John 5:47; Luke 16:31).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Confidence that the Christian faith rests on God's own testimony, not on human opinion. A new way of reading the Old Testament, looking for Christ. And humility, because the witness is God's, and our part is only to receive it and pass it on.

### Christology · The Son Equal with the Father
**Proposition:** Jesus Christ, the Son, is equal with God the Father in nature, power, and honour, while remaining distinct from the Father as the Son he loves and sends. He does whatever the Father does, and all must honour him as they honour the Father.

**Textual Anchor:** "making himself equal with God" (John 5:18), where 'equal' is *ison* (G2470, John 5:18); "for what things soever he doeth, these also doeth the Son likewise" (John 5:19); "That all men should honour the Son, even as they honour the Father." (John 5:23)

**Cross-References:** "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1); "Who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God:" (Philippians 2:6); "I and my Father are one." (John 10:30)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Arianism, and the Jehovah's Witnesses today: the Son is the highest creature, not God in the same sense as the Father.* Its holders point to texts where the Son receives from the Father or says the Father is greater (John 14:28). But a creature could not do 'what things soever' the Father does, nor receive the Father's honour (John 5:19, 23; Isaiah 42:8). (2) *Jesus was a good man whose followers later made him divine.* Many modern people admire Jesus as a moral teacher. His opponents in John 5 heard him claim equality with God and sought to kill him for it (John 5:18); he did not correct them. (3) *Modalism, including Oneness Pentecostal teaching: the Father and the Son are one person in different modes.* Its holders want to guard the oneness of God. But in John 5 the Father loves the Son, shows him all things, sends him, and gives him authority (John 5:20, 22, 23, 27): these are relations between persons, not masks of one person.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Worship of Jesus without hesitation or embarrassment. Security, because the one who saves us is not a lesser being who might fail. And reverence, because the Jesus who is gentle with the helpless is the Son to whom all honour is due.

### Christology · Life in Himself, Given to Whom He Will
**Proposition:** The Son has life in himself, as the Father does, by the Father's eternal gift, and so he is not only a messenger about life but its source. He gives life, spiritual now and bodily at the resurrection, to whom he will, by his sovereign word.

**Textual Anchor:** "For as the Father raiseth up the dead, and quickeneth them; even so the Son quickeneth whom he will." (John 5:21), with *zōopoiei* (G2227, John 5:21, V-PAI-3S); "so hath he given to the Son to have life in himself" (John 5:26).

**Cross-References:** "In him was life; and the life was the light of men." (John 1:4); "Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:" (John 11:25); "He that hath the Son hath life; and he that hath not the Son of God hath not life." (1 John 5:12)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus shows the way to life but is not himself its source.* Some teachers present Christ as an example or guide, like a prophet pointing beyond himself. Jesus says he has life 'in himself' and gives it (John 5:26, 21). (2) *Life can be found in other sources, religious or spiritual, equally well.* Religious pluralism, often held with sincere tolerance, sees many paths to the same life. Jesus says the Son quickens, and that those who will not come to him do not have life (John 5:40; 1 John 5:12). (3) *The Son gives life to all who first make themselves worthy.* Some teach that Christ gives life in response to a person's prior goodness. The Son quickens 'whom he will' (John 5:21), and the man at the pool had nothing to offer.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Hope for the hopeless, because life does not depend on the strength of the one who receives it. Prayer for the spiritually dead, because the Son still quickens whom he will. And joy in Christ himself as the fountain, not only the means, of life.

### Anthropology & Sin · Dead, Unwilling, and Seeking Honour from Men
**Proposition:** Apart from Christ, human beings are spiritually dead toward God. Their unbelief is not only a lack of information but a refusal of the will: they will not come to Christ, even when they know the Scriptures, because they do not have the love of God in them and they love honour from one another more than the honour that comes from God.

**Textual Anchor:** "the dead shall hear the voice of the Son of God" (John 5:25); "And ye will not come to me, that ye might have life." (John 5:40), with *thelete* (G2309, John 5:40); "How can ye believe, which receive honour one of another, and seek not the honour that cometh from God only?" (John 5:44)

**Cross-References:** "And you hath he quickened, who were dead in trespasses and sins;" (Ephesians 2:1); "For they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God." (John 12:43); "For I bear them record that they have a zeal of God, but not according to knowledge." (Romans 10:2)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Pelagianism, and its modern forms: people are spiritually able to come to God by their own power, needing only good examples and information.* Its holders want to protect human responsibility. Jesus calls unbelievers 'the dead' who live only when they hear his voice (John 5:25), and diagnoses their unbelief as unwillingness (John 5:40); responsibility remains, but ability comes from Christ. (2) *Unbelief is purely intellectual: people would believe if they had enough evidence.* Many sceptics and some apologists assume this. The authorities had John, the works, the Father's witness, and the Scriptures; the problem lay in the will and the love of honour (John 5:40, 44). (3) *Religious devotion proves spiritual life.* Some assume that zeal for God's law is the same as love for God. These men were zealous for the sabbath, and Jesus said, 'ye have not the love of God in you' (John 5:42).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Humility, because our own coming to Christ was his gift. Patience with unbelievers, since arguments alone will not raise the dead. And honest self-examination about the love of human approval.

### Soteriology & Grace · Passed from Death unto Life
**Proposition:** Whoever hears Christ's word and believes the Father who sent him has everlasting life now, as a present possession. That person will not come into condemnation, because the verdict of the last day has already been given in his favour, and he has passed, once and for all, from death into life. This is received by faith alone, not earned by works.

**Textual Anchor:** "Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life, and shall not come into condemnation; but is passed from death unto life." (John 5:24), with *echei* (G2192, John 5:24, V-PAI-3S), 'hath', and *metabebēken* (G3327, John 5:24, V-RAI-3S), 'is passed', a perfect.

**Cross-References:** "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit." (Romans 8:1); "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God:" (Ephesians 2:8); "We know that we have passed from death unto life, because we love the brethren." (1 John 3:14)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Eternal life is earned by good works, or by faith together with works.* Many religious systems, and many hearts, hold that God rewards those who do their best. Jesus names hearing and believing as the way to life (John 5:24), and John later calls believing 'the work of God' (John 6:29). (2) *No one can have assurance of eternal life in this life.* The Council of Trent (sixth session, verify chapter 12 and canon 16) taught that, apart from a special revelation, no one can know with the certainty of faith that he is among the elect and will persevere; its defenders want to guard against presumption. Jesus says the believer 'hath' everlasting life now and 'shall not come into condemnation' (John 5:24); assurance rests on his word, not on our feelings. (3) *Mere hearing, or bare assent, is enough.* Some treat a one-time profession or familiarity with the gospel as saving. Jesus joins hearing to believing, and his opponents in the same chapter heard him and did not believe (John 5:38, 40).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Assurance for anxious believers. Freedom from the fear of condemnation, and so freedom to serve out of gratitude. And urgency in evangelism, because the crossing from death to life happens now, when someone hears and believes.

### Eschatology · The Hour Now Is, the Hour Coming, and the Son as Judge
**Proposition:** The age to come has already broken in: those who hear the Son's voice now are raised from spiritual death. At the end of the age all the dead will hear his voice and rise bodily, the righteous to the resurrection of life and the wicked to the resurrection of condemnation. The Son of man will be the Judge of all, because the Father has committed all judgment to him.

**Textual Anchor:** "The hour is coming, and now is" (John 5:25); "for the hour is coming, in the which all that are in the graves shall hear his voice," (John 5:28); "And hath given him authority to execute judgment also, because he is the Son of man." (John 5:27), with *anastasin* (G386, John 5:29).

**Cross-References:** "And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt." (Daniel 12:2); "Because he hath appointed a day, in the which he will judge the world in righteousness by that man whom he hath ordained; whereof he hath given assurance unto all men, in that he hath raised him from the dead." (Acts 17:31); "For we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ; that every one may receive the things done in his body, according to that he hath done, whether it be good or bad." (2 Corinthians 5:10)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The resurrection is only spiritual, already past, with no bodily resurrection to come.* Some in Paul's day said 'the resurrection is past already' (2 Timothy 2:18), and some modern theologies reduce resurrection to a present spiritual experience. Jesus distinguishes the hour that 'now is' from the hour that 'is coming', when all in the graves come forth (John 5:25, 28–29). (2) *Universalism: all people will finally be raised to life.* Many hope, out of compassion, that no one will finally be lost. Jesus speaks of two resurrections with two outcomes (John 5:29). (3) *Only the righteous will be raised; the wicked simply stay dead.* Some traditions teach that the unrighteous are not raised at all. Jesus says 'all that are in the graves' will hear and come forth, including 'they that have done evil' (John 5:28–29; Acts 24:15).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Sobriety, because every person will stand before Christ. Comfort, because the Judge is the Saviour who has promised that believers will not come into condemnation. And hope at the graveside: the voice that called the man from his mat will call the dead from their graves.

### Christian Walk · Made Whole: Sin No More, and Seek God's Honour
**Proposition:** Those whom Christ has made whole are called to turn from sin, not in order to earn his mercy but because they have received it. They are to live before God's face, seeking the honour that comes from God only rather than the approval of people.

**Textual Anchor:** "Behold, thou art made whole: sin no more, lest a worse thing come unto thee." (John 5:14), with *hamartane* (G264, John 5:14, V-PAM-2S); "and seek not the honour that cometh from God only" (John 5:44).

**Cross-References:** "Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more." (John 8:11); "For do I now persuade men, or God? or do I seek to please men? for if I yet pleased men, I should not be the servant of Christ." (Galatians 1:10); "Who gave himself for us, that he might redeem us from all iniquity, and purify unto himself a peculiar people, zealous of good works." (Titus 2:14)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Antinomianism: grace makes holiness optional.* Some reason that since salvation is free, how we live does not matter. Jesus followed mercy with 'sin no more' (John 5:14). (2) *Sinless perfection: 'sin no more' means a believer can reach a state of no longer sinning in this life.* Some in the holiness tradition, following John Wesley's teaching on entire sanctification (verify), hold that believers can be made perfect in love here; they want to take God's commands seriously. The command is real, but John himself writes that 'If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves' (1 John 1:8), so the call is to a direction of life, not a claim of arrival. (3) *People-pleasing is simply kindness.* Many think seeking others' approval is harmless or even virtuous. Jesus says it can make faith itself impossible (John 5:44).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Gratitude that fuels obedience. Freedom from the tyranny of other people's opinions. And a quiet life lived before God, whose 'well done' is enough.

### Assumed Here
- **Pneumatology:** John 5 does not name the Holy Spirit, but it assumes his work: the voice of the Son that makes the dead live does so by the Spirit, for "It is the spirit that quickeneth" (John 6:63), and no one enters the kingdom unless 'born of water and of the Spirit' (John 3:5). Scripture teaches the Spirit's life-giving work plainly in John 3:5–8, John 6:63, and Romans 8:11.
- **Ecclesiology:** The chapter assumes a gathered people of God (the temple, the feast, the Scriptures read in Israel) but does not teach the church's nature. Scripture teaches it plainly in John 10:16, John 17:20–23, and Matthew 16:18.

## ⚖️ The Council's Hard Questions
### ❓ Question 1 · Did Jesus Break the Sabbath
**Moderator:** John 5:18 says the authorities sought to kill Jesus 'because he not only had broken the sabbath, but said also that God was his Father'. Is John telling us, in his own voice, that Jesus broke the sabbath? And if not, what did Jesus do?

**Historian:** The law forbade work on the sabbath (Exodus 20:10) and Jeremiah forbade carrying burdens through Jerusalem's gates (Jeremiah 17:21). By Jesus' day the teachers had defined 'work' in great detail; the Mishnah's list of thirty-nine kinds of work ends with carrying from one domain to another (verify). Healing was generally allowed on the sabbath only when life was in danger (verify the rabbinic sources). A man who had waited thirty-eight years was not in danger of dying that afternoon. So by their rules Jesus broke the sabbath twice: by healing a chronic illness, and by telling the man to carry his mat.

**Philologist:** John's sentence gives the reason in their minds. The verb is *eluen* (G3089, John 5:18, V-IAI-3S), 'he was loosing'. The clause 'because he not only had broken the sabbath, but said also that God was his Father' lists the two charges they held against him; the second is certainly their inference ('making himself equal'), and John reports it because they understood him rightly. The first is reported in the same breath. The grammar does not force us to read it as John's own verdict.

**Theologian:** And the rest of Scripture settles it. Jesus was 'in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin' (Hebrews 4:15); he kept the law of God perfectly. What he broke were the traditions that had grown up around it. In John 7 he argues from their own practice: they circumcise on the sabbath to keep the law of Moses, so why be angry 'because I have made a man every whit whole on the sabbath day?' (John 7:23). In Mark he asks whether it is lawful on the sabbath 'to do good' or 'to do evil' (Mark 3:4), and he calls himself 'Lord also of the sabbath' (Mark 2:28). In John 5 the answer goes deeper still: the Father works on the sabbath, and so does the Son (John 5:17). Jesus does not break the sabbath; he fulfils its purpose of rest and life, and he claims the Father's own freedom within it.

**Pastor:** For our people, the question is not first about first-century rules but about our own hearts. The authorities met a man walking for the first time in thirty-eight years and saw only a rule. Every church can do the same: notice who sat in the wrong place, sang the wrong song, or came dressed the wrong way, and miss what God is doing in them.

**Resolution:** John 5:18 reports the two charges the authorities held against Jesus. The second, that he made himself equal with God, is confirmed by Jesus himself in John 5:19–23; the first reflects their interpretation of the sabbath, which Jesus answers from the Father's own working (John 5:17) and elsewhere from the law's purpose (John 7:22–24). Scripture's witness is that Jesus was without sin (Hebrews 4:15) and kept his Father's law perfectly, while setting aside human traditions that turned the day of rest against the man made whole: "For the Son of man is Lord even of the sabbath day." (Matthew 12:8)

### ❓ Question 2 · The Angel at the Pool
**Moderator:** The KJV prints John 5:3b–4: the waiting for the moving of the water and the angel who troubled it. Many modern Bibles put these words in a footnote. How should we teach them, and what if someone in the group asks whether they belong in the Bible?

**Church Historian:** The words were known very early. Tertullian, writing around AD 200 on baptism, mentions an angel stirring the pool at Bethsaida, which is the same pool (verify). So the tradition goes back at least that far. At the same time, the earliest Greek manuscripts we have of John, papyri from around AD 200 and the great codices of the fourth century, do not include them (verify). The later manuscripts, which the Textus Receptus and the KJV follow, do.

**Philologist:** The internal evidence is mixed. On one side, several words in verse 4 appear nowhere else in the New Testament, including *nosēmati* (G3553, John 5:4), 'disease', and the wording varies from manuscript to manuscript, which is often a sign of later additions. On the other side, verse 7 makes little sense without some explanation of 'when the water is troubled'; the sick man plainly believes that the first one in is healed. The earliest scribes may have added verse 4 to explain verse 7, or dropped it because it seemed superstitious. Honest scholars weigh these differently.

**Literary Scholar:** Whichever way the question is settled, the story's meaning is the same. John sets the pool, with its crowd of the helpless competing for a rare healing, against Jesus, who heals the one man the system could never help, instantly and by a word. If verse 4 describes what happened, it shows God's occasional mercy through the pool and the cruelty of a healing only the least disabled could reach; if it describes what people believed, it shows a hope that had failed this man for thirty-eight years. Either way, the pool is what Jesus bypasses.

**Teacher:** In the group I would read the text as the KJV prints it, and if someone's Bible has a footnote, I would say simply: 'Some of the oldest manuscripts do not have these words; others do; the KJV includes them. Good Christians disagree about whether John wrote them. Nothing we believe depends on it, and the point of the story is the same either way.' Then I would move on to Jesus' question, 'Wilt thou be made whole?' That is where the passage wants us to look.

**Resolution:** This study teaches John 5:3–4 as the KJV prints it, in keeping with the decision for this course, and notes calmly that many modern editions omit the words because several of the earliest manuscripts lack them, while others defend them from early testimony and from John 5:7. No doctrine depends on the question. What the passage teaches is secure on either reading: the healing came not by the water but by the word of the Son, to a man who had 'no man' to help him: "Jesus saith unto him, Rise, take up thy bed, and walk." (John 5:8)

### ❓ Question 3 · Judged by Works
**Moderator:** John 5:24 says whoever hears and believes 'shall not come into condemnation'. Five verses later Jesus says 'they that have done good' will rise to life and 'they that have done evil' to damnation (John 5:29). Does Jesus teach salvation by works after all?

**Theologian:** No, but he does teach that the final judgment will be according to works, as the whole Bible does: God 'will render to every man according to his deeds' (Romans 2:6), and we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ (2 Corinthians 5:10). The key is to let John define 'doing good'. When the crowd asks what they must do to work the works of God, Jesus answers: "Jesus answered and said unto them, This is the work of God, that ye believe on him whom he hath sent." (John 6:29) And John has already told us that people come to the light or flee from it according to their deeds (John 3:19–21). Faith is the root; good works are the fruit. Those who have passed from death to life show it by what they do.

**Church Historian:** The church has read the verse in more than one way. Roman Catholic theology, as defined at the Council of Trent (sixth session, verify), teaches that good works done in grace truly merit an increase of grace and eternal life; its defenders appeal to texts like this one and want to honour the reality of good works. The Reformers answered that justification is by faith alone, and that good works are the necessary evidence of that faith, not its ground. Calvin, for example, took the verse to mark out believers by the fruit of their faith (verify). Some liberal scholars simply set John 5:29 against John 5:24 and call it a contradiction.

**Pastor:** People in our group will feel the weight of this. Some will hear John 5:29 and despair: 'Have I done enough good?' Others will hear John 5:24 and shrug: 'I believed once, so it doesn't matter how I live.' The passage answers both. To the first: the one who hears and believes 'hath everlasting life', now. To the second: the one who has truly crossed from death to life will not be comfortable doing evil.

**Literary Scholar:** Notice the order in the chapter. The promise to believers (John 5:24) comes before the description of the resurrection (John 5:28–29), and the whole speech is framed by a man who was healed by grace before he did anything, and then told to sin no more (John 5:14). John's own letter holds the two together in one sentence: 'We know that we have passed from death unto life, because we love the brethren' (1 John 3:14). The love is not the cause of the crossing; it is how we know it has happened.

**Resolution:** John 5:24 and John 5:29 do not contradict each other. Eternal life is received by hearing Christ's word and believing, and the believer will not come into condemnation. The final judgment, which belongs to the Son, will be according to what people have done, and those who have believed will be found among 'they that have done good', because the work God requires is faith in his Son and faith bears fruit. "For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them." (Ephesians 2:10)

### 🔦 The Life & Light Audit
**Pastor:** If a hearer obeyed this lesson exactly, then tonight they would read John 5:24 aloud and thank God that they have passed from death to life, and they would name to the Lord the person whose approval they most crave. Tomorrow they would contact one housebound, chronically ill, or forgotten person and ask what they most long for, as Jesus asked, and they would send John 5:24 with a sentence of testimony to one friend who feels stuck or dead. The dark place lit would be the love of human honour in their own heart, and, for some, a habit of reading the Bible as a source of knowledge or reputation rather than a road to Christ.

**Theologian:** The practices keep source discipline. None asks the hearer to make anyone alive; the Son quickens whom he will, and the hearer's part is to be 'the man' who brings people where they can hear his voice, and to be a lamp that points to him, as John was. The Inward practices are acts of receiving and confessing. The Abundance Guard is kept by the lesson's refusal to promise healing: the man's legs were a sign, and what Christ guarantees is everlasting life and resurrection.

**Teacher:** A Level 1 participant can do the Inward Life practice tonight: read one verse aloud three times and pray a sentence. The Outward practices name the kind of person to reach (housebound, chronically ill, long forgotten; a friend who feels spiritually dead; a person with no way to get to church) and leave a blank for the name. The Light practices are concrete: replace one criticism with an encouragement, and read one chapter of John with a friend.

**Fix:** The Audit found two risks and the unit has addressed them. First, the chapter's severe words to the authorities could be turned into permission to attack 'Pharisees' in other churches; so the Light questions and practices turn the lamp first on the participant's own love of honour and fault-finding, the Common Errors table corrects the misuse, and the Leader's Map below coaches the member who points the light at others. Second, John 5:14 could wound the chronically ill by suggesting that their illness is their fault; so the Walkthrough, the Cultural Landmines, and the Shepherding Notes all set John 5:14 beside John 9:3, and the Preaching Mistakes name the danger. The naming round reads only the second row of the Light Planner, never the private first row.

### ✅ Answer Key
**Q1.** Jesus went up to Jerusalem for 'a feast of the Jews' that John does not name (John 5:1). The pool was by the sheep gate (the KJV says 'sheep market'), was called Bethesda, and had five porches (John 5:2). In the porches lay 'a great multitude of impotent folk, of blind, halt, withered' (John 5:3).

**Q2.** He had been ill for thirty-eight years (John 5:5). Jesus asked, 'Wilt thou be made whole?' (John 5:6) The man did not say yes; he explained that he had no one to put him into the pool when the water was troubled, and someone else always got in first (John 5:7).

**Q3.** 'Rise, take up thy bed, and walk' (John 5:8). The man was made whole 'immediately', took up his bed and walked, and it was the sabbath (John 5:9).

**Q4.** They said, 'It is the sabbath day: it is not lawful for thee to carry thy bed' (John 5:10). He answered that the one who made him whole told him to (John 5:11). He did not know who it was, because Jesus had slipped away in the crowd (John 5:13).

**Q5.** In the temple (John 5:14). Jesus said, 'Behold, thou art made whole: sin no more, lest a worse thing come unto thee' (John 5:14). The man went and told the Jews that it was Jesus who had made him whole (John 5:15).

**Q6.** Because he had done these things on the sabbath (John 5:16), and because he 'said also that God was his Father, making himself equal with God' (John 5:18).

**Q7.** Another, the Father (John 5:32, 37); John the Baptist (John 5:33–35); the works the Father gave him to finish (John 5:36); the Scriptures (John 5:39); and Moses (John 5:45–46). The five usually counted are John, the works, the Father, the Scriptures, and Moses.

**Q8.** God alone gives life and takes it (Deuteronomy 32:39), and he gave to one like the Son of man an everlasting dominion (Daniel 7:13–14). He will raise the dead, some to everlasting life and some to contempt (Daniel 12:2). God rested from creation (Genesis 2:2–3), but he continues to work, and Jesus claims to share that work. Moses promised a Prophet whom Israel must hear (Deuteronomy 18:15–19), and the risen Jesus showed that all the Scriptures speak of him (Luke 24:27). Together they show that Jesus is the divine Son of man who gives life and judges, and that the Old Testament points to him.

**Q9.** 'My Father' claimed a unique relation to God, and 'I work' claimed the right to do what God does on the sabbath (John 5:17–18). Jesus confirms their understanding: he does whatever the Father does, gives life as the Father does, holds all judgment, and must be honoured as the Father is (John 5:19–23).

**Q10.** Jesus warns the man to stop sinning and speaks of something worse than his illness (John 5:14), but he does not say that the illness was caused by a particular sin; in John 9:3 he denies that the blind man's condition was punishment for his or his parents' sin. The 'worse thing' is most naturally the judgment of which he speaks later, 'the resurrection of damnation' (John 5:29).

**Q11.** In John 5:25 the hour 'now is'; the dead are the spiritually dead; those who hear 'shall live'. In John 5:28–29 the hour 'is coming' only; the dead are 'all that are in the graves'; they 'shall come forth' to life or to damnation. The first is the spiritual resurrection of new birth now; the second is the bodily resurrection of all people at the end (Daniel 12:2).

**Q12.** Model answer, not the only answer: View B fits the flow of John 5:39–40 somewhat better, because 'in them ye think ye have eternal life' describes what the hearers were already doing, and verse 40 sets their searching against their refusal to come. But View A is grammatically possible and true to the rest of Scripture (Acts 17:11). Both lead to the same point: the Scriptures 'testify of me' (John 5:39). Let members who hold View A state it at its strongest.

**Q13.** Saul still searched the Scriptures, but he read them now as witnesses to Christ, not as a path to his own righteousness and honour (Philippians 3:7–9; Acts 17:2–3). The change came when the risen Christ spoke to him (Acts 9:4–5). Answers to the second part will vary.

**Q14.** Answers will vary: Bible reading as a habit or achievement, church service for recognition, theological knowledge used to win arguments. Coming to Christ means bringing him our need and trusting him for life (John 5:40), for example by praying before reading, 'Show me Jesus, and help me come to him.'

**Q15.** Model answer, not the only answer: if the Son were a creature, he could not do all that the Father does, give life in his own right, or receive the Father's honour (John 5:19, 21, 23, 26). Only one who has life in himself can give eternal life, and only God can save; if Jesus were less than God, we would be trusting a creature for what only God can give (Isaiah 43:11).

**Q16.** A person must hear Christ's word and believe the Father who sent him (John 5:24). They have everlasting life now, in the present tense; they will not come into condemnation; and they have already passed from death to life. It is a gift received by faith, not wages earned by effort (Ephesians 2:8).

**Q17.** Model answer: Christ seeks people out even when they hardly know him, as he went and found the man in the temple (John 5:14). That means my poor knowledge of him has never stopped his pursuit of me; it moves me to thankfulness and to trust him more.

**Q18.** Answers will vary: parents, a spouse, colleagues, friends, a church community, social media. Wanting their approval can make us hide our faith, avoid obedience that would cost us, or believe only what is acceptable to them (John 5:44; John 12:43).

**Q19.** Answers will vary: speaking of Jesus openly in a conversation, praying before a decision, worshipping him by name in private prayer. Humbling ourselves means confessing that we have often honoured him less than he deserves (John 5:23).

**Q20.** Model answer, not the only answer: the Son, though equal with God, lives in total dependence on and submission to his Father (John 5:19, 30). If he, who has life in himself, 'can do nothing of himself', our proud self-reliance is exposed as foolish. His humility makes him more glorious, because his glory is not grasped but received and shared (Philippians 2:6–8).

**Q21.** Model answer, not the only answer: a friend with a long illness or disability, someone years into unemployment or addiction, a lonely elderly neighbour, a person who has given up on God. 'Sir, I have no man' (John 5:7).

**Q22.** Model answer, not the only answer: the thief may have stolen their hope that anything can change, their sense that anyone cares, or their life toward God. He comes "to steal, and to kill, and to destroy" (John 10:10).

**Q23.** Model answer, not the only answer: Christ gives life by his word to whom he will, and whoever hears and believes has everlasting life (John 5:21, 24). It is good news that the man did not need to reach the water first, because none of us can reach life by our own strength; the healer comes to us (John 5:8).

**Q24.** Model answer, not the only answer: a neighbour I could drive to church, a friend I could read the Gospel of John with, a relative I could visit and pray with. We can be the 'man' who brings them where they can hear his voice (John 5:7, 25; Mark 2:3). What stops us is often busyness, fear, or the thought that someone else will do it.

**Q25.** Model answer, not the only answer: wanting to be seen as spiritual, successful, kind, or right. The love of human approval can make us hide our faith, avoid confession, or perform for others (John 5:44). Jesus names it so that we may be free.

**Q26.** Model answer, not the only answer: noticing someone's wrong clothes, wrong words, or wrong habits at church before seeing the new life God is giving them. Jesus rejoiced in the man made whole; the authorities saw only the bed (John 5:10–14).

**Q27.** Model answer, not the only answer: a friend who grew up in church, a family member who knows Bible stories, a colleague who studies religion. Invite them to read John with you, ask gently what they make of Jesus, and share how the Scriptures led you to him (John 5:39–40; John 5:35).

### 🗺️ Life & Light Leader's Map
**Worked Life Planner Row:** *Who:* Mrs. Alvarez, an elderly widow two doors down who has been housebound with arthritis for six years, whose children live far away, and who told me once that nobody comes by anymore. *What the thief has stolen:* company, hope, and the sense that God has not forgotten her. *What Christ gives instead:* the Son who went to the one man nobody helped and gave him life by his word (John 5:6–8, 24). *What I will do this week:* on Wednesday afternoon, knock on her door with a loaf of bread, ask her what she most longs for, and, if she is willing, read her John 5:1–9 and pray with her; on Sunday, offer to bring her to church or bring the sermon to her. *Done?* A tick, and a note of what she said.

**Worked Light Planner Row:** *Where it is dark:* my own heart: I love being known as the person in our group who knows the Bible best, and I feel stung when someone else answers first. *What is hidden there:* the love of honour from people (John 5:44), and Bible study that has become more about me than about Christ. *What light Christ shines:* the Scriptures 'testify of me' (John 5:39), and faith seeks the honour that comes from God only. *How I carry it:* tonight, confess it to the Lord by name; at the next study, deliberately invite a quieter member to answer first and say nothing clever myself. This first row stays private; in the naming round members read their second row.

**When Someone Has No One to Name:** Do not accept 'no one' too quickly, and do not shame them. John 5 gives a clear picture: someone who is stuck, forgotten, or has 'no man' to help them. Ask: 'Who is housebound on your street or in our church? Who has been ill, unemployed, or grieving for a long time? Who has stopped coming, and nobody has called?' Most people can name one. If they still cannot, suggest that they ask the Lord this week to show them one person they have been walking past, since Jesus saw the one man in a great multitude (John 5:5–6).

**When Someone Points the Light at Others:** John 5 can easily become a weapon: 'Jesus exposed the Pharisees, so I will expose the legalists at my old church.' When a member names another church's or another person's 'Pharisaism' as their dark place, bring them gently back to the first row of their Light Planner. Remind the group that Jesus alone knows every heart ('I know you', John 5:42), that even his severe words were spoken 'that ye might be saved' (John 5:34), and that the chapter's warning about honour from men is aimed at religious people, which includes us. If there is a real concern about another person's sin, point to the private, humble way of Matthew 18:15, after the beam has come out of our own eye (Matthew 7:5).

### 🐑 Shepherding Notes
This chapter touches several tender places. Handle them with care.

- **Chronic illness and disability.** Members who live with long-term illness or disability, or care for someone who does, may hear this chapter with longing or with pain. Do not suggest that more faith would heal them; the man at the pool had no faith in Jesus at all before he was healed. Say clearly that the healing was a sign of the life Jesus gives, that he sees the one person in the crowd, and that the final answer is the resurrection (John 5:28–29).
- **Sickness and sin.** Some may have been told that their illness is punishment for sin. Read John 9:3 beside John 5:14. Sin is serious, and there is something worse than illness, but we are not to presume we know why someone suffers.
- **Loneliness.** 'I have no man' may be the sentence of someone in the room. Watch for members who are isolated, and make sure the group itself becomes the help they lack.
- **Bible knowledge and pride.** Longtime believers and teachers may feel exposed by John 5:39–44. Speak to yourself first. The aim is not to shame knowledge of Scripture but to turn it toward Christ.
- **Fear of judgment.** John 5:28–29 may frighten tender consciences. Point them back to John 5:24: the believer 'shall not come into condemnation'. Assurance rests on Christ's word, not on the feeling of having done enough.
- **Anti-Jewish readings.** If anyone speaks of 'the Jews' with contempt, gently correct it (see the Cultural Landmines). Jesus, his disciples, and the healed man were Jews.
- **The Light Planner's first row.** It is private: never ask anyone to read it aloud, and say so at the start of the naming round. If someone chooses to share, receive it quietly, pray, and keep it in the group.

### 🎯 Teaching Angles
**For a Tier 1 Group:** Bring a rolled-up mat or blanket. Tell the story of the man at the pool simply, and ask a volunteer to lie on the mat while you read John 5:5–9 aloud; at 'Rise, take up thy bed, and walk', have them roll it up and carry it. Ask: How long had the man been sick? Who helped him into the water? How did Jesus heal him? Why were some people angry? Then read John 5:24 and explain that Jesus gives a life that starts now and never ends. Use the Tier 1 section, and end by saying John 5:24 together.

**For a Tier 2 Group:** Trace three lines through the chapter. The line of equality: working, giving life, judging, being honoured, having life in himself (John 5:17–27). The line of witness: John, the works, the Father, the Scriptures, Moses (John 5:31–47); the word 'Father' comes 15 times in this chapter, and 'honour' in its forms 7 times. The line of the courtroom: Jesus is accused of breaking the sabbath and blasphemy, calls his witnesses, and ends by telling his accusers that Moses accuses them. Then set the helpless man beside the learned men: one who could not come, and was found; others who 'will not come' (John 5:40).

**Tough Question 1:** *If Jesus is equal with God, why does he say 'The Son can do nothing of himself' and 'I seek not mine own will'?* These words describe the Son's perfect unity with the Father, not a lack of power: he never acts apart from the Father, and he does everything the Father does (John 5:19). As the incarnate Son he also lived in perfect human obedience to his Father (John 5:30). Equality of nature and order in relationship are not in conflict; a son can be fully equal to his father in nature and still gladly honour him.

**Tough Question 2:** *Did Jesus heal only one man and leave the rest of the multitude lying there? Is that fair?* John tells us that Jesus healed one man that day, and he does not tell us why that one. The healing was a sign, not a program to end all sickness in Jerusalem. It pointed to the life the Son gives 'whom he will' (John 5:21) and to the resurrection, when 'all that are in the graves' will hear his voice (John 5:28). Our questions about why God heals one and not another are real, and Scripture does not answer them all; it does show us a Saviour who saw the one no one else helped, and who will one day make all things new.

**Tough Question 3:** *Does John 5:39 mean that we should not study the Bible so much, and just 'come to Jesus'?* No. Jesus does not criticise the Scriptures or the study of them; he says they 'testify of me' (John 5:39). The problem was studying them without coming to him. The answer is not less Bible but Bible read as witness: study it deeply, and let it lead you to Christ (Luke 24:27; Acts 17:11).
