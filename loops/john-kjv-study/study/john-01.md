## 🧭 At a Glance
### 🧵 Golden Thread
The eternal Word, who is God, became flesh to make the Father known, and all who receive him are born of God and become his sons.

### 🌗 Life & Light Line
John 1 answers a world that was made by the Word and yet did not know him, and people who cannot give themselves new birth by blood, effort, or another's will, with the Word in whom was life, who gives all who receive him power to become the sons of God. It exposes a darkness that did not comprehend the light, even among the religious who came asking questions, and it gives the light of the only begotten Son, who has declared the God no man has seen.

## 🏛️ Core Foundations
### ✝️ Christological Root
**Gospel Foundation:** John 1 rests on what the Word did and on what Jesus said. What he did is the center of the Prologue: "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth." (John 1:14) What he said closes the chapter with a promise about what his coming opens: "And he saith unto him, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Hereafter ye shall see heaven open, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of man." (John 1:51) Between the two stands his first invitation, simple enough for anyone: "Come and see" (John 1:39).

**The Bridge:** The doctrine grounded here is the incarnation: the eternal Word, who "was God" (John 1:1), became what he was not, flesh, without ceasing to be what he was. Because he is God, he can make God known: "the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him" (John 1:18). Because he became flesh, he can be the tabernacle where God dwells with men, the Lamb who dies for sinners, and the ladder that joins earth to heaven. Every title in the chapter depends on both truths at once.

**Canonical Echoes:** Jesus (John 1:14) → Promise (Exodus 25:8) → Doctrine (Colossians 2:9) → Life (Revelation 21:3)

**What Gets Lost:** Taught without Christ at the center, the Prologue becomes a meditation on reason or a poem about light, John the Baptist becomes a model of modest leadership, and the calling of the disciples becomes a lesson in networking. Lost is the one thing the chapter insists on: the Maker of the world came into it in person, God is known only in him, and the sin of the world is taken away by a Lamb whom God provided.

### ⚔️ Spiritual Warfare
**The Lie:** God is out of reach and cannot really be known, so every opinion about him is as good as another; and Jesus, however admirable, is one light among many, a teacher or a prophet, but not God.

**The Truth:** God has made himself known in one person: "No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him." (John 1:18) And that person is no creature: "the Word was God" (John 1:1).

**The Battleground:** The mind's picture of Jesus, and especially the prejudice that decides before it looks, as Nathanael did: "Can there any good thing come out of Nazareth?" (John 1:46)

**The Weapon:** Witness and invitation. John bore record of what he saw (John 1:34); Philip did not argue with Nathanael but answered, "Come and see" (John 1:46). The lie is overthrown not by cleverness but by bringing a person to Jesus and letting the Word speak.

### 🌱 Life Pathway
**The Theft:** In John 1 the thief works through rejection and false sources. The Word made the world, "and the world knew him not" (John 1:10); "He came unto his own, and his own received him not." (John 1:11) People look for life where it cannot be found: in blood (ancestry and heritage), in the will of the flesh (desire and effort), in the will of man (someone else's decision on their behalf) (John 1:13). And the leaders who came to investigate John missed the Life standing in their midst: "there standeth one among you, whom ye know not" (John 1:26).

**The Source:** The Word himself: "In him was life; and the life was the light of men." (John 1:4) His means in this chapter are his word, the witness of others, and his own invitation, and his gift is a new birth: "to them gave he power to become the sons of God" (John 1:12). He came "that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly" (John 10:10).

**The Channel:** John the Baptist, Andrew, and Philip pass on what they have received. Andrew "first findeth his own brother Simon" (John 1:41), "And he brought him to Jesus." (John 1:42) None of them is the source: John "was not that Light, but was sent to bear witness of that Light" (John 1:8). A believer carries life by bringing people to the one who gives it.

**The Abundance Guard:** Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, not prosperity, ease, or freedom from suffering. The Word who is life came to his own and was refused; the Lamb of God takes away sin by being slain. The grace we receive "of his fulness" (John 1:16) is himself, not a smoother road.

### 💡 Light Pathway
**The Darkness:** A darkness that "comprehended it not" (John 1:5); a world that "knew him not" (John 1:10); and a religious darkness that sends a committee to ask "Who art thou?" (John 1:19), wanting an answer to carry back rather than a Lord to follow, and a quick prejudice that writes off a person because of where he comes from.

**The Source:** "That was the true Light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world." (John 1:9) "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts" (2 Corinthians 4:6), and he shines there in the face of Jesus Christ.

**The Lamp:** Witness that points away from itself (John 1:7–8, 20); the Scriptures, since Moses and the prophets wrote of him (John 1:45); the honest invitation to come and see (John 1:46); hidden sin confessed to the Lamb who takes it away (John 1:29); the unfruitful works of darkness reproved (Ephesians 5:11); and good works that lead others to glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16).

**The Love Guard:** Light without love is glare. John bore witness to the Light; he did not shine it in anyone's face, and he spoke most sharply about himself ('I am not'). When Nathanael sneered at Nazareth, Jesus answered him with kindness and knowledge, not a rebuke. We carry the light the same way.

### 🏺 Setting the Scene
The Gospel of John opens not in Bethlehem or at the Jordan but before creation. Its first words echo the first words of the Bible, Genesis 1:1, and its key term, 'the Word', would have spoken to two audiences at once. A Jewish reader knew that God made the world by speaking (Genesis 1:3; Psalm 33:6) and that 'the word of the LORD' came to the prophets. A Greek reader knew *logos* from the philosophers, who used it for the reason that orders the universe; the Jewish writer Philo of Alexandria, a contemporary of Jesus, had already used it to bridge the two worlds. John begins with Genesis, but he says something neither audience expected: the Word is a person, he is God, and he became flesh. The church has received the author as John the son of Zebedee, writing late in the first century; that is tradition, and the Gospel itself never names him. In this Gospel 'John' always means John the Baptist.

After the Prologue (John 1:1–18), the chapter moves day by day: 'The next day' (John 1:29, 35) and 'The day following' (John 1:43), leading into 'the third day' of John 2:1. John the Baptist was a prophet preaching and baptizing at the Jordan. Jerusalem's leaders sent priests and Levites, the temple's officials, and Pharisees, the strict teachers of the law, to question him. Three figures were expected: the Christ, God's anointed king; Elijah, whom Malachi promised before the day of the LORD (Malachi 4:5); and 'that prophet', the Prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15). The KJV names the place Bethabara beyond Jordan; many modern editions read Bethany beyond Jordan, a different place from the Bethany near Jerusalem.

The last scenes move north to Galilee. Bethsaida was a fishing town on the Sea of Galilee; Nazareth was a small village that the Old Testament never mentions. John times the first visit 'about the tenth hour' (John 1:39): counted from sunrise, as Jews usually counted, that is about four in the afternoon. Watch for the titles given to Jesus as you read. The chapter gives him more than a dozen.

#### John 1:1–5 · The Word Before All Things
**1** In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.

**2** The same was in the beginning with God.

**3** All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made.

**4** In him was life; and the life was the light of men.

**5** And the light shineth in darkness; and the darkness comprehended it not.

#### John 1:6–13 · The Witness and the Light
**6** There was a man sent from God, whose name was John.

**7** The same came for a witness, to bear witness of the Light, that all men through him might believe.

**8** He was not that Light, but was sent to bear witness of that Light.

**9** That was the true Light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world.

**10** He was in the world, and the world was made by him, and the world knew him not.

**11** He came unto his own, and his own received him not.

**12** But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name:

**13** Which were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God.

#### John 1:14–18 · The Word Made Flesh
**14** And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth.

**15** John bare witness of him, and cried, saying, This was he of whom I spake, He that cometh after me is preferred before me: for he was before me.

**16** And of his fulness have all we received, and grace for grace.

**17** For the law was given by Moses, but grace and truth came by Jesus Christ.

**18** No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him.

#### John 1:19–28 · The Voice in the Wilderness
**19** And this is the record of John, when the Jews sent priests and Levites from Jerusalem to ask him, Who art thou?

**20** And he confessed, and denied not; but confessed, I am not the Christ.

**21** And they asked him, What then? Art thou Elias? And he saith, I am not. Art thou that prophet? And he answered, No.

**22** Then said they unto him, Who art thou? that we may give an answer to them that sent us. What sayest thou of thyself?

**23** He said, I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness, Make straight the way of the Lord, as said the prophet Esaias.

**24** And they which were sent were of the Pharisees.

**25** And they asked him, and said unto him, Why baptizest thou then, if thou be not that Christ, nor Elias, neither that prophet?

**26** John answered them, saying, I baptize with water: but there standeth one among you, whom ye know not;

**27** He it is, who coming after me is preferred before me, whose shoe's latchet I am not worthy to unloose.

**28** These things were done in Bethabara beyond Jordan, where John was baptizing.

#### John 1:29–34 · Behold the Lamb of God
**29** The next day John seeth Jesus coming unto him, and saith, Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world.

**30** This is he of whom I said, After me cometh a man which is preferred before me: for he was before me.

**31** And I knew him not: but that he should be made manifest to Israel, therefore am I come baptizing with water.

**32** And John bare record, saying, I saw the Spirit descending from heaven like a dove, and it abode upon him.

**33** And I knew him not: but he that sent me to baptize with water, the same said unto me, Upon whom thou shalt see the Spirit descending, and remaining on him, the same is he which baptizeth with the Holy Ghost.

**34** And I saw, and bare record that this is the Son of God.

#### John 1:35–42 · Come and See
**35** Again the next day after John stood, and two of his disciples;

**36** And looking upon Jesus as he walked, he saith, Behold the Lamb of God!

**37** And the two disciples heard him speak, and they followed Jesus.

**38** Then Jesus turned, and saw them following, and saith unto them, What seek ye? They said unto him, Rabbi, (which is to say, being interpreted, Master,) where dwellest thou?

**39** He saith unto them, Come and see. They came and saw where he dwelt, and abode with him that day: for it was about the tenth hour.

**40** One of the two which heard John speak, and followed him, was Andrew, Simon Peter's brother.

**41** He first findeth his own brother Simon, and saith unto him, We have found the Messias, which is, being interpreted, the Christ.

**42** And he brought him to Jesus. And when Jesus beheld him, he said, Thou art Simon the son of Jona: thou shalt be called Cephas, which is by interpretation, A stone.

#### John 1:43–51 · An Israelite Indeed
**43** The day following Jesus would go forth into Galilee, and findeth Philip, and saith unto him, Follow me.

**44** Now Philip was of Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter.

**45** Philip findeth Nathanael, and saith unto him, We have found him, of whom Moses in the law, and the prophets, did write, Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph.

**46** And Nathanael said unto him, Can there any good thing come out of Nazareth? Philip saith unto him, Come and see.

**47** Jesus saw Nathanael coming to him, and saith of him, Behold an Israelite indeed, in whom is no guile!

**48** Nathanael saith unto him, Whence knowest thou me? Jesus answered and said unto him, Before that Philip called thee, when thou wast under the fig tree, I saw thee.

**49** Nathanael answered and saith unto him, Rabbi, thou art the Son of God; thou art the King of Israel.

**50** Jesus answered and said unto him, Because I said unto thee, I saw thee under the fig tree, believest thou? thou shalt see greater things than these.

**51** And he saith unto him, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Hereafter ye shall see heaven open, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of man.

### 👣 Case Study · Scripture in Action
Moses had spoken with God face to face, as a man speaks with his friend (Exodus 33:11), yet after the golden calf he asked for more: "I beseech thee, shew me thy glory." (Exodus 33:18) God answered that he would make all his goodness pass before Moses and proclaim his name, but, "Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live." (Exodus 33:20) He hid Moses in a cleft of the rock, covered him with his hand, and let him see only his back as his glory passed by (Exodus 33:22–23). Then the LORD proclaimed himself "merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6). Moses received the law, heard the name, and saw the edge of the glory. He longed for more than he was given, and he rightly went no further than God allowed. John writes with this scene in view: "For the law was given by Moses, but grace and truth came by Jesus Christ." (John 1:17)

### 🏙️ Modern Example
Daniel is an engineer, and at lunch a colleague he respects said that Jesus was clearly a great moral teacher, but that calling him God was something the church invented centuries later. Daniel felt the old urge to win the argument, and he knew he could not answer every point. That evening he read John 1 again and noticed how the chapter itself handles doubt. Nathanael sneers at Nazareth, and Philip does not debate him; he says, 'Come and see.' The next day Daniel told his colleague that he did not have every answer, but that the earliest Christians plainly called Jesus God, and that the best way to decide was to read one of the Gospels for himself. He offered to read John with him over lunch, a few verses a week. His colleague said he would think about it. Daniel is still praying, and he has stopped trying to be the light himself.

### 📜 Voices from Church History
The Nicene Creed, drawn up at the Council of Nicaea in 325 and enlarged at Constantinople in 381, was written against Arius, a presbyter of Alexandria who taught that the Son was the first and highest creature, so that there was a time when he was not. In summary (paraphrased here, not quoted), the creed confesses one Lord Jesus Christ: the only Son of God, eternally begotten of the Father rather than made; God from God and light from light; sharing the one being of the Father; the one through whom all things were made; who for us and for our salvation came down from heaven and became man. Almost every line of that confession is drawn from John 1: the Word in the beginning, with God and God, the maker of all things, the light, the only begotten, and the Word made flesh.

### ⛪ Doctrine in My Own Words
- **Theology Proper:** The one true God is known only as the Son makes him known, for the Word was with God and was God (John 1:1, 18).
- **Christology:** The Word is eternal and fully God, the maker of all things and not himself made (John 1:1–3).
- **Christology:** The eternal Word became truly human and dwelt among us, full of grace and truth, without ceasing to be God (John 1:14).
- **Pneumatology:** The Spirit descended and remained on Jesus, and Jesus is the one who baptizes with the Holy Ghost (John 1:33).
- **Anthropology & Sin:** The world made by the Word is in darkness and does not know or receive him on its own (John 1:10–11).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** All who receive Christ are given the right to become God's children, born not of human will but of God (John 1:12–13).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** Jesus is the Lamb of God, provided by God, who takes away the sin of the world (John 1:29).
- **Christian Walk:** A disciple follows Jesus, stays with him, and brings others to come and see (John 1:37–46).

### 👑 Christocentric & Humility · Level 4
Every problem in John 1 is answered in Jesus. No one has seen God, and the only begotten Son has declared him. The world is dark, and he is the true Light. The world did not know him and his own did not receive him, yet he gives to those who receive him the power to become God's children. No one can give himself new birth, and those who believe are born of God. The sin of the world cannot be removed by any priest or Levite, and he is the Lamb of God who takes it away. Heaven is shut to sinners, and in him heaven stands open. Every truth in the chapter is found in him too: the life that is the light of men, the glory that filled the tabernacle, the grace and truth that Moses heard proclaimed, the Spirit who remains, and the fulfilment of everything Moses and the prophets wrote.

The chapter humbles us as well. The greatest prophet of his age answers every question about himself with 'I am not', and calls himself a voice and not the Word, unworthy even to untie the Lord's sandal. Andrew is remembered chiefly as the man who brought his brother. Nathanael is loved and known before he has made up his mind. Humility here is not thinking poorly of ourselves; it is being glad that Christ is greater, and getting out of the way so that he can be seen.

**Humility Practices**

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy
### 📌 Core Concept
Before there was a world, before there were stars or mountains or people, Jesus was already there with God, and he is God. John calls him 'the Word', because just as your words tell people what you are thinking, Jesus shows us what God is like. Everything that exists was made by him. Then one day he did something amazing: he became a real human baby and lived among us, so we could see God's goodness with our own eyes. Not everyone welcomed him. But everyone who does welcome him and trusts him becomes part of God's own family. John the Baptist pointed to Jesus and said he is the Lamb of God, who takes away our sins. And the first people who followed Jesus went and told their brothers and friends: come and see him.

### 📖 Key Verses
"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1) Jesus was with God before anything was made, and he is God.

"In him was life; and the life was the light of men." (John 1:4) Jesus gives real life, and he shows us the way like a light in the dark.

"But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name:" (John 1:12) Anyone who welcomes and trusts Jesus becomes one of God's children.

"The next day John seeth Jesus coming unto him, and saith, Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world." (John 1:29) Jesus came to take away the wrong things we have done.

### 🪞 Analogy
Imagine a grandfather who lives far away and writes you letters for years. The letters tell you a lot about him, and you treasure them. Then one day the doorbell rings, and he is standing on your porch, and he says he has come to live on your street. Now you do not only read about him; you can see his face, hear his laugh, and sit at his table. God spoke to his people for many years through the prophets, like letters. Then Jesus came, and God himself came to live among us.

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium
### 📜 Walkthrough
#### John 1:1–2 · In the Beginning Was the Word
**Says:** "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1) Then John repeats the thought: "The same was in the beginning with God." (John 1:2)

**Means:** The first three words are the first three words of the Bible: "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1) John means us to hear Genesis. But Genesis tells what God *did* in the beginning; John tells what the Word already *was*. The verb 'was' is *ēn* (G1510, John 1:1, V-IAI-3S), the imperfect of the verb 'to be', which describes continuing existence. When the beginning began, the Word already was. There was never a time when he was not.

The verse makes three claims in three clauses. First, existence: in the beginning the Word was. Second, relationship: the Word was 'with God', present with him in personal fellowship, and so distinct from him; one is not with oneself. (Some preachers say the preposition means 'face to face'. It expresses presence and relationship here, but the word alone cannot carry that picture, so do not press it.) Third, identity: 'the Word was God'. Not a god, not godlike, but God. John holds the second and third claims together without apology, and the rest of his Gospel explains how: the Word is the Son, and God is his Father (John 1:14, 18).

Why 'the Word'? In the Old Testament, God creates by speaking, "By the word of the LORD were the heavens made" (Psalm 33:6), and his word goes out and does what he sends it to do (Isaiah 55:11). A word expresses the mind of the one who speaks. So the Word is God's own self-expression: all that God is, spoken. Greek thinkers also used *logos* for the reason that orders the world, and John's readers would have heard that too. But John fills the word with something no philosopher said: the Word is a person, and he will become flesh. Verse 2 underlines that the Word's fellowship with God is as old as the beginning. Jesus will pray, "glorify thou me with thine own self with the glory which I had with thee before the world was" (John 17:5).

**Asks:** Before we ask what Jesus can do for us, we must ask who he is. The one who calls you to follow him is the eternal God. Let that set the tone of your worship and your obedience this week: you are not dealing with a religious teacher you may take or leave, but with your Maker.

#### John 1:3–5 · All Things Made, and Life and Light
**Says:** "All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made." (John 1:3) "In him was life; and the life was the light of men." (John 1:4) "And the light shineth in darkness; and the darkness comprehended it not." (John 1:5) *Comprehended* here means either grasped with the mind or seized and overcome (see the View A / View B panel).

**Means:** Verse 3 says the same thing twice, positively and negatively, so that nothing escapes it. Everything that came into being came into being through the Word. The verb changes: 'were made' is *egeneto* (G1096, John 1:3, V-2ADI-3S), 'came into being', a different verb from the 'was' of verse 1. Created things came to be; the Word simply was. That places the Word on the Creator's side of the line between God and everything else. Paul says the same: "For by him were all things created" (Colossians 1:16), and Hebrews says God made the worlds by his Son (Hebrews 1:2).

'In him was life': the Word does not merely have life, as creatures do; life is in him as in its fountain. "For with thee is the fountain of life: in thy light shall we see light." (Psalm 36:9) Life and light are two of John's great words. In this chapter 'light' occurs 6 times, and in the whole Gospel 24 times. In Genesis, light is the first thing God speaks into being (Genesis 1:3); here the light is the Word's own life shining on mankind.

'The light shineth' is present tense, *phainei* (G5316, John 1:5, V-PAI-3S): it is still shining as John writes. 'Comprehended' is past tense and looks back on a decisive moment. The darkness is the world as it has become, turned from God (John 3:19). The STEPBible lexicon places this verse under the sense 'to overtake', with John 12:35; the KJV's 'comprehended' leans toward understanding. Both are true of the Gospel story: the darkness never understood the light, and it never put it out, though on the night of the betrayal it seemed to have won: "and it was night" (John 13:30).

**Asks:** When the darkness around you seems to be winning, remember the tense: the light shines. And ask the harder question: have you let the light in, or have you only lived near it?

#### John 1:6–8 · A Man Sent from God
**Says:** "There was a man sent from God, whose name was John." (John 1:6) "The same came for a witness, to bear witness of the Light, that all men through him might believe." (John 1:7) "He was not that Light, but was sent to bear witness of that Light." (John 1:8)

**Means:** The contrast is sharp. The Word 'was' in the beginning; John 'was' (the Greek is the came-into-being verb again) a man, sent from God. 'Sent' is one of this Gospel's key words: John is sent, and later Jesus will speak of the Father 'that sent me' again and again.

John came 'for a witness'. The noun is *marturian* (G3141, John 1:7), testimony, the word of a courtroom. In this chapter the KJV's 'witness' occurs 4 times and 'record' 3 times, and they often render the same Greek: the 'record of John' in verse 19 is *marturia* (G3141, John 1:19), the very noun of verse 7. John's whole Gospel reads like a trial in which witnesses take the stand: John, the works, the Father, the scriptures, Moses (John 5:31–39). The purpose of John's witness is the purpose of the whole book: 'that all men through him might believe' (compare John 20:31).

Why insist, 'He was not that Light'? John the Baptist had a large following, and years later Paul met disciples at Ephesus who knew only John's baptism (Acts 19:1–7). Many think the Gospel writer is gently correcting anyone who ranked the Baptist too high; that is a likely background, not something John states. Either way, the Baptist himself would have agreed.

**Asks:** A witness is not the source. You are called to bear witness to the Light, not to be it. That is a relief (you do not have to shine on your own) and a responsibility (you do have to speak).

#### John 1:9–11 · The True Light and the World That Knew Him Not
**Says:** "That was the true Light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world." (John 1:9) "He was in the world, and the world was made by him, and the world knew him not." (John 1:10) "He came unto his own, and his own received him not." (John 1:11) *Lighteth* means gives light to.

**Means:** 'True' is *alēthinon* (G228, John 1:9): genuine, real, the reality rather than the copy. John uses the same word for the true bread (John 6:32) and the true vine (John 15:1). Other lights were real but lesser. Jesus will say of the Baptist, "He was a burning and a shining light" (John 5:35), and the Greek word there is *luchnos* (G3088, John 5:35), a lamp: a lamp is lit, while the Light is the source. God's word is "a light unto my path" (Psalm 119:105), but it is the word about him. Jesus is the Light itself.

The phrase 'that cometh into the world' can be read with 'every man' (as the KJV reads it: every human being born into the world) or with 'the true Light' (the Light was coming into the world). The Greek participle can go either way, and faithful translators differ; the next verses, which speak of the Light coming into the world, make the second reading attractive. What it means that he 'lighteth every man' is debated (see the Debate Corner in Tier 3).

Verse 10 uses 'world' three times in one verse, and the chapter uses it 5 times in all. Here the world is mankind, made by the Word and yet ignorant of its Maker. Verse 11 narrows the circle. In Greek the first 'his own' is neuter, his own things, his own home (*idia* (G2398, John 1:11)); the second is masculine, his own people (*idioi* (G2398, John 1:11)). He came home, and his household did not take him in. The KJV's repeated 'his own' is faithful but hides the shift. Israel, prepared for centuries, largely did not receive him; the rest of the Gospel tells how.

**Asks:** It is possible to live surrounded by his works, even inside his church, and not know him. Have you received him, or only lived in his house?

#### John 1:12–13 · Power to Become the Sons of God
**Says:** "But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name:" (John 1:12) "Which were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God." (John 1:13)

**Means:** 'But' turns the tragedy. 'As many as' sets no limit of nation, class, or past. To receive him is explained as to 'believe on his name', that is, to trust him as he truly is. The gift is 'power', *exousian* (G1849, John 1:12): the STEPBible lexicon gives 'liberty or power to act' and 'authority'; here it is the right or standing to become God's children. The KJV's 'sons' is *tekna* (G5043, John 1:12), children, those who are born (the lexicon's gloss is 'that which is begotten, born'). John usually keeps the word 'Son' for Jesus, the only begotten, and calls believers God's children, as in "Behold, what manner of love the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called the sons of God" (1 John 3:1), where the Greek is again the word for children.

Verse 13 then says how such children come to be, with three denials and one affirmation. Not 'of blood', literally 'bloods': not by ancestry or heritage. Not 'of the will of the flesh': not by human desire or effort. Not 'of the will of man': not by another person's decision on our behalf. 'But of God.' The verb *egennēthēsan* (G1080, John 1:13, V-API-3P) is passive: they were born. No one gives birth to himself. Jesus will tell Nicodemus, "Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." (John 3:3)

How do believing (verse 12) and being born of God (verse 13) relate? The verses set them side by side without saying which comes first. The Reformed tradition reads new birth as the source of faith, pointing to "Whosoever believeth that Jesus is the Christ is born of God" (1 John 5:1), where 'is born' is the perfect *gegennētai* (G1080, 1 John 5:1, V-RPI-3S), a birth already complete that stands behind the believing; others read faith as the condition God meets with new birth. All agree on what verse 13 denies: the new birth is not our achievement.

**Asks:** Have you received him? Not your parents' faith, not your church's membership roll, not your own resolve, but him. And if you have, rest: your place in God's family is God's doing, and he does not undo it.

#### John 1:14 · The Word Made Flesh
**Says:** "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth." (John 1:14) *Dwelt* here means pitched his tent; *only begotten* means the one and only son; *beheld* means looked upon.

**Means:** This is the hinge of the Prologue. The Word 'was made', again *egeneto*, 'became': the eternal one who always 'was' now became something he had not been. He became 'flesh', *sarx* (G4561, John 1:14), full human nature in its weakness and mortality: a body, a human mind and will, hunger and weariness and tears (John 4:6; John 11:35). He did not stop being what he was. The Word who 'was God' (John 1:1) became flesh without ceasing to be God. Nor did he only seem human, as some early teachers claimed; John's letter makes confessing that 'Jesus Christ is come in the flesh' the test of truth (1 John 4:2).

'Dwelt' is *skēnoō* (G4637, John 1:14). The STEPBible lexicon defines it as 'to have one's tabernacle, to dwell', and notes that in the Greek Old Testament it chiefly renders the Hebrew *shakhan* (H7931), the verb of "that I may dwell among them" (Exodus 25:8). From the same Hebrew root comes *mishkan* (H4908), the tabernacle. So John is saying: the Word pitched his tent among us; he is the tabernacle. And what filled the tabernacle? "The glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle." (Exodus 40:34) Now John says, 'we beheld his glory'. (Some hear a sound-play between the Greek for tent and the later rabbinic word Shekinah for God's dwelling presence; that is a suggestion, not a certainty.) At the end of the Bible the same picture returns: "Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them" (Revelation 21:3).

'We beheld', *etheasametha* (G2300, John 1:14), is eyewitness language: "which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon, and our hands have handled, of the Word of life" (1 John 1:1). The glory is 'as of the only begotten of the Father', the glory that belongs to a father's one and only son. And he is 'full of grace and truth', which answers the name the LORD proclaimed to Moses: "merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6). The God who passed by Moses has come to live next door.

In John the glory is seen in the signs (John 2:11), but above all in the hour of the cross and resurrection, when the Son is glorified (John 12:23; John 17:1).

**Asks:** God did not stay far off and shout instructions; he came. Where the Word pitched his tent is where we meet God, so go to Jesus, not to a place or a feeling. And behold his glory: set aside time this week simply to look at him in the Gospels.

#### John 1:15–17 · Grace for Grace
**Says:** "John bare witness of him, and cried, saying, This was he of whom I spake, He that cometh after me is preferred before me: for he was before me." (John 1:15) "And of his fulness have all we received, and grace for grace." (John 1:16) "For the law was given by Moses, but grace and truth came by Jesus Christ." (John 1:17) *Bare* is the past tense of bear; *preferred before me* means ranked ahead of me.

**Means:** John's words are a riddle with three parts. Jesus comes after John in time and ministry; he ranks ahead of him; and the reason is that 'he was before me'. John was born about six months before Jesus (Luke 1:36), so 'before me' cannot mean earlier birth. It means that Jesus existed before John, which the Prologue has just said: the Word was in the beginning.

'Of his fulness have all we received': the Word is 'full of grace and truth' (John 1:14), and everything believers have is drawn from that fulness, *plērōmatos* (G4138, John 1:16). 'Grace for grace' is *charin anti charitos*, and the little word *anti* (G473, John 1:16) is the puzzle. It commonly means 'instead of, in place of', and the STEPBible lexicon lists John 1:16 under that sense. So some read 'grace in place of grace': the grace of the new covenant replacing the grace given through Moses, which verse 17 then explains with its 'For'. Others read 'grace upon grace', one gift following another without end, like waves on a shore. Both readings say that grace never runs out in Christ; the first ties verse 16 more closely to verse 17.

Verse 17 does not set law against grace as bad against good. The law was 'given', a gift of God through a servant. But grace and truth 'came' (*egeneto* again, came into being) by Jesus Christ: they were not merely spoken about, they arrived in a person. Here the name 'Jesus Christ' appears for the first time in the Gospel. Moses heard the LORD proclaimed abundant in goodness and truth (Exodus 34:6); in Jesus that goodness and truth took flesh.

**Asks:** Do not live on yesterday's leftovers. Christ's fulness does not run dry, so draw on it daily in prayer and in his word. And do not play the law against the gospel: the law led us to the one in whom grace and truth came.

#### John 1:18 · He Hath Declared Him
**Says:** "No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him." (John 1:18) *Bosom* is the place of closest affection; *declared* means made known, explained.

**Means:** 'No man hath seen God at any time': God told Moses, "there shall no man see me, and live" (Exodus 33:20). Yet Isaiah says, "I saw also the Lord sitting upon a throne" (Isaiah 6:1). John himself resolves it: "These things said Esaias, when he saw his glory, and spake of him." (John 12:41) The Lord Isaiah saw was the Son. The Father, in his own being, no one has seen; wherever God has been seen, it is the Son who has shown him.

'The only begotten Son' is the reading the KJV prints, and this study teaches it as printed (plan.md D3). Some early Greek manuscripts read 'only begotten God' here, and many modern editions follow them; Tier 3 explains why, calmly. Either reading, set beside John 1:1, points to the same person: the one and only Son, who is God.

He is 'in the bosom of the Father', and the Greek participle is present, *ōn* (G1510, John 1:18), 'who is' or 'who is being': even while he walks the roads of Galilee, the Son has never left the Father's side. At the Last Supper the beloved disciple will lean on Jesus' bosom (John 13:23); as the disciple is to Jesus, Jesus is to the Father. 'Declared' is *exēgēsato* (G1834, John 1:18), which the STEPBible lexicon defines first as leading or showing the way, and figuratively as unfolding, narrating, declaring. Our English word 'exegesis' comes from this Greek verb. Jesus is the unfolding of God. "He that hath seen me hath seen the Father" (John 14:9).

The Prologue ends where it began. It opened with the Word 'with God' (John 1:1) and closes with the Son 'in the bosom of the Father' (John 1:18): the whole passage is wrapped in the eternal fellowship of the Father and the Son.

**Asks:** If you want to know what God is like, look at Jesus. Do not build your picture of God from your moods, your fears, or your guesses; test every thought of God by the Son who declared him.

#### John 1:19–23 · The Voice in the Wilderness
**Says:** "And this is the record of John, when the Jews sent priests and Levites from Jerusalem to ask him, Who art thou?" (John 1:19) He "confessed, and denied not; but confessed, I am not the Christ." (John 1:20) To 'Art thou Elias?' he says, "I am not" (John 1:21), and to 'Art thou that prophet?' "No" (John 1:21). Pressed for an answer, "He said, I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness, Make straight the way of the Lord, as said the prophet Esaias." (John 1:23) *Record* means testimony; *Elias* is Elijah; *Esaias* is Isaiah.

**Means:** 'The Jews' here are the authorities in Jerusalem who could send priests and Levites, the temple's officials; the phrase does not mean the Jewish people as a whole (John the Baptist, Jesus, and every disciple in this chapter are Jews). This is an official inquiry into an unlicensed prophet drawing crowds.

John's answer is told with unusual emphasis: he confessed, did not deny, confessed. And what he confesses is a denial: 'I am not'. His answers get shorter each time, from 'I am not the Christ' to 'I am not' to 'No'. The man everyone is talking about refuses to talk about himself. The reader will soon hear the opposite from Jesus, whose great sayings begin 'I am' (John 8:12; John 8:58).

The three figures were the expected signs of the end. The Christ was the anointed king. Elias was promised: "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD" (Malachi 4:5), and many expected Elijah himself to return. 'That prophet' was the Prophet like Moses: "The LORD thy God will raise up unto thee a Prophet from the midst of thee, of thy brethren, like unto me" (Deuteronomy 18:15). Why John denies being Elias, when Jesus says he is, is taken up in the Council's Hard Questions.

At last John says what he is, in the words of Isaiah 40:3. Isaiah reads, "Prepare ye the way of the LORD" (Isaiah 40:3), where 'LORD' is the covenant name of God. John applies it to the one coming after him. If John is the voice preparing the way of the LORD, then the one who comes is the LORD. John is a voice; Jesus is the Word.

**Asks:** When people ask who you are, can you answer by pointing to Jesus? There is great freedom in being only a voice. You do not need to be important; you need to be clear about who is.

#### John 1:24–28 · Why Baptizest Thou Then
**Says:** "And they which were sent were of the Pharisees." (John 1:24) They ask, "Why baptizest thou then, if thou be not that Christ, nor Elias, neither that prophet?" (John 1:25) John answers, "I baptize with water: but there standeth one among you, whom ye know not" (John 1:26), one "whose shoe's latchet I am not worthy to unloose" (John 1:27). "These things were done in Bethabara beyond Jordan, where John was baptizing." (John 1:28) A *latchet* is the leather strap of a sandal.

**Means:** The Pharisees' question assumes that baptizing Israelites was an act that needed end-time authority. The prophets had promised a cleansing in the last days: "Then will I sprinkle clean water upon you, and ye shall be clean" (Ezekiel 36:25). Some Jews later immersed Gentile converts, and if that practice was known in John's day (scholars debate how early it began), John's baptism was striking: he treated Israelites as though they too needed to come in from outside.

John will not defend himself. He turns the question to someone else: 'there standeth one among you, whom ye know not'. The irony is heavy. The investigators have come a long way to examine the wrong man, while the one they must know stands unrecognized in the crowd, just as 'the world knew him not' (John 1:10). John's own place is lower than a slave's: untying a sandal strap was menial work. A saying preserved in later rabbinic writings held that a disciple should do for his teacher everything a slave does except untie his sandal (verify the source); if that reflects John's world, he claims to be unworthy of a task too low even for a disciple.

On the place: the KJV follows the Greek text that reads *Bēthabara* (G962, John 1:28); many modern editions read Bethany beyond Jordan, a village east of the river, not the Bethany near Jerusalem of John 11. Origen, in the third century, is said to have preferred Bethabara because he could not find a Bethany by the Jordan (verify). The site is still uncertain; the point is that John baptized on the far side of the Jordan, where Israel had once crossed into the land.

**Asks:** The one you most need may be standing, unrecognized, in your own life. Stop investigating the messengers, the preachers, the churches, the Christians who disappoint you, and look at him.

#### John 1:29–31 · Behold the Lamb of God
**Says:** "The next day John seeth Jesus coming unto him, and saith, Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world." (John 1:29) "This is he of whom I said, After me cometh a man which is preferred before me: for he was before me." (John 1:30) "And I knew him not: but that he should be made manifest to Israel, therefore am I come baptizing with water." (John 1:31) *Made manifest* means revealed.

**Means:** 'Behold' is *ide* (G2396, John 1:29): look! The word 'Lamb' occurs 2 times in John's Gospel, both in this chapter (John 1:29, 36). ('Feed my lambs' in John 21:15 is a different word, *arnia* (G721, John 21:15).) What lamb did John mean? The Old Testament offers several, and they converge on Jesus:
- the lamb God would provide: "God will provide himself a lamb for a burnt offering" (Genesis 22:8);
- the Passover lamb, whose blood sheltered Israel and whose bones were not to be broken (Exodus 12:46), a text John later sees fulfilled at the cross (John 19:36);
- the servant of the LORD, "brought as a lamb to the slaughter" (Isaiah 53:7), of whom Isaiah says, "the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:6);
- the lambs offered morning and evening on the altar, "two lambs of the first year day by day continually" (Exodus 29:38).

John the Baptist may not have understood everything his words meant; like Caiaphas later (John 11:49–52), he spoke better than he knew. The Gospel writer means all of it.

'Taketh away' is *airōn* (G142, John 1:29, V-PAP-NSM), a present participle of a verb meaning to take up and carry off. The STEPBible lexicon lists this verse under 'to bear or take away, carry off, remove', with 1 John 3:5. The picture is of a burden lifted and borne away, like the sins confessed over the goat sent into the wilderness (Leviticus 16:21). 'Sin' is singular: the whole mass of the world's guilt. And it is 'the sin of the world', not of Israel only. Christians differ on how 'world' bears on the extent of the atonement, some reading it of every person and others of people from every nation; all agree that John is announcing a Saviour for more than one people (John 4:42; 1 John 2:2).

Verse 31, 'I knew him not', is striking, since Luke tells us their mothers were kinswomen (Luke 1:36). John means he did not know Jesus as the Coming One until God showed him. His whole ministry of baptism had one aim: that Jesus 'should be made manifest to Israel'.

**Asks:** Look to the Lamb. Not to your record, not to your remorse, not to your efforts to make up for what you have done. Your sin is taken away by what he carried, and nothing you add will carry it further.

#### John 1:32–34 · The Spirit Abode upon Him
**Says:** "And John bare record, saying, I saw the Spirit descending from heaven like a dove, and it abode upon him." (John 1:32) God had told him, "Upon whom thou shalt see the Spirit descending, and remaining on him, the same is he which baptizeth with the Holy Ghost." (John 1:33) "And I saw, and bare record that this is the Son of God." (John 1:34) *Abode* means stayed, remained.

**Means:** John's Gospel does not narrate Jesus' baptism; it gives John's testimony about what he saw (the other Gospels tell the scene, as in Mark 1:10). The Spirit descending recalls the prophecies of the anointed one: "And the spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him" (Isaiah 11:2); "I have put my spirit upon him" (Isaiah 42:1). Some readers also recall the Spirit moving over the waters at creation (Genesis 1:2), fitting a chapter that began 'In the beginning'; that is a suggestion, not something John says.

The key word is 'abode'. In the Old Testament the Spirit came upon judges and prophets for a task and a time. On Jesus the Spirit stays: *emeinen* (G3306, John 1:32, V-AAI-3S), and in verse 33 'remaining', *menon* (G3306, John 1:33, V-PAP-ASN). "For God giveth not the Spirit by measure unto him." (John 3:34) This is John's great word *menō*, which the KJV renders 'abide', 'dwell', and 'remain'; it will return three times in the next scene and at the heart of John 15.

The one on whom the Spirit remains is the one who 'baptizeth with the Holy Ghost'. John's water pointed to a greater washing: the promised outpouring of the Spirit (Joel 2:28; Ezekiel 36:26), fulfilled when Jesus was glorified (Acts 1:5). Here the whole Trinity is in view: the Father who sent John and gave the sign, the Spirit who descends and remains, and the Son on whom he rests. John's testimony reaches its peak: 'this is the Son of God'. His verbs are perfects, *memarturēka* (G3140, John 1:34, V-RAI-1S), 'I have borne record', a testimony given that still stands.

**Asks:** The Spirit who rested on Jesus is the Spirit he gives. Do not seek the Spirit apart from Christ, as though he were a power to be had on its own; he is given by the Son and he glorifies the Son (John 16:14).

#### John 1:35–39 · What Seek Ye
**Says:** "Again the next day after John stood, and two of his disciples" (John 1:35); John says again, "Behold the Lamb of God!" (John 1:36), "And the two disciples heard him speak, and they followed Jesus." (John 1:37) Jesus turns and asks, "What seek ye?" (John 1:38) They answer, "Rabbi, (which is to say, being interpreted, Master,) where dwellest thou?" (John 1:38) "He saith unto them, Come and see. They came and saw where he dwelt, and abode with him that day: for it was about the tenth hour." (John 1:39) *Master* means teacher; *dwellest* means are you staying.

**Means:** John points, and he loses two disciples. He is glad to: "He must increase, but I must decrease." (John 3:30) 'Followed' means walking behind, and it is also the word of discipleship; the story works on both levels.

The first words Jesus speaks in this Gospel are a question: 'What seek ye?' It is a question for every reader, and John will bring it back at the arrest, "Whom seek ye?" (John 18:4), and at the empty tomb, "whom seekest thou?" (John 20:15). The disciples' answer is better than it sounds: 'where dwellest thou?' uses *meneis* (G3306, John 1:38, V-PAI-2S), the abiding word again. They do not want a lecture; they want to be where he is. He answers with an invitation: 'Come and see'. They 'abode with him that day', and the word appears a third time in two verses.

'About the tenth hour' is about four in the afternoon, counting from sunrise as Jews usually did. Some, B. F. Westcott among them (verify), have argued that John counts from midnight, which would make it ten in the morning; most commentators prefer the Jewish reckoning. Either way, someone remembered the hour, the kind of detail an eyewitness keeps. One of the two is Andrew; the other is not named. Many since the early church have identified him with the disciple whom Jesus loved, the author of the Gospel; that is tradition, not something the text states.

**Asks:** What are you seeking from Jesus: an answer, a blessing, a fix? He answers the question with himself. Come and see, and stay with him; faith grows by abiding, not by visiting.

#### John 1:40–42 · Andrew Finds Simon
**Says:** "One of the two which heard John speak, and followed him, was Andrew, Simon Peter's brother." (John 1:40) "He first findeth his own brother Simon, and saith unto him, We have found the Messias, which is, being interpreted, the Christ." (John 1:41) "And he brought him to Jesus." (John 1:42) Jesus looked at him and said, "Thou art Simon the son of Jona: thou shalt be called Cephas, which is by interpretation, A stone." (John 1:42) *Messias* is the Greek spelling of Messiah.

**Means:** The first thing Andrew does after abiding with Jesus is to find his own brother. 'Messias' is the Hebrew title, transliterated into Greek; 'Christ' is its Greek translation; both mean 'anointed'. The KJV uses 'Messias' 2 times in the whole Bible, both in John (John 1:41; John 4:25). John keeps translating for readers who do not know Hebrew or Aramaic (Rabbi, Messias, Cephas), a sign that he writes for the wider world.

Andrew is always bringing someone in John's Gospel: here his brother, later a boy with loaves (John 6:8–9), later Greeks who want to see Jesus (John 12:20–22). He is never at the center, and he is always useful.

Jesus 'beheld him', *emblepsas* (G1689, John 1:42): he looked at him intently. Then he renames him. In Scripture, naming shows authority, and God renamed Abram and Jacob to mark what he would do with them (Genesis 32:28). *Kēphas* (G2786, John 1:42) is the Aramaic for rock or stone, in Greek Petros, Peter. Jesus names Simon not for what he is yet but for what he will make of him. The denials of John 18 and the restoration of John 21 lie ahead; the name is a promise.

**Asks:** Whom do you need to find first? Often it is the people closest to us, and the hardest to talk to. And whose name for you will you believe: your past's, or Jesus'?

#### John 1:43–46 · Can Any Good Thing Come out of Nazareth
**Says:** "The day following Jesus would go forth into Galilee, and findeth Philip, and saith unto him, Follow me." (John 1:43) "Now Philip was of Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter." (John 1:44) Philip tells Nathanael, "We have found him, of whom Moses in the law, and the prophets, did write, Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph." (John 1:45) "And Nathanael said unto him, Can there any good thing come out of Nazareth? Philip saith unto him, Come and see." (John 1:46)

**Means:** This time Jesus himself does the finding. Andrew found Simon, and Philip will find Nathanael, but behind every finding is the Lord who first finds: 'Follow me'. Philip comes from Bethsaida, Andrew and Peter's town, so the chain of witness runs along lines of family and neighbourhood.

Philip's announcement is sweeping: 'him, of whom Moses in the law, and the prophets, did write'. The whole Old Testament points to Jesus. He himself will say, "For had ye believed Moses, ye would have believed me: for he wrote of me." (John 5:46) Philip's description, 'Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph', is true as far as it goes: Joseph was his legal father, and Nazareth his home. But the reader of the Prologue knows where he truly comes from (John 1:14, 18). Misunderstanding about where Jesus comes from will run through the Gospel: "Shall Christ come out of Galilee?" (John 7:41); "out of Galilee ariseth no prophet" (John 7:52).

Nathanael, who came from Cana (John 21:2), sneers at the village a few miles away. Nazareth was small and unremarkable; the Old Testament never mentions it. Philip does not argue. He repeats his Master's words: 'Come and see'. The disciple's invitation is the Lord's invitation, passed on.

**Asks:** When someone doubts, answer with an invitation. And examine your own prejudices: where have you decided, before looking, that nothing good could come from a place, a people, or a person?

#### John 1:47–49 · An Israelite Indeed
**Says:** "Jesus saw Nathanael coming to him, and saith of him, Behold an Israelite indeed, in whom is no guile!" (John 1:47) Nathanael asks, "Whence knowest thou me?" (John 1:48) Jesus answers, "Before that Philip called thee, when thou wast under the fig tree, I saw thee." (John 1:48) "Nathanael answered and saith unto him, Rabbi, thou art the Son of God; thou art the King of Israel." (John 1:49) *Guile* means deceit, cunning; *whence* means from where, how.

**Means:** 'Indeed' is *alēthōs* (G230, John 1:47), truly. 'An Israelite indeed, in whom is no guile' recalls the man first named Israel. Jacob had taken his brother's blessing by deceit: "Thy brother came with subtilty" (Genesis 27:35). The Greek Old Testament uses the same word there as John does here, *dolos* (G1388, John 1:47) (verify the Septuagint wording). Jacob, the deceiver, was renamed Israel (Genesis 32:28); Nathanael is an Israel without the Jacob in him, a man like the one the psalm calls blessed, "in whose spirit there is no guile" (Psalm 32:2). Jesus is not flattering him. Nathanael has just spoken bluntly and even rudely about Nazareth; but he is honest, and Jesus honours honesty.

'Whence knowest thou me?' Jesus had seen him under the fig tree before Philip called him. What Nathanael was doing there John does not say, and we should not invent it. The fig tree was a picture of peace and the messianic age, "they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree" (Micah 4:4), and later rabbinic writings speak of studying the law under a fig tree (verify). What matters is that Jesus knew him completely, as he will know Simon, the Samaritan woman, and all men (John 2:25; John 4:18).

Nathanael's confession is the chapter's climax of human testimony. 'Son of God' and 'King of Israel' belong together in Psalm 2, where God sets his king on Zion (Psalm 2:6) and says to him, "Thou art my Son; this day have I begotten thee" (Psalm 2:7). On Nathanael's lips it may mean 'the Messiah'. John's readers know from the Prologue that it means more. 'King of Israel' will return when the crowds hail him (John 12:13) and on the title over the cross (John 19:19).

**Asks:** Jesus knows you completely, the fig tree and all. You cannot surprise him and need not hide from him. Come to him without guile, honest even about your doubts.

#### John 1:50–51 · Heaven Open
**Says:** Jesus answers, "Because I said unto thee, I saw thee under the fig tree, believest thou? thou shalt see greater things than these." (John 1:50) "And he saith unto him, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Hereafter ye shall see heaven open, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of man." (John 1:51) *Verily* means truly; *hereafter* means from now on.

**Means:** Jesus gently weighs Nathanael's faith: you believe because of this? You will see greater things. The greater things begin at Cana (John 2:11) and end at the cross and the empty tomb.

'Verily, verily' is *amēn* (G281, John 1:51), doubled. The doubled form, which the KJV renders 'Verily, verily', occurs 25 times in John and nowhere else in the Bible. It marks a solemn word spoken on Jesus' own authority; the prophets said 'Thus saith the LORD', and Jesus says 'I say unto you'.

Here the KJV's pronouns show what modern English hides. In verse 50 Jesus speaks to Nathanael alone, and every pronoun is singular: "believest thou? thou shalt see greater things than these" (John 1:50). In verse 51 the pronoun turns plural: "I say unto you, Hereafter ye shall see heaven open" (John 1:51). The promise widens from one man to all the disciples, and to every reader.

The promise itself is Genesis 28. Jacob, fleeing after his deceit, dreamed of "a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it" (Genesis 28:12). He woke and said, "this is none other but the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven" (Genesis 28:17). Jesus puts himself where the ladder was. He is the meeting place of heaven and earth, the true Bethel (which means 'house of God'), the gate of heaven: the tabernacle of verse 14, the temple of John 2:21, and the way of John 14:6. Some note that the angels go up before they come down, as if the Son of man were already standing on the earth; that is a suggestion, not something the verse explains.

'Son of man' is Jesus' favourite way of speaking of himself. It recalls the heavenly figure of Daniel, "one like the Son of man came with the clouds of heaven" (Daniel 7:13), who receives an everlasting kingdom. In John, the Son of man is the one who came down from heaven (John 3:13) and will be lifted up (John 3:14). 'Hereafter' renders a phrase in the Greek text behind the KJV, 'from now on'; some modern editions do not have it. Either way, the open heaven begins with the ministry about to unfold.

**Asks:** Heaven is open in Jesus. You do not need a ladder of your own making, a better record, or a holier place; come to the Father through the Son (John 14:6). And expect greater things, not as spectacle but as more and more of his glory.

### 🧨 Cultural Landmines and Misconceptions
**'The Word' is not Greek philosophy in Christian dress.** Some readers are told that John borrowed the Logos from Heraclitus, the Stoics, or Philo. John certainly knew his readers would hear those echoes, and he may have chosen the word partly for them. But his sentences come from Genesis and the prophets: in the beginning, God speaking, light, the word of the LORD, the tabernacle, the glory. And the claim that the Word became flesh would have scandalized the philosophers, for whom matter was the lesser realm.

**The Lamb was not a pet.** Modern readers picture a lamb as soft and sweet. For John's hearers, a lamb 'of God' meant sacrifice: the Passover lamb killed and eaten, the morning and evening lambs burned on the altar, the servant led to the slaughter. 'Behold the Lamb of God' is an announcement of death.

**'The Jews' in John 1:19.** In John the phrase often means the religious authorities in Judea, not the Jewish people. Everyone in this chapter who meets Jesus is Jewish, and Nathanael is praised as 'an Israelite indeed'. Teach the chapter without any trace of contempt; that reading has fed terrible sins in the church's history.

**Two Johns.** New readers often assume the John of John 1:6 wrote the Gospel. He is John the Baptist. The Gospel's author never names himself; the church has received him as John the son of Zebedee, which is tradition, not a statement of the text.

**'Son of God' and 'Son of man' are not simply 'divine' and 'human'.** It is common to say that Son of God names Jesus' deity and Son of man his humanity. There is truth in it, but on Nathanael's lips 'Son of God' may first mean the Messiah-king of Psalm 2, and 'Son of man' comes from Daniel 7, where it names a heavenly figure who receives an everlasting kingdom. Both titles are grander than the shorthand.

**'Sons of God' and gender.** The KJV's 'sons of God' (John 1:12) translates a word for children of either sex. Women in the group are not left out of the promise; the lexicon's gloss is simply 'child'.

## 🔴 Tier 3 — Expert
### 🏗️ Theological Framework
John 1 is the doorway to the doctrines of God and of Christ, and it is best read through the Reformed conviction that Scripture interprets Scripture: the Prologue is read with Genesis 1, Exodus 33–40, Isaiah 40 and 53, and with the rest of John, whose later chapters it announces.

*The Trinity.* John 1:1 requires both distinction ('with God') and identity of nature ('was God'); John 1:18 names the distinction Father and Son; John 1:32–33 adds the Spirit, who descends from heaven and remains on the Son. The church's doctrine of one God in three persons is not imposed on this chapter; it is the only account that keeps all of its sentences true at once, alongside the Bible's unbending monotheism: "I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God." (Isaiah 44:6)

*The incarnation.* John 1:14 is the text on which the church's Christology rests. The Council of Chalcedon (451) confessed one Son, perfect in deity and perfect in humanity, in two natures that are neither mixed together nor divided, each keeping its own properties in the one person. John's grammar anticipates this: the same subject, the Word, 'was God' and 'was made flesh'. The Reformed tradition added a guard that John 1:18 supports: the Word, united to flesh, was not confined by it, but continued to be in the bosom of the Father and to uphold all things (Calvin, *Institutes* 2.13.4, verify; the later name for this is the *extra Calvinisticum*). The Westminster Confession (chapter 8) summarizes the same teaching in its own terms: the eternal Son took a true human nature, and the two natures were joined in one person, neither turned into each other nor blended together (verify the chapter; summarized, not quoted).

*Revelation.* The chapter distinguishes a light given to all (John 1:4–5, 9; compare Romans 1:20 and Acts 14:17) from the saving knowledge of God given in the Son (John 1:18) and received by those born of God (John 1:12–13). Reformed theology calls these general and special revelation, and insists that the first leaves people without excuse but does not by itself save.

*Regeneration and grace.* John 1:13 is a classic text for the Reformed teaching that the new birth is God's work, not the product of human descent, desire, or decision (the Westminster Confession, chapter 10, on effectual calling, verify). Faith and new birth arrive together in experience; in the order of causes, the Reformed read God's begetting as the root of faith (1 John 5:1).

*Covenant.* John 1:17 does not pit Moses against Christ. The Westminster Confession (chapter 7) teaches one covenant of grace, administered under the law by promises, prophecies, sacrifices, and types that foreshadowed Christ, and under the gospel by the word and sacraments, in greater fulness and clarity (verify). "grace and truth came by Jesus Christ" (John 1:17) is that fulness arriving.

*Atonement.* "the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world" (John 1:29) gathers the sacrificial system into one person and grounds the doctrine of Christ's substitutionary sacrifice (Isaiah 53:6; 1 Corinthians 5:7).

### 📚 Scholarly Insights
- **Athanasius**, *On the Incarnation*: argues that the Word through whom the world was made is the one who took a body to renew his fallen creation and to undo death from within, so that creation and redemption are the work of the same Word. His reading of John 1:3 and 1:14 as one story shaped the Nicene defence against Arius.
- **Augustine**, *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (the first tractates): reasons that the Word through whom all things were made cannot himself be among the things made, and uses the analogy of a word conceived in the mind before it is spoken to describe the Word's relation to the Father (verify the details). In a sermon on John the Baptist he sets John as the voice beside Christ as the Word: the voice sounds and passes, the word remains (verify the source).
- **John Chrysostom**, *Homilies on the Gospel of John*: dwells on John's use of 'was' for the Word, as against 'was made' for creation and for the Baptist, as a sign of the Word's eternity (verify).
- **John Calvin**, *Commentary on the Gospel According to John*: reads 'with God' against Sabellius and 'was God' against Arius, and on John 1:9 takes the light as a light of reason and conscience given to all people, distinct from the saving illumination of the Spirit (verify).
- **J. C. Ryle**, *Expository Thoughts on the Gospels* (John): draws practical lessons from the Baptist's humility and from the way the first disciples brought others to Christ, urging ordinary believers to do the same (verify the particular points).
- **B. F. Westcott**, *The Gospel According to St. John*: argues that John reckons hours from midnight, so that the tenth hour of John 1:39 is ten in the morning (verify); most later commentators have not followed him.
- **Leon Morris**, *The Gospel According to John* (New International Commentary on the New Testament): holds that John's *logos* speaks to both Jewish and Greek backgrounds but is filled with Old Testament content (verify).
- **D. A. Carson**, *The Gospel According to John* (Pillar New Testament Commentary, 1991): argues for 'grace instead of grace' at John 1:16, the grace of the new covenant replacing the grace of the law (verify).
- **Raymond E. Brown**, *The Gospel According to John* (Anchor Bible, 1966): regards the Prologue as an early Christian hymn adapted by the evangelist, with the verses about the Baptist added in prose (verify). Many conservative scholars accept that the Prologue has a poetic shape without accepting a separate source.
- **E. C. Colwell** (an article on the article with predicate nouns, *Journal of Biblical Literature*, 1933) and **Philip B. Harner** (*Journal of Biblical Literature*, 1973): on the grammar of 'the Word was God'; Harner argues that the article-less predicate placed first stresses the nature or quality of the Word, which is fully God, rather than making him 'a god' (verify).
- **Bruce M. Metzger**, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*: explains why the editors of the United Bible Societies' text chose 'only begotten God' at John 1:18 as the earlier and harder reading (verify). **Edward F. Hills**, *The King James Version Defended*: argues for 'only begotten Son' from the great majority of manuscripts and from John's own usage (verify).

### ⚔️ Debate Corner
**The question:** The true Light 'lighteth every man that cometh into the world' (John 1:9). What does the Light give to every person?

**Position 1 · An inner light in everyone, sufficient if obeyed.** The early Quakers, systematized by Robert Barclay in *An Apology for the True Christian Divinity* (1676, verify), taught that Christ gives to every person a measure of his inward light, a saving seed which, if not resisted, will lead to salvation even without the outward knowledge of Scripture. They read John 1:9 at face value: every man is lit. Its strength is that it takes 'every man' seriously and honours God's goodness to all.

**Position 2 · A universal prevenient grace.** Wesleyan and many Arminian Christians hold that God gives every person a grace that goes before conversion, restoring enough light and freedom to respond to the gospel, which each may accept or refuse. John Wesley taught this in his sermons (verify the sermon), and read John 1:9 as one of its texts. Its strength is that it explains how fallen people can be held responsible for refusing the light, and it guards against the idea that God is stingy with light.

**Position 3 · The Light shines on all, but saving sight is the new birth.** Reformed interpreters (Calvin among them, verify) read the verse in one of two ways that often combine. Either the Light gives every person the light of reason, conscience, and the witness of creation (Romans 1:20), which leaves all without excuse but does not save; or 'every man' means that the Light, now coming into the world, shines on all people without distinction, Jew and Gentile alike, and not on Israel only. Saving illumination is a further gift, given to those 'born... of God' (John 1:13).

**Most consistent with Reformed hermeneutics.** Scripture interprets Scripture, and the nearest interpreter of verse 9 is its own paragraph. Verse 5 says the darkness 'comprehended it not'; verse 10 says 'the world knew him not'; verse 11 says 'his own received him not'. Whatever 'lighteth every man' means, it cannot mean that every person has saving light, or that the light, once given, is being received; the next three sentences deny it. Verses 12–13 then locate the difference between those who receive and those who do not in a birth 'of God', not 'of the will of man'. Paul describes the same act: God "hath shined in our hearts" (2 Corinthians 4:6), while "the god of this world hath blinded the minds of them which believe not" (2 Corinthians 4:4). Position 3 keeps every clause of the passage true, and it is the view this study holds. It should be held with humility: Position 2 is held by many faithful Christians, and all three views agree that the Light is Christ alone and that every good gift comes from him.

**Pastoral Warning:** This verse can do real harm when it is misused in either direction. Taken as Position 1 is sometimes popularized, it turns people inward to 'follow your inner light' and away from Christ, the Scriptures, and the church; every sincere impulse becomes the voice of God. Taken coldly in the Reformed direction, it can make a church careless about outreach, as if God's sovereignty made witness optional. John 1 answers both: the Light shines on every person, so we speak to every person, and only God gives sight, so we speak with prayer and patience rather than pressure. Do not let a small group divide over the verse; let it send them out.

### 🔤 Original Language Deep Dive
Every definition below comes from one source: the brief Greek and Hebrew lexicon published by STEPBible.org (Tyndale House, Cambridge), whose Greek entries are drawn largely from G. Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament*. It is called 'the STEPBible lexicon' below.

**'Was' and 'came to be'.** The Prologue sets two verbs against each other. Of the Word: *ēn* (G1510, John 1:1, V-IAI-3S), the imperfect of 'to be', three times in verse 1 and again in verse 2, 4, 9, and 10. Of creation: *egeneto* (G1096, John 1:3, V-2ADI-3S), 'came into being'; and of John: *egeneto* (G1096, John 1:6, V-2ADI-3S), 'There was a man', literally 'there came to be a man'. Then, at the hinge, the Word himself takes the second verb: *egeneto* (G1096, John 1:14, V-2ADI-3S), 'was made flesh'. The eternal one who always 'was' 'became'. The grammar tells the gospel: the Creator entered the created order without ceasing to be the Creator.

**'The Word was God'.** In verse 1c the word for God has no article and stands before the verb: *theos* (G2316, John 1:1, N-NSM-T). In verse 1b, 'with God', it has the article: *theon* (G2316, John 1:1, N-ASM-T). The Jehovah's Witnesses' New World Translation reads 'a god', arguing that a noun without the article is indefinite. But Greek has no indefinite article, and a predicate noun placed before the verb commonly lacks the article whether or not it is definite (Colwell's observation, verify); Harner argued that the construction stresses quality or nature: what God is, the Word is (verify). The balance is exact. Had John written the article in 1c, he would have said that the Word and the Father are the same person, the error of Sabellius; had he written 'divine' (Greek has an adjective for that), he would have said less than he means. The article-less *theos* says that the Word shares fully in the nature of God while remaining distinct from the Father with whom he is. John's monotheism (John 5:44; John 17:3) rules out 'a god' beside God.

**'Comprehended'.** *Katelaben* (G2638, John 1:5, V-2AAI-3S) is the second aorist of a compound verb. The STEPBible lexicon gives three senses: to lay hold of or seize; to overtake, listing 'of evils' John 1:5 and John 12:35; and, of the mind, to apprehend or comprehend (in the New Testament in the middle voice). The form in John 1:5 is active, which leans toward 'overtake' or 'overcome', the sense the lexicon assigns. The KJV's 'comprehended' chose understanding. John is fond of words that work on two levels, and both are true of the darkness: it did not understand the light, and it did not overpower it.

**'Dwelt'.** *Eskēnōsen* (G4637, John 1:14, V-AAI-3S) is the aorist of *skēnoō*, which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'to have one's tabernacle, to dwell', noting that in the Greek Old Testament it chiefly renders the Hebrew *shakhan* (H7931). The lexicon lists the New Testament uses as John 1:14 and four in Revelation, including the dwelling of God with men in the new creation (Revelation 21:3). The Hebrew noun *mishkan* (H4908), 'dwelling place, tabernacle', comes from the same Hebrew verb. Some add that the Greek tent word shares its consonants with the later rabbinic term Shekinah; the likeness is real in sound, but whether John intended it cannot be proved.

**'Only begotten'.** *Monogenēs* (G3439, John 1:18, A-NSM), and in verse 14 *monogenous* (G3439, John 1:14, A-GSM). The STEPBible lexicon defines it as 'only, only begotten', used of only sons and daughters (Luke 7:12; Luke 8:42; Luke 9:38) and of Christ (John 3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9). Scholars debate whether its second half comes from the word for kind (so 'unique, one of a kind') or from the word for begetting (so 'only begotten'). Hebrews 11:17 calls Isaac Abraham's *monogenē* (G3439, Hebrews 11:17, A-ASM), though Abraham had another son, which shows that the word stresses uniqueness, the one son of promise. The KJV's 'only begotten' is well supported, and John 1:14 joins the word to 'of the Father', a father's one and only son. The church's teaching that the Son is eternally begotten of the Father does not rest on this one word, but on the whole Father-Son relation John describes; the word fits that teaching without having to prove it alone.

**The reading of John 1:18.** The Greek text behind the KJV reads *monogenēs* (G3439, John 1:18) *huios* (G5207, John 1:18), 'the only begotten Son'. Several of the oldest surviving manuscripts, including two early papyri and two great fourth-century codices, read 'only begotten God' instead (the manuscript details are standard in the textual commentaries; verify the list), and many modern editions follow them. Those who prefer 'God' argue that it is the older and harder reading, since a scribe would more likely change an unusual phrase to the familiar 'only begotten Son' of John 3:16, 18 than the reverse. Those who keep 'Son' point out that it is the reading of the great majority of manuscripts, is found in many early translations and church writers, and matches John's own usage in John 3:16, 3:18, and 1 John 4:9. (The STEPBible lexicon, following Abbott-Smith, lists John 1:18 under the phrase with 'God', because Abbott-Smith worked from a critical Greek text; verify.) This study teaches the KJV as printed. Nothing in the chapter's teaching hangs on the difference: the Son who is in the bosom of the Father is the Word who 'was God' (John 1:1), whichever noun stands in verse 18.

**'Declared'.** *Exēgēsato* (G1834, John 1:18, V-ADI-3S), aorist middle deponent. The STEPBible lexicon defines the verb as 'to lead, show the way; metaphorically, to unfold, narrate, declare', and lists John 1:18 with God as the understood object. English 'exegesis' is borrowed from the related Greek noun. The claim is not that the verb 'literally means' exegesis; it is that John chose a verb of telling and unfolding, and the Son is the one who tells God out.

**'Grace for grace'.** *Charin anti charitos*: the preposition *anti* (G473, John 1:16) takes the genitive. The STEPBible lexicon's main New Testament sense is 'instead of, in place of, for', and John 1:16 is listed under it, directly after a use 'of succession' (Matthew 2:22, Archelaus reigning in place of Herod). The lexicon does not decide between the two readings. 'Grace in place of grace' (Carson and others, verify) takes the replacement sense and reads verse 17 as its explanation: new covenant grace replacing the grace given through Moses. 'Grace upon grace' takes the succession sense: one gift following another, each replacing the last as it is received. Both stand within the lexicon's range.

**'Taketh away'.** *Airōn* (G142, John 1:29, V-PAP-NSM), a present active participle: the Lamb is characterized as the one who takes away. The STEPBible lexicon's third sense is 'to bear or take away, carry off, remove', and it cites John 1:29 and 1 John 3:5 for the taking away of sin by Christ. The first sense, to lift or take up, lies close by: he takes up the sin in order to take it away.

**'Abide'.** *Menō* appears in this chapter as *emeinen* (G3306, John 1:32, V-AAI-3S), of the Spirit remaining on Jesus; *menon* (G3306, John 1:33, V-PAP-ASN), again of the Spirit; *meneis* (G3306, John 1:38, V-PAI-2S), 'where dwellest thou?'; *menei* (G3306, John 1:39, V-PAI-3S), 'where he dwelt'; and *emeinan* (G3306, John 1:39, V-AAI-3P), 'abode with him'. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 1:32–33 among its uses of the Holy Spirit abiding, beside John 14:17. The KJV's English words (abode, remaining, dwellest, dwelt) hide a single thread that runs from the Spirit abiding on the Son to the disciples abiding with him, and on to John 15.

### 🚷 Preaching Mistakes to Avoid
- **Information without life:** a lecture on Logos in Heraclitus and Philo, Colwell's rule, and the manuscripts of John 1:18 that never brings the hearer to receive the Word made flesh and believe on his name.
- **Heat without light:** using John 1:1 as a club against Jehovah's Witnesses or other groups, with scorn instead of patience. The Baptist's weapon was witness, and Philip's was an invitation.
- **Overclaiming the Greek:** 'the Greek literally means face to face', 'monogenēs proves eternal generation', 'exegesis is the literal meaning of declared'. Say what the lexicon supports and no more.
- **Evangelism as technique:** turning 'Come and see' and Andrew's example into a method for growing a church. The finding in John 1 begins with Jesus finding (John 1:43); the disciples only pass on his invitation.
- **The preacher as the light:** a sermon on John the Baptist that ends up being about the preacher's own ministry. John's message about himself was 'I am not'.
- **Skipping the second half:** preaching the Prologue and never reaching the Lamb of God. John 1:1–18 tells who he is; John 1:29 tells why he came.

## 🟣 Tier 4 — Christocentric, Humility, Life & Light
### 👑 Every Answer in Jesus
John 1 leaves no room for self-glory. The world he made did not know him. His own people did not receive him. The religious authorities investigated the wrong man. The greatest prophet of the age could say only 'I am not' and 'I am the voice'. The first disciples did not know what they were seeking, and one of them began with a sneer. No one in the chapter rises to God; the Word comes down, the Lamb is provided, the Spirit descends, Jesus turns and speaks first, Jesus finds Philip, Jesus sees Nathanael before he is called. Even new birth is 'nor of the will of man, but of God'.

And every good thing the chapter holds is found in him. Life is in him; light is his; glory, grace, and truth are his fulness, from which we receive. The knowledge of God is his to give, for no one has seen God and he has declared him. The removal of sin is his work as the Lamb. Access to heaven is through him, the ladder on which the angels ascend and descend. This is what Paul means when he says of Christ, "In whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." (Colossians 2:3) John 1 humbles us by showing that we bring nothing but our darkness, and it makes Christ supreme by showing that he is the beginning, the middle, and the end of the story: the Word in the beginning, the Lamb in the middle, and the Son of man in whom heaven is opened.

### 🙇 Humility Practices
- Before you lead this study, read John 1:19–27 slowly and pray the publican's prayer honestly, "God be merciful to me a sinner" (Luke 18:13), asking the Lord to keep you from making the evening about yourself.
- In the group, when you are tempted to show what you know about Greek or history, ask a question instead and let a participant find the answer in the text, choosing to decrease so that Christ increases (John 3:30).
- Tomorrow, write a short note of thanks to the person who first brought you to Jesus, or who taught you the faith, and credit the Lord who used them, for "A man can receive nothing, except it be given him from heaven" (John 3:27).

- **Outward:** Identify one person in the group or church who seems isolated or rejected, a newcomer, a widower, a single parent (__________), and on Sunday sit with them, or on one evening this week call them or share a meal, as the Word came and 'dwelt among us' (John 1:14).
- **Outward:** Today, ask one young person or new believer you know (__________) to set one evening to read John 1 with you, and that evening tell them plainly how you came to believe, as Andrew told his brother (John 1:41).

## 📚 Doctrine
### Theology Proper · The Triune God, Known in the Son
**Proposition:** The one living God exists eternally as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The Son is personally distinct from the Father and shares his deity fully, and the invisible God is known truly and savingly only as the Son declares him.

**Textual Anchor:** "the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1); "No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him." (John 1:18) The Spirit is seen "descending from heaven like a dove" (John 1:32) on the Son at the Father's appointed sign (John 1:33).

**Cross-References:** "I and my Father are one." (John 10:30); "he that hath seen me hath seen the Father" (John 14:9); "whom no man hath seen, nor can see" (1 Timothy 6:16)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Oneness or modalism.* Its holders, among them Oneness Pentecostals today and Sabellius in the early church, say that Father, Son, and Spirit are three names or modes of one person: Jesus is the Father manifest in flesh. John 1 answers that the Word was 'with God' and is 'in the bosom of the Father'; one is not with oneself, and the Father sends while the Son is sent. (2) *God cannot be known, so every religion is as good as another.* Its holders say that since no one has seen God, all paths are human guesses. John agrees that no one has seen God, and then says the Son has declared him. The question is not whether God can be known but whether we will hear the one who has made him known.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Worship directed to the Father, through the Son, by the Spirit. Confidence in prayer: we do not guess at God's character; we look at Jesus. And humility before a mystery we can confess but not master.

### Christology · The Eternal Deity of the Word
**Proposition:** The Word existed before all creation, eternally with the Father, and is himself fully God. He is the Creator through whom everything that was made came to be, and so he is not himself a creature.

**Textual Anchor:** "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1) "All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made." (John 1:3) The Baptist, born before Jesus, says, "for he was before me" (John 1:15).

**Cross-References:** "Before Abraham was, I am." (John 8:58); "And he is before all things, and by him all things consist." (Colossians 1:17); "who is over all, God blessed for ever. Amen." (Romans 9:5)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The Son is the first and highest creature.* Arius in the fourth century, and the Jehovah's Witnesses today, hold that God created the Son first and made everything else through him; hence their rendering of John 1:1, with *a god* at its end. But John 1:3 puts every made thing on one side and the Word on the other, the grammar of 1:1c ascribes to the Word the nature of God, and the Bible allows no second, lesser god beside the LORD (Isaiah 44:6). (2) *Adoptionism.* Some in the early church held that Jesus was a man adopted as God's Son at his baptism. John 1:32–34 is the baptism, and it reveals rather than creates: the one on whom the Spirit descends was 'before' John and 'in the beginning with God' (John 1:2, 30).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Worship of Jesus without reserve, as Thomas worshipped him (John 20:28). Trust in his words as God's own words. And a humbling sense that the one who washes feet and calls fishermen is the Maker of the sea.

### Christology · The Word Made Flesh
**Proposition:** The eternal Son took to himself a true and complete human nature, body and soul, and became one person who is truly God and truly man. He did this without ceasing to be God, in order to make the Father known and to redeem his people.

**Textual Anchor:** "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). He is a real man in a real town, "Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph" (John 1:45), and yet the glory seen in him is "the glory as of the only begotten of the Father" (John 1:14).

**Cross-References:** "God sent forth his Son, made of a woman, made under the law" (Galatians 4:4); "Every spirit that confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God" (1 John 4:2); "Forasmuch then as the children are partakers of flesh and blood, he also himself likewise took part of the same" (Hebrews 2:14)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Docetism.* Some early teachers held that matter is unworthy of God, so the Christ only seemed to have a body. John says the Word became flesh, and his first letter makes the confession of Christ come in the flesh the test of the Spirit (1 John 4:2). (2) *Kenotic Christology in its strong form.* Some nineteenth-century theologians (Gottfried Thomasius among them, verify) taught that the Son laid aside attributes such as omniscience and omnipresence in order to become man, pointing to Philippians 2:7. The text there says he emptied himself by taking the form of a servant, not by ceasing to be God, and John 1 shows the incarnate Son still 'in the bosom of the Father' (John 1:18) and seeing Nathanael where no man could (John 1:48). (3) *Two persons.* The view associated with Nestorius was taken to divide Christ into a divine person and a human person joined in will. John's single subject forbids it: the same Word 'was God' and 'was made flesh'.

**Pastoral Fruit:** A Saviour who knows weakness from the inside and can be "touched with the feeling of our infirmities" (Hebrews 4:15). A new dignity for bodily life. And the wonder that God is found not by climbing up but in one who came down.

### Pneumatology · The Spirit Who Remains on the Son and Is Given by Him
**Proposition:** The Holy Spirit came upon the incarnate Son and remained on him without measure, marking him as the promised Messiah, and the Son is the one who baptizes his people with the Holy Ghost.

**Textual Anchor:** "I saw the Spirit descending from heaven like a dove, and it abode upon him." (John 1:32) "Upon whom thou shalt see the Spirit descending, and remaining on him, the same is he which baptizeth with the Holy Ghost." (John 1:33)

**Cross-References:** "And the spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him, the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and of the fear of the LORD" (Isaiah 11:2); "for God giveth not the Spirit by measure unto him" (John 3:34); "For by one Spirit are we all baptized into one body" (1 Corinthians 12:13)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Spirit baptism as a second blessing for some believers.* Classical Pentecostal teaching holds that baptism in the Spirit is a distinct experience after conversion, often evidenced by tongues, which believers should seek. Its holders rightly insist on the Spirit's power. But John 1:33 names Jesus as the baptizer, and Paul says that by one Spirit all believers are baptized into one body (1 Corinthians 12:13); the Reformed hold that every believer is baptized with the Spirit in union with Christ, while believers may be filled afresh many times. (2) *The Spirit sought apart from Christ.* Some forms of spirituality treat the Spirit as a force to be accessed by technique. John 1 shows the Spirit resting on the Son and given by the Son, who glorifies the Son (John 16:14).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Assurance for the ordinary believer: if you belong to Christ, you have his Spirit. Dependence: we look to Jesus, not to methods, for the Spirit's help. And hope: the Spirit who remained on Jesus remains with his people (John 14:16–17).

### Anthropology & Sin · A World in Darkness That Knew Not Its Maker
**Proposition:** Mankind, made by the Word and given light by him, has fallen into darkness, so that the world does not know or receive its Maker and cannot of itself produce spiritual life. This blindness reaches the religious as well as the irreligious.

**Textual Anchor:** "the darkness comprehended it not" (John 1:5); "the world was made by him, and the world knew him not" (John 1:10); "his own received him not" (John 1:11); and to the delegation from Jerusalem, "there standeth one among you, whom ye know not" (John 1:26).

**Cross-References:** "And this is the condemnation, that light is come into the world, and men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil." (John 3:19); "And you hath he quickened, who were dead in trespasses and sins" (Ephesians 2:1); "In whom the god of this world hath blinded the minds of them which believe not" (2 Corinthians 4:4)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Human nature is basically sound and needs only information or example.* In the tradition of Pelagius, many today hold that people fail to know God only because they lack good teaching and good models. But the light was already shining, and the Maker was already in the world, and still the world did not know him. The problem is not a shortage of light but a darkness that does not receive it. (2) *Religion is the cure.* The priests, Levites, and Pharisees of John 1:19–26 had Scripture, temple, and law, and still did not know the one standing among them. Religious privilege does not give sight.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Humility: I was in the same darkness. Compassion rather than contempt for unbelievers, since only grace made the difference. And prayer for friends and family, because argument alone cannot give sight.

### Soteriology & Grace · Born of God: Children by Grace
**Proposition:** All who receive Christ by believing on his name are given the right to be God's children, and their new birth is the work of God, not the product of human descent, human desire, or human decision.

**Textual Anchor:** "But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name" (John 1:12); "Which were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God." (John 1:13); "And of his fulness have all we received, and grace for grace." (John 1:16)

**Cross-References:** "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God" (Ephesians 2:8); "Of his own will begat he us with the word of truth" (James 1:18); "Being born again, not of corruptible seed, but of incorruptible, by the word of God, which liveth and abideth for ever." (1 Peter 1:23)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Salvation by descent.* In Jesus' day some trusted that being Abraham's children was enough (Matthew 3:9); today some trust that a Christian family, a baptism, or a church membership makes them God's children. (Paedobaptist Reformed churches, which baptize infants, do not teach this; they call baptized children to personal faith.) John 1:13 says: not of blood. (2) *Decisional regeneration.* Some evangelistic practice treats the new birth as produced by a person's decision or a prayer said on cue: 'pray this prayer and you are born again'. Arminian Christians, stated fairly, hold that faith precedes regeneration but do not claim that the will itself produces the birth. The Reformed answer from verse 13 and 1 John 5:1 that God's begetting lies at the root of faith; the call to believe is real and urgent, and the birth is God's gift.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Assurance resting on God's act, not on the strength of our decision. Humility: we are children by gift, not by merit. And boldness in witness: since the new birth is God's work, no one is too far gone to be born of God.

### Soteriology & Grace · The Lamb of God Who Takes Away Sin
**Proposition:** God has provided his Son as the sacrificial Lamb who bears and removes sin by his death in the place of sinners, fulfilling the Passover and the sacrifices of the law, for people of every nation.

**Textual Anchor:** "Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world." (John 1:29) And again, as two disciples listen and follow: "Behold the Lamb of God!" (John 1:36)

**Cross-References:** "For even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us" (1 Corinthians 5:7); "But with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot" (1 Peter 1:19); "For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him." (2 Corinthians 5:21)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The cross as example only.* The moral influence view, often traced to Peter Abelard (verify) and common in liberal theology, holds that Christ's death saves by displaying God's love and moving us to repent. The cross does display love (Romans 5:8), but a lamb 'of God' in Israel's Scriptures is a sacrifice, and the verb is 'taketh away': something is removed, not merely demonstrated. (2) *Universalism.* Some read 'the sin of the world' as meaning that every person is already forgiven, whether they believe or not. The same chapter distinguishes those who receive him from those who do not (John 1:11–12), and John later says, "he that believeth not is condemned already" (John 3:18). The Lamb is offered to the whole world; his benefits are received by faith.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Freedom from the burden of guilt: sin is taken away, not managed. Worship: "Worthy is the Lamb that was slain" (Revelation 5:12). And evangelism without distinction: the Lamb is for the sin of the world, so no one is outside the invitation.

### Christian Walk · Follow Him, Abide with Him, Bring Others
**Proposition:** Discipleship begins when Jesus calls and we follow him, grows as we abide with him, and overflows as we bring others to come and see, always pointing away from ourselves to him.

**Textual Anchor:** "and they followed Jesus" (John 1:37); "Come and see. They came and saw where he dwelt, and abode with him that day" (John 1:39); "He first findeth his own brother Simon" (John 1:41); "Follow me" (John 1:43); "Philip saith unto him, Come and see." (John 1:46)

**Cross-References:** "Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men." (Matthew 4:19); "He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing." (John 15:5); "He must increase, but I must decrease." (John 3:30)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Faith as a private matter.* Many today hold that religion is personal and should not be shared. The first thing the first disciples did was tell their brothers and friends. (2) *Evangelism as salesmanship.* Some methods treat witness as persuasion by technique and pressure. Philip neither argued nor pressed; he invited, and left Nathanael to meet Jesus. (3) *Activity without abiding.* It is possible to bring others without staying with Jesus oneself. The disciples 'abode with him that day' before they went out.

**Pastoral Fruit:** A simple, doable pattern for every believer: follow, stay, bring. Freedom from the pressure to be persuasive. And joy, as those we bring meet the one who already saw them under the fig tree.

### Assumed Here
- **Ecclesiology:** The chapter gathers the first disciples around Jesus and gives Simon the name Cephas, but it does not teach the church's order or ordinances; the church built on Christ is taught plainly in Matthew 16:18, Ephesians 2:19–22, and 1 Peter 2:4–5.
- **Eschatology:** 'Hereafter ye shall see heaven open' (John 1:51) and the title Son of man from Daniel 7:13 point toward the kingdom that is coming, and the tabernacle of John 1:14 toward the day when God dwells with men (Revelation 21:3); the last things are taught plainly in John 5:25–29 and Revelation 21–22.

## ⚖️ The Council's Hard Questions
### ❓ Question 1 · How Can the Word Be With God and Be God
**Moderator:** John 1:1 says the Word 'was with God' and 'was God'. The Jehovah's Witness at the door says that is a contradiction, so the second must mean 'a god'. A Oneness believer says the Word simply is the Father. How do we answer both?

**Philologist:** The grammar holds both halves. In 'with God' the word for God has the article; it points to the Father as a person. In 'was God' it has no article and comes first for emphasis. If John had put the article there, he would have said the Word and the Father are simply the same person. If he had wanted to say 'divine' in a lesser sense, Greek had an adjective for it. He chose the construction that says: what God is, the Word is, and yet the Word is with him.

**Church Historian:** The church fought over exactly these two errors. Sabellius in the third century collapsed the persons; Arius in the fourth made the Son a creature. The Council of Nicaea answered Arius by confessing the Son of one being with the Father, and it drew that language from John 1. The Witness position today is Arius's, and the Oneness position is close to Sabellius's.

**Theologian:** And both errors come from holding one sentence of Scripture and letting go of another. The Bible insists there is one God, "I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God" (Isaiah 44:6). It also presents the Father and the Son as two who love, send, and speak to one another, and it calls the Son God (John 1:1, 18; John 20:28). The doctrine of the Trinity is simply the refusal to let go of any of these.

**Teacher:** For our group, I would keep it to what John says and not reach for analogies like water, ice, and steam, which actually teach modalism. Three sentences are enough: there is one God; the Word is with God; the Word is God. We believe all three because John says all three.

**Resolution:** John 1:1 teaches that the Word is personally distinct from the Father ('with God') and shares fully in the one divine nature ('was God'). 'A god' is ruled out by John's monotheism and by John 1:3, which puts the Word outside the class of made things; identifying the Word with the Father is ruled out by 'with God' and 'in the bosom of the Father' (John 1:18). The doctrine of the Trinity is the faithful summary of both halves.

### ❓ Question 2 · No Man Hath Seen God, and the Reading of John 1:18
**Moderator:** John 1:18 says no man has seen God at any time. But Jacob said he saw God face to face (Genesis 32:30), Moses saw his back (Exodus 33:23), and Isaiah saw the Lord on a throne. And then many modern Bibles read this verse differently from the KJV. What are we to say?

**Literary Scholar:** Notice where the verse stands. It closes the Prologue by answering its opening: the Word 'with God' becomes the Son 'in the bosom of the Father'. And John himself interprets the Old Testament visions later: when Isaiah saw the Lord's glory, John says, "These things said Esaias, when he saw his glory, and spake of him." (John 12:41) The one Isaiah saw was the Son.

**Theologian:** So the two statements fit. God in his own being, the Father, "whom no man hath seen, nor can see" (1 Timothy 6:16), has never been seen. Whenever God has shown himself, in the angel of the LORD, in the glory over the tabernacle, in Isaiah's vision, it was through the one who would become flesh. The incarnation is the fullest of those showings, not the first.

**Philologist:** On the reading: the Greek behind the KJV reads 'only begotten Son'. Several very old manuscripts read 'only begotten God', and many modern editions follow them, arguing that the older and harder reading is more likely original. Defenders of the KJV's reading answer that 'Son' is in the great majority of manuscripts and matches John's own phrase in John 3:16 and 3:18. Both sides are arguing about evidence, not about the deity of Christ.

**Historian:** It is worth knowing that church writers in the second and third centuries quote the verse both ways (verify the particular writers), so both readings are old. Neither was invented by a modern party.

**Pastor:** Then let us not make a new believer feel that their Bible is unstable. We teach the KJV's text as printed. If someone asks, we explain calmly: both readings point to the same person, the only begotten, who is in the Father's bosom and is God (John 1:1). And we go back to the verse's own point: if you want to know God, look at Jesus.

**Resolution:** 'No man hath seen God' refers to God in his own being; the Old Testament appearances of God were appearances of the Son, as John 12:41 teaches. On the text, the KJV reads 'the only begotten Son', supported by most manuscripts and John's usage; many modern editions read 'only begotten God' from several early manuscripts. This study teaches the KJV as printed and notes the difference without scorn; both readings, with John 1:1, confess the Son's deity.

### ❓ Question 3 · Was John the Baptist Elias or Not
**Moderator:** In John 1:21 the Baptist says plainly that he is not Elias. But Jesus says, "And if ye will receive it, this is Elias, which was for to come." (Matthew 11:14) Is there a contradiction?

**Historian:** The question the priests asked assumed a particular expectation. Malachi had promised, "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet" (Malachi 4:5), and since Elijah had been taken up alive, many expected the man himself to return. When they ask 'Art thou Elias?', they mean: are you Elijah come back?

**Theologian:** And to that question the honest answer is no. John is not Elijah returned from heaven. But the angel told Zacharias that John would go before the Lord "in the spirit and power of Elias" (Luke 1:17). Jesus speaks of the role: John fulfills what Malachi promised. Note his condition, 'if ye will receive it', which marks it as a matter of understanding the prophecy rightly.

**Literary Scholar:** There is also a reason the Gospel records John's denials so starkly. In this chapter every title John refuses is a title the reader will see belongs to someone else, or points to someone else. John will not be the Christ, will not take the Prophet's place, and will not even claim the honour of being Elijah. He is a voice.

**Pastor:** That is the lesson for us. John could have claimed a great title with some justification, and he declined it to keep the light on Jesus. Many of us grasp at titles and recognition that are not even partly ours.

**Teacher:** For the group, a simple chart helps: what the priests meant by Elijah (the man himself returned), what Malachi meant (a prophet like Elijah before the day of the LORD), and what Jesus said (John fulfilled Malachi). Then ask why John answered so briefly.

**Resolution:** There is no contradiction. John denied being Elijah in person, which is what his questioners meant; Jesus affirmed that John fulfilled Malachi's promise of Elijah's coming, as the angel had said he would go "in the spirit and power of Elias" (Luke 1:17). John's refusal to claim the title is itself part of his witness to Christ.

### 🔦 The Life & Light Audit
**Pastor:** If a hearer obeyed this lesson exactly, they would receive Christ again by name, thank the Father that their place in his family is a gift, and go to one family member they had written off, a brother, a sister, a parent, with a sentence like Andrew's. The dark place lit would be their picture of God, tested against the Son who declared him, and a prejudice like Nathanael's, confessed.

**Theologian:** The practices keep source discipline. None asks the hearer to produce life or to be the light. The life practices bring people to Jesus and let him speak; the light practices begin with the hearer's own heart and with the Lamb who takes sin away, and the outward ones are invitations, not confrontations, shaped by the Love Guard. The Baptist's 'He was not that Light' governs them all.

**Teacher:** A Level 1 participant can do the Inward Life practice tonight and the Outward practice of inviting a friend to read John 1:1–18. The Outward practices name the kind of person to reach and leave a blank for the name. The one risk is that 'invite a skeptic' feels frightening to a new believer; the Line to Say gives them words that do not require answers they do not have.

**Fix:** Two changes were needed, and both are in the unit. The Outward Life practices now name a kind of person (a family member you assumed would never be interested; a curious or skeptical friend or coworker) rather than a general 'someone'; and the Line to Say was written so that a participant can say it honestly without having every answer ('I don't have all the answers, but I have found someone who does'). The naming round reads only the second row of the Light Planner.

### ✅ Answer Key
**Q1.** The Word was in the beginning, was with God, and was God; he was in the beginning with God; all things were made by him, and nothing that was made was made without him (John 1:1–3).

**Q2.** He was a man sent from God, named John; he came for a witness, to bear witness of the Light, so that all men through him might believe; he was not that Light (John 1:6–8).

**Q3.** The world, though made by him, knew him not, and his own received him not (John 1:10–11). But to as many as received him, believing on his name, he gave power to become the sons of God, born of God (John 1:12–13).

**Q4.** He denies being the Christ, Elias, and that prophet, and describes himself as "the voice of one crying in the wilderness" (John 1:23), quoting Isaiah (John 1:20–23).

**Q5.** He says, "Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world" (John 1:29). God had told him that the one on whom he saw the Spirit descending and remaining would be the one who baptizes with the Holy Ghost; he saw it, and bore record that this is the Son of God (John 1:32–34).

**Q6.** John points; two disciples follow Jesus; one, Andrew, finds his brother Simon and brings him; Jesus finds Philip; Philip finds Nathanael and says 'Come and see'; Nathanael confesses Jesus (John 1:35–49). Titles include the Word, God, the Light, the only begotten of the Father, Jesus Christ, the only begotten Son, the Lord (as Isaiah's words are applied to him), the Lamb of God, the Son of God, Rabbi and Master, the Messias and the Christ, him of whom Moses and the prophets wrote, Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph, the King of Israel, and the Son of man (John 1:1, 29, 41, 45, 49, 51).

**Q7.** Jesus is the Word by whom God spoke creation and light into being (Genesis 1:1–3); the glory Moses could not look on, now seen in a face (Exodus 33:18–23; John 1:18); the glory that filled the tabernacle, now dwelling among us (Exodus 40:34; John 1:14); the LORD whose way the voice prepares (Isaiah 40:3); the lamb led to the slaughter (Isaiah 53:7); and the ladder joining earth to heaven (Genesis 28:12–17; John 1:51). God comes near to sinners by coming himself, veiling his glory in flesh and providing the sacrifice.

**Q8.** Keeping only 'with God' makes Jesus a second, lesser being, a creature or a lesser god. Keeping only 'was God' erases the distinction between the Father and the Son, so there is no one to be sent, no one to love and be loved, and no one to declare the Father. John needs both to say what he says in John 1:18 (John 1:1).

**Q9.** The Word 'tabernacled' among us: God's presence, once in the tent of meeting (Exodus 25:8; Exodus 40:34), now lives in a human life. The disciples beheld his glory, full of grace and truth, and saw God declared in the Son, while Moses saw only the back parts (Exodus 33:23; John 1:14, 18).

**Q10.** Model answer: the lamb God would provide in Isaac's place (Genesis 22:8); the Passover lamb whose blood sheltered Israel (Exodus 12:3–7; John 19:36); the suffering servant led as a lamb to the slaughter, bearing others' iniquity (Isaiah 53:6–7). Jesus is God's own provision, the true Passover, and the silent substitute (John 1:29).

**Q11.** Model answer, not the only answer: View A fits the KJV and the next verses, where the world did not know him (John 1:10). View B fits the STEPBible lexicon's sense and John's use of the same verb in John 12:35. Many readers think John intends both: the darkness neither understood nor overpowered the light, and the light still shines (John 1:5).

**Q12.** Moses asked to see God's glory and was given God's goodness passing by, God's name proclaimed, and a view of his back from the cleft of the rock (Exodus 33:18–23; Exodus 34:6). In Jesus we are given what Moses longed for: the glory of God in a human face, grace and truth in person, and the Father declared by the Son (John 1:14, 17–18; 2 Corinthians 4:6).

**Q13.** Answers will vary. Point to Philip, who answered prejudice with an invitation rather than an argument (John 1:46), and to the readiness to give a reason "with meekness and fear" (1 Peter 3:15).

**Q14.** If the Son were a creature, he could not make God fully known, and worshipping him would be idolatry; he also could not give eternal life, which is God's to give. John 1:1–3 says the Word was in the beginning and made every made thing, so he is not made (John 1:3; John 20:28).

**Q15.** Only God can reveal God fully and give eternal life; only a man can die in the place of men and be the Lamb. If Jesus were not God, the Lamb could not take away the sin of the world; if he were not man, he could not be sacrificed at all (John 1:1, 14, 29; Hebrews 2:14, 17).

**Q16.** Answers will vary. Point to John 1:13 and to the gift of grace "not of yourselves" (Ephesians 2:8). The fitting response is gratitude, not pride.

**Q17.** Model answer: it means his love came first, before I sought him, as Nathanael was seen before he was called (John 1:48). "We love him, because he first loved us." (1 John 4:19)

**Q18.** Model answers: deflecting praise at work to others who helped, sharing what God has done rather than what I have done, serving in a hidden role at church, speaking of Jesus by name in a conversation. Humbling ourselves means doing it quietly before God first (John 3:30; John 1:36).

**Q19.** It shows that Jesus is infinitely greater than the greatest of the prophets: John ranks himself below a slave before him (John 1:27, 30). Model answer for the second part: wanting credit for my service, my knowledge, or my family's faith (John 3:30).

**Q20.** Model answers: a relative who trusts a Christian upbringing, a coworker who is trying hard to be good, a friend who assumes whatever their family believes is enough (John 1:13).

**Q21.** Model answers: the knowledge of the one who made them, the welcome of the one who came to them, the hope of a new start (John 1:10–11). The thief comes "to steal, and to kill, and to destroy" (John 10:10).

**Q22.** He gives life (John 1:4), the right to become God's children (John 1:12), and grace upon grace from his fulness (John 1:16). No one can earn or inherit it because it is a birth "of God" (John 1:13) and a gift (Ephesians 2:8).

**Q23.** Model answer: a brother, a parent, a close friend I have never told about Jesus because it feels awkward. Andrew's first act was to find his own brother (John 1:41).

**Q24.** Model answers: a habit, a resentment, a fear, a doubt I have never admitted. Jesus already sees it (John 1:48), and the Lamb takes sin away when it is confessed (John 1:29; 1 John 1:9).

**Q25.** Model answers: a neighbourhood, a nationality, a political group, a denomination, a family member with a past. Nathanael was wrong about Nazareth, and Jesus still received him kindly (John 1:46–47).

**Q26.** Model answer: a friend or relative with questions; listen, answer honestly, and point to Jesus rather than to yourself, as John did (John 1:7–8, 23), inviting them to read the Gospel for themselves (John 1:46).

### 🗺️ Life & Light Leader's Map
**Worked Life Planner Row:** *Who:* my older brother Mark, who was raised in church with me and says he is 'Christian by background' but has not prayed in years. *What the thief has stolen:* the knowledge that faith is more than family heritage; he is resting on blood and the will of man (John 1:13). *What Christ gives instead:* the right to become a child of God by receiving Christ himself (John 1:12). *What I will do this week:* call him on Saturday, tell him in two sentences what Jesus has become to me lately, and ask if he would read John 1 with me over the phone. *Done?* A tick, and a note of what he said.

**Worked Light Planner Row:** *Where it is dark:* my own heart: I have written off my new neighbours because of where they come from, and I have been short with them. *What is hidden there:* a Nathanael prejudice, and pride about my own background. *What light Christ shines:* he saw Nathanael under the fig tree and answered his sneer with grace (John 1:47–48). *How I carry it:* confess it to the Lord tonight, and this week take the neighbours something from my kitchen and learn their names. This first row stays private; in the naming round members read their second row.

**When Someone Has No One to Name:** Do not accept 'no one' too quickly, and do not shame the person either. Andrew began with his own brother, and Philip with his friend from the same town (John 1:41, 44–45). Walk through their circles with them: family, old friends, coworkers, neighbours, the person they sit beside on Sunday. Ask, 'Who in those places has questions about Jesus, or is resting on a family faith?' If they still cannot name anyone, suggest they ask the Lord for one name this week (Matthew 9:38).

**When Someone Points the Light at Others:** When a member uses John 1 to talk about Jehovah's Witnesses, 'cults', or a relative's false beliefs, bring them gently back to the first row of the Light Planner: their own heart. Remind the group that John the Baptist spoke most sharply about himself ('I am not') and pointed to the Lamb, not at sinners, and that Jesus met Nathanael's prejudice with kindness (John 1:47). If there is a real concern for someone, encourage prayer and a patient invitation to read the Gospel together, not an argument (John 1:46; 2 Timothy 2:24–25).

### 🐑 Shepherding Notes
This chapter touches several tender places. Handle them with care.

- **Rejection by one's own.** 'His own received him not' (John 1:11) may stir memories of rejection by family, a church, or a community. Say plainly that Jesus knows that pain from the inside, and that the next verse opens a family that cannot reject its children (John 1:12).
- **Fathers and family.** 'Sons of God' and 'in the bosom of the Father' are good news, but for someone with an absent or abusive father the words may be hard to hear at first. Let the Son define the Father for them (John 1:18; John 14:9), rather than their earthly father defining God.
- **Religious background and assurance.** Some who grew up in church will hear 'not of blood' and fear they were never truly born of God. Do not press them to doubt everything; point them to receiving Christ now, by name, and to the promise that he casts out no one who comes (John 6:37).
- **Relatives in groups that deny Christ's deity.** Members with Jehovah's Witness or Oneness family members may feel anxious or defensive in Question 1. Keep the tone respectful; these are people we love and want to invite to come and see.
- **Pressure and past evangelism.** Some have been hurt by pushy evangelism and will be wary of the 'bring others' application. Emphasize that John 1's witness is an invitation, not pressure, and that Jesus does the finding (John 1:43).
- **The Light Planner's first row.** It is private: never ask anyone to read it aloud, and say so at the start of the naming round. If someone chooses to share, receive it quietly, pray, and keep it in the group.

### 🎯 Teaching Angles
**For a Tier 1 Group:** Use the letter-and-doorbell analogy from Tier 1. Draw a simple timeline: 'In the beginning' at the far left, before creation; then Jesus born in Bethlehem; then the first disciples. Ask: Where was Jesus before he was born? What did he make? What did John the Baptist call him? Then act out the chain of finding: one person finds another and says 'Come and see'. Finish with the memory verse.

**For a Tier 2 Group:** Trace two lines. The dwelling line: the tabernacle (Exodus 25:8; Exodus 40:34), the Word who 'dwelt among us' (John 1:14), the body as temple (John 2:21), and the tabernacle of God with men (Revelation 21:3). The witness line: John's witness (John 1:7, 19, 32, 34), Andrew and Philip's witness (John 1:41, 45), and the purpose of the whole Gospel (John 20:31). Then read verse 51 with Genesis 28 and ask what it means that Jesus is the meeting place of heaven and earth.

**Tough Question 1:** *If Jesus is God, whom was he praying to? Isn't this three gods?* The Bible teaches one God (Isaiah 44:6) and also shows the Father and the Son as two persons who love and speak to each other (John 1:18; John 17:5). Christians do not believe in three gods, nor in one person wearing three masks, but in one God who is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. When the Son prays, the Son speaks to the Father, as he has from eternity 'with God' (John 1:1). We can confess this without fully comprehending it.

**Tough Question 2:** *What about people who never hear about Jesus? Doesn't John 1:9 say everyone has enough light?* John 1:9 says the true Light shines on every man, and Romans 1:20 says creation leaves all without excuse. But the same passage says the world did not know him (John 1:10), and the saving knowledge of God comes through the Son who declares him (John 1:18). That is why the Baptist bore witness 'that all men through him might believe' (John 1:7), and why we must send and go (Romans 10:14). We can trust the Judge of all the earth to do right, and we can obey his command to tell.

**Tough Question 3:** *The Jehovah's Witness Bible ends John 1:1 with the words a god. Isn't that a fair translation?* It is a real argument, and it deserves a respectful answer. Greek has no word for 'a', and a predicate noun before the verb often lacks the article even when it is definite; the construction stresses what the Word is. John also says that everything made was made by the Word (John 1:3), so he cannot be a made god, and the Bible allows no second god (Isaiah 44:6). The Gospel ends with Thomas calling Jesus "My Lord and my God." (John 20:28) Offer to read John with the questioner from beginning to end.
