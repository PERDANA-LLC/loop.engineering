# John 4 · Living Water and the Saviour of the World (KJV)
> Unit john-04 · John 4:1–54 · loop john-kjv-study v2.0 · DRAFT for human doctrine review

## 🧭 At a Glance
### 🧵 Golden Thread
Jesus crosses every barrier to give living water to the thirsty and make true worshippers, and his word alone is enough to give life.

### 🌗 Life & Light Line
John 4 answers the death of a thirst that comes back every day, a life spent returning to wells that cannot satisfy, with the living water Christ gives, which becomes in the one who drinks it "a well of water springing up into everlasting life" (John 4:14). It exposes the darkness of a hidden history and of worship argued about but not known, and it gives the light of the Messiah who tells a woman all that she ever did and, in the same conversation, reveals himself to her as the Christ.

### 🎯 Objectives
- Trace from John 4:7–29 how Jesus leads the Samaritan woman from thirst to testimony, naming each step: his request, his offer, the exposure of her life, the question of worship, his self-revelation, and her witness.
- Explain from John 4:21–24 and John 4:42 why worship and salvation are no longer tied to a mountain or a nation, and what it means that the Father seeks worshippers 'in spirit and in truth'.
- **Doing:** Within the next day, offer the living water to one person you would usually avoid, by asking them a small favour or a kind question as Jesus did (John 4:7) and, if the door opens, telling them in one sentence what Christ has given you (John 4:14).

## 🏛️ Core Foundations
### ✝️ Christological Root
**Gospel Foundation:** The chapter rests on two things Jesus said to a Samaritan woman at a well. The first is his offer: "But whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst; but the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life." (John 4:14) The second is his claim: "Jesus saith unto her, I that speak unto thee am he." (John 4:26) The offer shows what he gives; the claim shows who he is. The town's confession draws the two together: "And said unto the woman, Now we believe, not because of thy saying: for we have heard him ourselves, and know that this is indeed the Christ, the Saviour of the world." (John 4:42) And the second sign shows that his word is enough to give what it promises: "thy son liveth" (John 4:50).

**The Bridge:** The living water is not a thing Jesus points to, like a prophet pointing to God's gift; it is something he says he will give, and 'the gift of God' (John 4:10) is bound up with 'who it is that saith to thee' (John 4:10). The doctrines grounded here are the person of Christ as the Messiah and the Saviour of the world (John 4:26, 42), and salvation as God's free gift, received by asking and by believing his word (John 4:10, 41, 50). John himself interprets the living water later as the Spirit whom those who believe on Jesus would receive (John 7:38–39).

**Canonical Echoes:** Jesus (John 4:14) → Promise (Jeremiah 2:13; Isaiah 55:1) → Doctrine (John 7:38–39) → Life (Revelation 22:17)

**What Gets Lost:** Taught without Christ at the center, John 4 becomes a lesson in cross-cultural manners ('be kind to outsiders'), a technique for evangelism ('ask a question, build a bridge, raise the topic of sin'), or a moral story about a woman with a past. All of those contain something true. But the woman did not go back to her town saying 'come, meet a kind man' or 'come, learn a method'. She said, 'is not this the Christ?' (John 4:29). Remove Christ and the chapter becomes advice; keep him at the center and it becomes a spring that does not run dry.

### ⚔️ Spiritual Warfare
**The Lie:** Your past disqualifies you, and people like 'them' are beyond God's reach. Its religious twin says that the real question is which mountain, which tradition, which group is right, so that the argument about worship can stand in for worship itself.

**The Truth:** The Father is looking for worshippers, and he finds them in unlikely places: "the hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him" (John 4:23). The one who knows all that we ever did is the same one who offers the living water (John 4:10, 29).

**The Battleground:** Two places. The first is the walls between people, ethnic, religious, moral, and social, that say 'no dealings' (John 4:9). The second is the private place where a person keeps a history they do not want named, and where they reach for a safer subject whenever the conversation comes close (John 4:16–20).

**The Weapon:** The word of Christ himself, spoken and heard. Jesus overcomes the woman's defences with his own words, and the town believes "because of his own word" (John 4:41). The nobleman's son lives by a word spoken in Cana, while the boy lies sick in Capernaum (John 4:46, 50). The believer's part is the woman's: to say 'Come, see' and to bring people where they can hear him.

### 🌱 Life Pathway
**The Theft:** In John 4 the thief steals by thirst. The woman comes every day to the same well at the hottest hour, and Jesus puts his finger on the rule of her life: "Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again" (John 4:13). Five husbands, and now a man who is not her husband (John 4:18): whatever the reasons, each of those wells has run dry. The thief also steals by walls: the old hostility that says Jews and Samaritans have no dealings (John 4:9) would have kept her from ever hearing the offer. And he steals by delay, the voice that says 'there are yet four months' (John 4:35), when the fields are already white.

**The Source:** Christ himself, who gives the water that becomes in the drinker 'a well of water springing up into everlasting life' (John 4:14). He came "that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly" (John 10:10). His means in this chapter are his word (John 4:26, 41, 50), his gift freely given to those who ask (John 4:10), and, as John explains later, his Spirit (John 7:39).

**The Channel:** The woman leaves her waterpot and goes into the city: "Come, see a man, which told me all things that ever I did: is not this the Christ?" (John 4:29) She does not argue or explain; she points and invites, and then the town hears him for themselves (John 4:42). A believer passes life on the same way: by telling what Christ has done, by bringing people to hear his word, and by stepping back so that they believe 'not because of thy saying' but because they have heard him themselves.

**The Abundance Guard:** Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, not prosperity, ease, or freedom from suffering. The woman still went back to the same town and the same reputation; the living water did not change her circumstances first, it changed her. Jesus himself sat wearied at the well and called doing the Father's will his 'meat' (John 4:34), not comfort. And the nobleman's son was healed as a sign, not as a promise that every sick child will be raised from a sickbed; the life Christ guarantees is 'everlasting life' (John 4:14, 36).

### 💡 Light Pathway
**The Darkness:** John 4 exposes two kinds of darkness. One is personal: a history the woman would rather not discuss, which Jesus names quietly (John 4:16–18). The other is religious: worship argued over but not known. "Ye worship ye know not what" (John 4:22), Jesus tells her, and the old quarrel between Gerizim and Jerusalem had become a way of keeping God at the distance of a debate.

**The Source:** The Messiah, of whom the woman says "he will tell us all things" (John 4:25), and who has just told her all things she ever did (John 4:29). He is "the light of the world" (John 8:12). "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts" (2 Corinthians 4:6).

**The Lamp:** Truth told about ourselves: the woman's plain 'I have no husband' (John 4:17), which Jesus calls well said. Worship in truth, according to what God has revealed, and not in the fog of 'ye know not what'. Testimony that names what the light showed: 'He told me all that ever I did' (John 4:39). The unfruitful works of darkness reproved, first in our own lives (Ephesians 5:11), and good works that glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16).

**The Love Guard:** Light without love is glare. Jesus names the woman's history privately, after he has offered her the gift of God, and without a word of contempt; her 'all that ever I did' becomes the start of her witness, not the end of her dignity. We turn the lamp on our own hidden things first, and when we speak to others we speak as Jesus did: alone, gently, with the offer of living water in the same breath.

### 🏺 Setting the Scene
John 4 opens with Jesus leaving Judaea, where his disciples have been baptizing more people than John the Baptist (John 3:22–26; John 4:1–2), and heading north to Galilee. Samaria lay between the two. Its people were descended partly from Israelites of the old northern kingdom and partly from the peoples the king of Assyria settled there after the fall of Samaria (2 Kings 17:24). Jews regarded them as a mixed people with a mixed religion: "They feared the LORD, and served their own gods" (2 Kings 17:33). By Jesus' day the Samaritans worshipped the one God of Israel, accepted only the five books of Moses as Scripture, and held that the true place of worship was Mount Gerizim, above the town of Shechem, not Jerusalem. They had built a temple there, which a Jewish ruler, John Hyrcanus, destroyed more than a century before Jesus was born (verify the date, usually given as the late second century BC). The hostility ran both ways and ran deep.

The well lies at the foot of Mount Gerizim, near the ancient town of Shechem, in the plot of ground Jacob bought (Genesis 33:18–19) and later gave to Joseph (Genesis 48:22), where Joseph's bones were buried (Joshua 24:32). The Old Testament does not say that Jacob dug a well there, but the local tradition that he did was old and proud (John 4:12), and a deep well at that site is still shown today. Wells in Scripture are also meeting places: Abraham's servant found Rebekah at a well (Genesis 24:11), Jacob met Rachel at a well (Genesis 29:10), and Moses sat down by a well in Midian (Exodus 2:15) and met his wife there.

The chapter ends back in Cana of Galilee, where Jesus had turned water into wine (John 2:1–11). A 'nobleman', an official in the service of a king, most likely Herod Antipas, who ruled Galilee, comes from Capernaum, on the shore of the lake, to beg Jesus to come and heal his dying son.

**View A:** 'In spirit and in truth' (John 4:23–24) describes the worshipper's own inner life: worship from the heart, sincere and real, not just outward and bound to a place. God looks for honesty within, as David prayed: "Behold, thou desirest truth in the inward parts" (Psalm 51:6).

**View B:** 'In spirit and in truth' describes worship by the Holy Spirit and through Jesus, who is the truth. Jesus calls the Spirit "the Spirit of truth" (John 14:17) and himself "the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6), and Paul describes believers as those "which worship God in the spirit, and rejoice in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 3:3).

### 👣 Case Study · Scripture in Action
John the son of Zebedee was one of the disciples who came back from the town in John 4:27 and wondered why Jesus was talking with a Samaritan woman. Some time later Jesus sent messengers into a Samaritan village that refused to receive him, and John and his brother had their answer ready: "Lord, wilt thou that we command fire to come down from heaven, and consume them, even as Elias did?" (Luke 9:54). Jesus rebuked them. After the resurrection Jesus told the apostles that they would be his witnesses "both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea, and in Samaria" (Acts 1:8). When Philip preached Christ in Samaria and many believed, the apostles in Jerusalem "sent unto them Peter and John" (Acts 8:14). And on the way home, John, who once wanted fire on Samaritan villages, "preached the gospel in many villages of the Samaritans" (Acts 8:25). The fields Jesus showed him at Sychar were white; John came back years later to reap them.

**Q13.** What had to change in John before he could carry the gospel to the people he once wanted destroyed, and who do you find it hardest to imagine as a fellow worshipper (John 4:9, 42)?

### 🏙️ Modern Example
Diane had lived next door to Carol for six years and had spoken to her perhaps a dozen times. Everyone on the street knew Carol's story, or thought they did: three marriages, a son who did not visit, a man's truck in the driveway most nights. Diane went to church; Carol did not, and Diane had quietly decided that Carol would not be interested. Then Diane's group studied John 4, and she noticed that Jesus began with a request, not a lecture. The next Saturday, when her car battery died, Diane knocked on Carol's door and asked to borrow jumper cables. Carol was so surprised that she talked for twenty minutes. Over the next months the favours went both ways. One evening Carol said she had given up on church because she knew what church people thought of her. Diane told her plainly that Jesus knew all about her, far more than the neighbours did, and that he had offered living water to a woman with a history very like hers. She showed her John 4:14. Carol did not believe that night. But she asked if she could read the chapter herself, and she began coming to Diane's kitchen table on Thursday mornings to read the Gospel of John.

**Q14.** Who have you quietly decided would 'not be interested', and what small request or act of kindness could open a door to them, as 'Give me to drink' did (John 4:7)?

### 📜 Voices from Church History
Horatius Bonar, a Scottish minister of the Free Church, wrote the hymn *I Heard the Voice of Jesus Say* (published around 1846, verify). Each of its three stanzas has the same shape: first Jesus' invitation, drawn from his own words in John's Gospel, then the singer's answer telling how he came and what he found. The first stanza hears the call to the weary to come and rest. The second, drawn from John 4 and John 7, hears Jesus offering living water freely to the thirsty, and tells how the singer came, drank, and found his thirst quenched and his soul revived, so that he now lives in Christ. The third hears Jesus say that he is the light of the dark world, and tells how the singer looked to him and found in him his star and his sun, and will walk in that light until his travelling days are done. The hymn puts John 4 in the first person: not a story about a Samaritan woman long ago, but the testimony of anyone who has come to Christ and drunk.

**Q15.** Bonar's hymn joins life and light, the living water and the light of the world, in one song of testimony. How would you put John 4:14 into your own words as a testimony of what Christ has done for you, and to whom could you say it?

### ⛪ Doctrine in My Own Words
- **Theology Proper:** God is Spirit; he is not confined to any mountain or building, and the Father himself seeks true worshippers (John 4:23–24).
- **Christology:** Jesus is the Messiah, who revealed himself to a Samaritan woman, and the Saviour of the world (John 4:26, 42).
- **Christology:** Jesus is truly man, wearied and thirsty at the well, and he is the Lord who knows all that we ever did (John 4:6, 29).
- **Pneumatology:** The living water that Jesus gives is the gift of the Spirit, springing up within the believer into everlasting life (John 4:10, 14; John 7:39).
- **Anthropology & Sin:** Every human being is thirsty, and every well apart from Christ leaves us thirsting again (John 4:13).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** Salvation is the free gift of God, promised through Israel and offered to all, received by asking the one who gives it (John 4:10, 22, 42).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** Saving faith takes Jesus at his word, and grows from hearing about him to hearing him (John 4:41–42, 50).
- **Ecclesiology:** The Father gathers one people of true worshippers from every nation, no longer bound to Gerizim or Jerusalem (John 4:21–23).
- **Eschatology:** The hour of true worship and the harvest of everlasting life have already begun in Jesus (John 4:23, 35–36).
- **Christian Walk:** Doing the Father's will is the believer's food, and every believer sows or reaps in a harvest that belongs to God (John 4:34–38).

### 👑 Christocentric & Humility · Level 4
Every problem in John 4 is answered in Jesus. The woman is thirsty, and he gives living water that becomes a spring within. Her life has been a string of empty wells, and he names it without turning away. Jews and Samaritans have no dealings, and he asks her for a drink and stays two days in her town. Samaritans worship what they do not know, and he reveals the Father and himself. Worship is split between two mountains, and he announces an hour when the true worshippers will worship the Father in spirit and in truth. The woman waits for a Messiah to tell her all things, and he says, 'I that speak unto thee am he.' The disciples think of lunch, and he is fed by doing his Father's will. A father is losing his son in another town, and a single word from Jesus gives the boy life. Every truth in the chapter is found in him too: the gift of God, the water of life, the knowledge of God, salvation, the harvest, and the life-giving word are all located in the one who sat wearied on Jacob's well.

The chapter humbles us from several sides. It humbles the respectable, because one of the longest conversations the Gospels record between Jesus and a single person is with a Samaritan woman with a broken history, and she becomes the first to carry his name into a town. It humbles the disciples, and us with them, because they came back with food and missed the harvest. It humbles every evangelist, because the town believes at last 'not because of thy saying' (John 4:42) but because they heard him. And it humbles the nobleman, a man of rank, who has to go home with nothing but a word.

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy
### 📌 Core Concept
One hot day, Jesus was tired from walking and sat down by an old well. A woman came to get water, and Jesus asked her for a drink. She was surprised, because Jews and Samaritans did not get along at all. Then Jesus told her that he could give her a different kind of water, 'living water'. If you drink water from a well, you get thirsty again. But the water Jesus gives is like a spring inside you that never stops, and it gives you life with God that lasts for ever. Jesus knew everything about her, even the sad and wrong things in her life, and he still offered her this gift. Then he told her who he was: the Christ, the Saviour God had promised. She was so excited that she left her water jar and ran to tell the whole town, 'Come and see!' Later Jesus healed a boy who was very sick in another town, just by saying, 'Your son lives', and the boy got better at that very moment.

### 📖 Key Verses
"But whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst; but the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life." (John 4:14) Jesus gives a life inside us that keeps springing up, like water that never runs out.

"God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth." (John 4:24) We can worship God anywhere, with our whole heart, in the way he has shown us.

"Come, see a man, which told me all things that ever I did: is not this the Christ?" (John 4:29) When Jesus changes us, we want to tell other people to come and meet him.

"Jesus saith unto him, Go thy way; thy son liveth. And the man believed the word that Jesus had spoken unto him, and he went his way." (John 4:50) Jesus' word is so powerful that it works even when he is far away.

### 🪞 Analogy
Imagine two ways of getting water. One is a bucket you carry to a well every day. You fill it, carry it home, use it up, and the next day you are thirsty again and have to go back. The other is a spring that bubbles up right in your own garden, fresh every morning, and never runs dry. Lots of things in life are like the bucket: toys, friends' praise, winning games. They are good, but they run out, and we need more. What Jesus gives is like the spring. It comes from him, it lives inside us, and it never runs out.

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium
### 📜 Walkthrough
#### John 4:1–4 · He Must Needs Go Through Samaria
**Says:** "When therefore the Lord knew how the Pharisees had heard that Jesus made and baptized more disciples than John," (John 4:1) "(Though Jesus himself baptized not, but his disciples,)" (John 4:2) "He left Judaea, and departed again into Galilee." (John 4:3) "And he must needs go through Samaria." (John 4:4) *Must needs* means had to.

**Means:** The chapter opens with a narrator's explanation. The end of John 3 showed the Baptist's disciples troubled that 'all men come to him' (John 3:26); now the Pharisees have heard the same report. John calls Jesus 'the Lord', *kurios* (G2962, John 4:1), a title the narrator uses only rarely before the resurrection, and he adds a correction in brackets: Jesus himself did not baptize; his disciples did. The baptism was done under his authority, so both statements are true (see John 3:22). Rather than let a rivalry with John be stirred up, or let the conflict with the Pharisees come to a head before the Father's time (John 2:4), Jesus withdraws north to Galilee.

'He must needs go through Samaria.' The verb is *edei* (G1163, John 4:4, V-IAI-3S), 'it was necessary'. There was a geographical reason: the direct road from Judaea to Galilee ran through Samaria, and the historian Josephus says Galileans commonly took it on their way to the feasts (verify the reference, usually given in his *Antiquities*, book 20). Strict Jews could, and some did, go around by the Jordan valley. But John uses the same word for the necessities God sets: 'Ye must be born again' (John 3:7), the Son of man 'must' be lifted up (John 3:14), 'He must increase' (John 3:30). It comes 3 times in this chapter (John 4:4, 20, 24). Many readers conclude that John means both: the road went that way, and the Father had an appointment for his Son at a well. The text does not force the second reading, but the rest of the chapter makes it hard to miss.

**Asks:** What 'musts' in your week look like mere routes and schedules? The Lord of the harvest may have an appointment waiting on a road you would rather avoid. Go where he sends you, even through Samaria.

#### John 4:5–6 · Wearied at Jacob's Well
**Says:** "Then cometh he to a city of Samaria, which is called Sychar, near to the parcel of ground that Jacob gave to his son Joseph." (John 4:5) "Now Jacob's well was there. Jesus therefore, being wearied with his journey, sat thus on the well: and it was about the sixth hour." (John 4:6) *Parcel* means plot; *wearied* means worn out.

**Means:** Sychar lay near ancient Shechem, at the foot of Mount Gerizim; many identify it with the village of Askar, others with Shechem itself (verify). The 'parcel of ground' takes the reader back to Jacob, who "bought a parcel of a field, where he had spread his tent" (Genesis 33:19), gave Joseph "one portion above thy brethren" (Genesis 48:22), and whose son's bones were buried there on Israel's return (Joshua 24:32). The ground is soaked in the history the Jews and Samaritans both claimed.

John calls the well a *pēgē* (G4077, John 4:6), a spring. The STEPBible lexicon (Tyndale House, whose Greek entries draw largely on Abbott-Smith) gives 'a spring, fountain', and lists this verse as 'a well fed by a spring'. Remember the word: Jesus will use it for what his water becomes in the believer (John 4:14).

Then one of the most human sentences in the Gospels. Jesus was 'wearied', *kekopiakōs* (G2872, John 4:6, V-RAP-NSM), a perfect participle: tired out, and still tired. The Word who was God (John 1:1), who made all things (John 1:3), sat down on the edge of a well because his legs were tired. Isaiah says of the LORD that he "fainteth not, neither is weary" (Isaiah 40:28); John shows us the eternal Son in true human flesh, able to be weary. The letter to the Hebrews draws the comfort: "For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities" (Hebrews 4:15). 'Sat thus' probably means simply 'just as he was', as a tired traveller sits.

'About the sixth hour': counting from sunrise, as the Jews did, noon, the hottest part of the day. (A minority, following B. F. Westcott, read John's hours by a Roman count from midnight, making it six in the morning or evening; verify, and most commentators prefer noon.) Women usually drew water in the cool of the morning or evening: "the time that women go out to draw water" (Genesis 24:11). Why this woman came at noon John does not say. Many infer that she was avoiding the other women; that is possible, but it is an inference, not the text. John will mention the sixth hour once more, when Pilate brings out Jesus and says 'Behold your King!' (John 19:14), and the wearied man at the well will say from the cross, 'I thirst' (John 19:28).

**Asks:** Jesus knows tiredness from the inside. Bring your weariness to him without pretending, and let his weariness at the well humble you: this is how far the Son came to meet you.

#### John 4:7–9 · Give Me to Drink
**Says:** "There cometh a woman of Samaria to draw water: Jesus saith unto her, Give me to drink." (John 4:7) "(For his disciples were gone away unto the city to buy meat.)" (John 4:8) "Then saith the woman of Samaria unto him, How is it that thou, being a Jew, askest drink of me, which am a woman of Samaria? for the Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans." (John 4:9) *Meat* means food of any kind; *no dealings* means no contact.

**Means:** Three walls stand between Jesus and this woman. She is a Samaritan, and he a Jew. She is a woman, and teachers did not normally talk with women in public; the disciples will be astonished at exactly this (John 4:27). And, as the conversation will show, she carries a history that would have made a religious man keep his distance. Jesus steps over all three with four words: 'Give me to drink.' He does not open with a rebuke or a sermon, but with a request. He puts himself in her debt. The disciples are away buying food, so the conversation is private; John adds the note in brackets so that we know no one else heard.

Her reply is astonishment. She can tell he is a Jew, perhaps by his dress or accent. John explains for his readers: 'the Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans.' The verb is *sugchrōntai* (G4798, John 4:9, V-PNI-3P). The STEPBible lexicon defines it as 'to use together with', and so, of persons, 'to associate with', and lists this verse. Some scholars, following David Daube (verify), take the older sense literally: Jews do not use vessels in common with Samaritans, since they regarded Samaritan vessels as unclean; that would explain her surprise that he would drink from her jar. Others take the general sense: Jews avoid contact with Samaritans. Either way, Jesus is willing to do what his people would not.

**Asks:** Who are your 'no dealings' people? Jesus began with a small request that honoured a despised person. You can begin the same way: ask, receive, say thank you, and let the wall come down from your side first.

#### John 4:10–12 · The Gift of God and Living Water
**Says:** "Jesus answered and said unto her, If thou knewest the gift of God, and who it is that saith to thee, Give me to drink; thou wouldest have asked of him, and he would have given thee living water." (John 4:10) "The woman saith unto him, Sir, thou hast nothing to draw with, and the well is deep: from whence then hast thou that living water?" (John 4:11) "Art thou greater than our father Jacob, which gave us the well, and drank thereof himself, and his children, and his cattle?" (John 4:12) *Whence* means where from; *thereof* means of it.

**Means:** Jesus turns the conversation round. She thinks he is the one in need; in fact she is. Two things she does not know: 'the gift of God', and 'who it is that saith to thee'. The gift is *dōrea* (G1431, John 4:10), a free gift; the STEPBible lexicon lists this verse under 'the gift of God', beside Acts 8:20 and the gift of the Spirit in Acts 2:38. The gift and the giver are bound together: if she knew who was asking, she would be the one asking, and he would give. All she has to do is ask.

'Living water' is *hudōr zōn*, water that is living, *zōn* (G2198, John 4:10, V-PAP-ASN). In ordinary speech it meant running water, fresh from a spring or a stream, as against the still water of a cistern. The Hebrew adjective *chay* (H2416) has the same range; the STEPBible lexicon includes 'flowing, fresh (of water)' among its senses. But the prophets had given the phrase a deeper meaning. God says of his people, "they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water" (Jeremiah 2:13). Zechariah sees a day when "living waters shall go out from Jerusalem" (Zechariah 14:8). Jesus is offering what God alone gives.

The woman takes him at the ordinary level, as Nicodemus did (John 3:4). He has no bucket, *antlēma* (G502, John 4:11), and the well is deep. She now calls the well a *phrear* (G5421, John 4:11), a dug well, where John had called it a spring. And she calls him 'Sir', *kurie* (G2962, John 4:11), the ordinary polite address; the reader, who has just seen John call him 'the Lord' in verse 1, hears more than she means. Her last question is full of John's irony: is he 'greater than our father Jacob'? The expected answer is no. The true answer is yes. The same question will be asked about Abraham: "Art thou greater than our father Abraham, which is dead?" (John 8:53).

**Asks:** What would you ask for if you knew who is speaking to you? The gift of God is not earned or drawn up with your own bucket. It is asked for, and given.

#### John 4:13–15 · A Well Springing Up
**Says:** "Jesus answered and said unto her, Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again:" (John 4:13) "But whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst; but the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life." (John 4:14) "The woman saith unto him, Sir, give me this water, that I thirst not, neither come hither to draw." (John 4:15) *Hither* means here.

**Means:** Jesus answers her question about Jacob without arguing about Jacob. He compares the two waters. Everyone who drinks the well's water 'shall thirst again', *dipsēsei* (G1372, John 4:13, V-FAI-3S): the thirst comes back, every day, for as long as we live. The present participle behind 'whosoever drinketh' describes someone who keeps drinking. By contrast, whoever drinks of the water Christ gives (here the verb is an aorist, a simple act of drinking) 'shall never thirst'. The Greek is emphatic: a double negative, with *eis ton aiōna*, 'unto the age', *aiōna* (G165, John 4:14): he will not thirst, ever. This does not mean that a believer never longs for God again; the psalmists who knew God thirsted for more of him (Psalm 42:1–2). It means that the deep thirst of a life without God is met at its source.

The reason follows: the water becomes 'in him a well of water', and here the word is *pēgē* (G4077, John 4:14), a spring, the word John used for Jacob's well in verse 6. Jesus does not give a bucketful that must be refilled; he gives a spring. It is 'springing up', *hallomenou* (G242, John 4:14, V-PNP-GSN). The STEPBible lexicon defines *hallomai* as 'to leap', and 'of water, to spring up', listing this verse; the same verb describes the lame man at the temple gate 'leaping up' (Acts 3:8). And it springs up 'into everlasting life', *aiōnion* (G166, John 4:14): its source is Christ, it lives in the believer, and it rises toward the life of the age to come. Isaiah promised it: "Therefore with joy shall ye draw water out of the wells of salvation." (Isaiah 12:3) And God's invitation had been open for centuries: "Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters" (Isaiah 55:1).

The woman asks. It is a real request, but still on the earthly level: she wants to be spared the daily walk in the heat. Jesus will not leave her there.

**Asks:** Which well do you keep returning to? Name it honestly: a relationship, success, approval, comfort, even religion. Then ask for the water only Christ gives, and let him make it a spring in you.

#### John 4:16–18 · Go, Call Thy Husband
**Says:** "Jesus saith unto her, Go, call thy husband, and come hither." (John 4:16) "The woman answered and said, I have no husband. Jesus said unto her, Thou hast well said, I have no husband:" (John 4:17) "For thou hast had five husbands; and he whom thou now hast is not thy husband: in that saidst thou truly." (John 4:18)

**Means:** This looks like a change of subject, but it is the answer to her request. She has asked for the water; Jesus now shows her why she needs it. Her thirst is not only in her throat. He asks for the one person she cannot produce. Her answer is short and true, and perhaps meant to close the subject: 'I have no husband.' Jesus does not contradict her; he commends her twice: 'Thou hast well said', and 'in that saidst thou truly', *alēthes* (G227, John 4:18). Then he says what she did not: five husbands, and the man she now has is not her husband.

What does this tell us about her? The text states the facts and does not give the causes. In that world a woman could be widowed more than once, and it was husbands who ordinarily divorced wives, not the reverse, so some of her losses may have been griefs done to her. But the present arrangement Jesus plainly distinguishes from marriage, and the conversation shows her as someone whose life has been a string of wells that ran dry. The preacher's picture of a notorious woman shunned by the town goes beyond the text; so does the picture of a pure victim. What the text makes clear is that Jesus knew all of it, named it without a word of scorn, and kept talking with her. He had done the same with Nathanael: "Before that Philip called thee, when thou wast under the fig tree, I saw thee." (John 1:48) He "knew what was in man" (John 2:25).

**Asks:** Let Jesus name what you would rather he left alone. He does not name it to shame you but to give you water. 'Thou hast well said' is the voice of a Lord who welcomes honest confession.

#### John 4:19–22 · This Mountain or Jerusalem
**Says:** "The woman saith unto him, Sir, I perceive that thou art a prophet." (John 4:19) "Our fathers worshipped in this mountain; and ye say, that in Jerusalem is the place where men ought to worship." (John 4:20) "Jesus saith unto her, Woman, believe me, the hour cometh, when ye shall neither in this mountain, nor yet at Jerusalem, worship the Father." (John 4:21) "Ye worship ye know not what: we know what we worship: for salvation is of the Jews." (John 4:22) *Perceive* means see, recognize; *ought* here means must.

**Means:** 'I perceive', *theōrō* (G2334, John 4:19, V-PAI-1S): she has seen something. Only a prophet, *prophētēs* (G4396, John 4:19), could know what he knows. The Samaritans, who accepted only the books of Moses, looked for a prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15, 18). Then she raises the great question dividing Samaritans and Jews: where must God be worshipped? 'This mountain' is Gerizim, which rose above them as they talked. The Samaritans pointed to Moses, who told Israel to "put the blessing upon mount Gerizim" (Deuteronomy 11:29); the Jews pointed to "the place which the LORD your God shall choose" (Deuteronomy 12:5), which God showed to be Jerusalem, 'the mount Zion which he loved' (Psalm 78:68). The word 'ought' is again *dei* (G1163, John 4:20): where is it necessary to worship?

Is she deflecting, or asking in earnest? Probably both. The subject has come uncomfortably close, and a religious controversy is safer. But if he is a prophet, this is the question a Samaritan would want a prophet to settle, and a woman with her history may well have wondered where, if anywhere, she could meet God. Jesus takes the question seriously, and he answers it on a level she did not expect.

First, 'Woman, believe me': the same respectful address he used for his mother (John 2:4). 'The hour cometh' when the question of the mountain will be over: neither Gerizim nor Jerusalem will be the place. In John, 'the hour' is the hour of Jesus' death and glory (John 12:23; John 13:1; John 17:1), and his body is the new temple (John 2:19, 21). Second, he does not pretend that the two sides are equal. 'Ye worship ye know not what: we know what we worship'. The Samaritans had set aside the prophets and the writings, and their worship, sincere as it may have been, lacked the full revelation God had given. 'For salvation is of the Jews': *sōtēria* (G4991, John 4:22). God's saving purpose came through Israel: the covenants, the law, the prophets, and the Messiah himself, "of whom as concerning the flesh Christ came" (Romans 9:5). The promise that the sceptre would not depart from Judah (Genesis 49:10) stands behind the words. Jesus speaks as a Jew, and he says 'we'.

**Asks:** Notice that Jesus neither flatters nor scorns her tradition. He tells her the truth and opens a door beyond both sides. When the conversation about God turns into an argument about places and parties, ask the question beneath it: do I know the one I worship?

#### John 4:23–24 · In Spirit and in Truth
**Says:** "But the hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him." (John 4:23) "God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth." (John 4:24)

**Means:** 'And now is': the new age of worship is not only coming; with Jesus present at the well, it has begun. Jesus says the same of the dead who will hear his voice: "The hour is coming, and now is" (John 5:25).

'The true worshippers': *alēthinoi* (G228, John 4:23), true in the sense of real, genuine, as against shadow or copy. John uses the word for the 'true Light' (John 1:9), the 'true bread' (John 6:32), and the 'true vine' (John 15:1). 'Worshippers' is *proskunētai* (G4353, John 4:23); the noun occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. The verb, *proskuneō* (G4352, John 4:20), means to bow down, do reverence, worship; 'worship' in some form comes 10 times in John 4. The whole conversation has turned on it.

'For the Father seeketh such to worship him': *zētei* (G2212, John 4:23, V-PAI-3S), a present, 'is seeking'. This is the heart of the passage. The woman came to draw water; the Father had come looking for a worshipper. The Son of man "is come to seek and to save that which was lost" (Luke 19:10), and here he is doing it.

'God is a Spirit': in the Greek, *pneuma* (G4151, John 4:24) stands first for emphasis, and there is no indefinite article in Greek, so the words can be rendered 'God is spirit'. The point is God's nature: he is not a body, not bound to a place, not contained in a mountain or a building. Solomon knew it (1 Kings 8:27), and so did Isaiah: "The heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool: where is the house that ye build unto me?" (Isaiah 66:1). Because God is spirit, worship that pleases him is 'in spirit and in truth', and the necessity is *dei* (G1163, John 4:24) again: it 'must' be so. One preposition governs both words, as with water and Spirit in John 3:5: this is one kind of worship described two ways, not two separate requirements.

What do 'spirit' and 'truth' mean? Some take 'spirit' as the worshipper's own inward being: real, heartfelt worship as against outward, place-bound ritual. Others take it as the Holy Spirit, whom the new age brings (John 7:39), and 'truth' as the truth revealed in Jesus, who is 'full of grace and truth' (John 1:14) and 'the truth' (John 14:6). The two readings are closer than they look, because the Spirit is the one who makes worship inward and real, and he is 'the Spirit of truth' (John 14:17). (See the View A / View B panel and the Debate Corner.) Either way, Jesus has moved worship from the place to the person: not where you worship, but whether you worship the Father, as he has made himself known, with a heart made alive by his Spirit.

**Asks:** The Father is seeking worshippers. Does he find one in you? Worship is not first a place you go or a style you prefer, but the whole heart bowing before the Father as he has revealed himself in his Son.

#### John 4:25–26 · I That Speak unto Thee Am He
**Says:** "The woman saith unto him, I know that Messias cometh, which is called Christ: when he is come, he will tell us all things." (John 4:25) "Jesus saith unto her, I that speak unto thee am he." (John 4:26) *Messias* is the Greek form of the Hebrew word for Messiah.

**Means:** The woman reaches for the last certainty she has: the Messiah is coming, and when he comes he will settle all this. *Messias* (G3323, John 4:25) is the Hebrew title *mashiach* (H4899), 'anointed one', in Greek letters; John translates it, as he did in John 1:41, 'which is called Christ', *christos* (G5547, John 4:25). The Samaritans' own hope, drawn from the prophet like Moses of Deuteronomy 18:15–18, was for a coming restorer; later Samaritan writings call him the Taheb (verify, since those sources are later than the New Testament). 'He will tell us all things': *anangelei* (G312, John 4:25, V-FAI-3S), he will announce, declare. The irony is rich: the man in front of her has just told her 'all things that ever I did' (John 4:29).

Jesus' answer is the plainest self-disclosure in the Gospel up to this point: 'I that speak unto thee am he.' In Greek it is *egō* (G1473, John 4:26), 'I', and *eimi* (G1510, John 4:26, V-PAI-1S), 'am', with 'the one speaking to you'; the KJV supplies 'he'. In context the sense is plain: I am the Messiah you are waiting for. Many readers also hear in *egō eimi* an early note of the 'I am' sayings to come, above all "Before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58), where the words stand alone and echo God's name in Exodus 3:14. That echo is possible here, but it should not be pressed; the claim in John 4:26 is already astonishing enough. In Jerusalem Jesus 'did not commit himself' to the crowds (John 2:24), and in the other Gospels he often tells people to keep quiet about who he is. But to a Samaritan woman, at a well, at noon, he says it outright.

**Asks:** The one who knows everything about you is the one who reveals himself to you. Do not keep him at the distance of 'when he is come'. He is speaking to you now.

#### John 4:27–30 · The Waterpot Left Behind
**Says:** "And upon this came his disciples, and marvelled that he talked with the woman: yet no man said, What seekest thou? or, Why talkest thou with her?" (John 4:27) "The woman then left her waterpot, and went her way into the city, and saith to the men," (John 4:28) "Come, see a man, which told me all things that ever I did: is not this the Christ?" (John 4:29) "Then they went out of the city, and came unto him." (John 4:30) *Marvelled* means were astonished.

**Means:** The disciples arrive at the very moment of revelation, and they are astonished, *ethaumasan* (G2296, John 4:27), not that he is talking with a Samaritan, but 'that he talked with the woman'; the Greek, *gunaikos* (G1135, John 4:27), can be read 'with a woman'. Rabbinic sayings of a later period warn teachers against long talk with women (verify the particular sayings); the disciples share the instinct. Yet 'no man said' a word. Whatever they thought, they had learned enough to hold their tongues.

The woman 'left her waterpot'. The word is *hudria* (G5201, John 4:28); it occurs 3 times in the New Testament, all in John: the six waterpots at Cana (John 2:6–7) and this one. At Cana, water in the jars became wine; here, the jar is simply left behind, because its owner has found other water. John does not explain why she left it; it may be haste, or that she meant to return, or that she no longer needed it. John leaves the detail for us to feel.

Her message is simple. 'Come, see' echoes Jesus' first invitation (John 1:39) and Philip's to Nathanael (John 1:46). 'A man, which told me all things that ever I did': the fact she might have hidden has become the first line of her testimony. And then a cautious question, with *mēti* (G3385, John 4:29) in the Greek, a particle that expects a hesitant answer: 'is not this the Christ?', something like 'this could not be the Christ, could it?' She does not overclaim; she invites. And the town comes. The imperfect *ērchonto* (G2064, John 4:30) pictures them on their way, coming across the fields toward him, while Jesus speaks to the disciples in the verses that follow.

**Asks:** What would it mean for you to leave your waterpot? You do not need to have all the answers to invite someone. 'Come, see' is enough, and your own story of what Christ knows about you and has done for you is part of the invitation.

#### John 4:31–34 · Meat Ye Know Not Of
**Says:** "In the mean while his disciples prayed him, saying, Master, eat." (John 4:31) "But he said unto them, I have meat to eat that ye know not of." (John 4:32) "Therefore said the disciples one to another, Hath any man brought him ought to eat?" (John 4:33) "Jesus saith unto them, My meat is to do the will of him that sent me, and to finish his work." (John 4:34) *Prayed him* means urged him; *ought* means anything.

**Means:** Now the disciples misunderstand, as the woman did. She heard 'water' and thought of buckets; they hear 'meat', *brōsin* (G1035, John 4:32), food, and wonder who brought him lunch. John's Gospel uses these misunderstandings as doorways: the listener's earthly sense is the step from which Jesus lifts them to the heavenly one.

'My meat is to do the will of him that sent me'. *Brōma* (G1033, John 4:34), food. Jesus was wearied and hungry at noon. He is not saying that he no longer needed food; he is saying what truly sustained him. Moses wrote, and Jesus quoted in the wilderness: "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God." (Matthew 4:4) 'Him that sent me' is one of John's constant descriptions of the Father; the Son is the Sent One (John 3:17, 34), and he lives by his Father's will: "For I came down from heaven, not to do mine own will, but the will of him that sent me." (John 6:38)

'And to finish his work': *teleiōsō* (G5048, John 4:34, V-AAS-1S), to bring to completion. The STEPBible lexicon lists this verse beside John 17:4, where Jesus prays, "I have finished the work which thou gavest me to do" (John 17:4), and John 19:28, where the Scripture is 'fulfilled' just before he dies. The conversation at the well is part of a work that will end at the cross.

**Asks:** What feeds you? Jesus was nourished by doing his Father's will, even when tired and hungry. Ask him to make obedience your food, not a duty you swallow, but the thing that sustains you.

#### John 4:35–38 · Fields White Already to Harvest
**Says:** "Say not ye, There are yet four months, and then cometh harvest? behold, I say unto you, Lift up your eyes, and look on the fields; for they are white already to harvest." (John 4:35) "And he that reapeth receiveth wages, and gathereth fruit unto life eternal: that both he that soweth and he that reapeth may rejoice together." (John 4:36) "And herein is that saying true, One soweth, and another reapeth." (John 4:37) "I sent you to reap that whereon ye bestowed no labour: other men laboured, and ye are entered into their labours." (John 4:38) *Herein* means in this; *bestowed no labour* means did no work.

**Means:** 'There are yet four months, and then cometh harvest' may be a farming proverb, the usual gap between sowing and harvest, or it may be a remark about the actual season, which would put this scene in winter, about four months before the grain harvest. Readers differ; both fit. Either way, Jesus sets it against his own 'behold': 'Lift up your eyes', *eparate* (G1869, John 4:35, V-AAM-2P), 'and look on the fields; for they are white already'. White, *leukai* (G3022, John 4:35): ripe grain turns pale before harvest. Some have suggested that the Samaritans coming out across the fields in their white garments were in view as he spoke (John 4:30); that is an attractive guess, but only a guess. The point is plain: the harvest is not in four months; it is now.

In ordinary farming, reaping follows sowing after a long wait. In this harvest, Amos's promise is coming true: "the plowman shall overtake the reaper" (Amos 9:13). The reaper is already receiving 'wages', *misthon* (G3408, John 4:36), and gathering 'fruit unto life eternal', and sower and reaper 'rejoice together'. 'One soweth, and another reapeth' was a saying usually spoken with a sigh: someone else gets the benefit of my work. Jesus turns it into joy: in God's harvest nobody's labour is wasted, and nobody's reaping is his own achievement.

'I sent you', *apesteila* (G649, John 4:38, V-AAI-1S). The disciples are sent to reap 'that whereon ye bestowed no labour'. The verb for 'laboured' is *kekopiakasin* (G2872, John 4:38, V-RAI-3P), the same verb as 'wearied' in verse 6: Jesus wearied himself at the well, and the disciples will reap. Who are the 'other men'? Some say the prophets and John the Baptist, who prepared the way; others say Jesus himself, and the woman; others look ahead to the harvest in Samaria in Acts 8, where Philip sowed and Peter and John came later (Acts 8:5, 14). The saying is broad enough for all. Paul says the same of his own ministry: "I have planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase." (1 Corinthians 3:6)

**Asks:** Where have you been saying 'four months', meaning 'later', 'not yet', 'they are not ready'? Lift up your eyes. And if you reap, remember who sowed before you; if you sow and never see the harvest, remember that sower and reaper rejoice together.

#### John 4:39–42 · The Saviour of the World
**Says:** "And many of the Samaritans of that city believed on him for the saying of the woman, which testified, He told me all that ever I did." (John 4:39) "So when the Samaritans were come unto him, they besought him that he would tarry with them: and he abode there two days." (John 4:40) "And many more believed because of his own word;" (John 4:41) "And said unto the woman, Now we believe, not because of thy saying: for we have heard him ourselves, and know that this is indeed the Christ, the Saviour of the world." (John 4:42) *Besought* means begged; *tarry* and *abode* mean stay and stayed.

**Means:** The harvest comes in two waves. First, many believe 'for the saying of the woman', because she 'testified', *marturousēs* (G3140, John 4:39, V-PAP-GSF). John, the Gospel of witness, counts her among the witnesses: John the Baptist bore witness (John 1:7), and now a Samaritan woman does. The content of her witness is still 'He told me all that ever I did'.

The Samaritans ask him to 'tarry', *meinai* (G3306, John 4:40), to abide, the word John uses for staying with Jesus (John 1:39) and later for abiding in him (John 15:4). A Jewish teacher staying two days in a Samaritan town was itself a wonder. Then 'many more believed because of his own word', *logon* (G3056, John 4:41). They tell the woman that their faith now rests not on her 'saying', *lalian* (G2981, John 4:42), a different word: talk, report. The STEPBible lexicon gives 'talk, speech, conversation' for it. They are not belittling her; they are saying what every witness hopes to hear: we have met him ourselves.

Their confession is the high point of the chapter: 'this is indeed the Christ, the Saviour of the world'. 'Saviour', *sōtēr* (G4990, John 4:42), is used in Scripture of God (Luke 1:47) and of Christ (Luke 2:11). The STEPBible lexicon notes that it was also a frequent title of kings in the Ptolemaic and Roman periods; the emperor could be hailed as saviour. The phrase 'the Saviour of the world' comes 2 times in the New Testament: here, and in John's first letter: "the Father sent the Son to be the Saviour of the world" (1 John 4:14). In the mouths of Samaritans it means more than 'our Saviour' or even 'the Jews' Saviour': he is the Saviour not of one nation or one mountain but of the world God loved (John 3:16). That does not mean everyone in the world is saved regardless of faith; the Samaritans who say it are those who 'believed'. It means that no people are outside the reach of his saving work.

**Asks:** Has your faith moved from 'they told me' to 'I have heard him myself'? Thank God for those who first told you. Then go to his word yourself, and help others to do the same.

#### John 4:43–45 · A Prophet in His Own Country
**Says:** "Now after two days he departed thence, and went into Galilee." (John 4:43) "For Jesus himself testified, that a prophet hath no honour in his own country." (John 4:44) "Then when he was come into Galilee, the Galilaeans received him, having seen all the things that he did at Jerusalem at the feast: for they also went unto the feast." (John 4:45) *Thence* means from there.

**Means:** These verses are among the puzzles of the chapter. Jesus goes to Galilee, and John explains with 'For': Jesus had said 'that a prophet hath no honour in his own country', *patridi* (G3968, John 4:44). Then the Galileans 'received him'. Why would a saying about dishonour explain a welcome? The other Gospels place the saying at Nazareth, where the town rejected him (Matthew 13:57; Mark 6:4; Luke 4:24). Readers of John have answered in several ways. Some think 'his own country' here means Judaea, the homeland of the prophets and of the Messiah's lineage, where he had just met suspicion (John 4:1), so that he goes to Galilee for a hearing (Origen took this view, verify). Others think it means Galilee itself, and that the Galileans' welcome was the wrong kind of honour: they received him 'having seen all the things that he did at Jerusalem at the feast', the same sign-based enthusiasm to which Jesus did not commit himself in John 2:23–25. The next verses support that second reading: Jesus says in Galilee, 'Except ye see signs and wonders, ye will not believe' (John 4:48). (See the Council's Hard Questions.)

The 'feast' is the Passover of John 2:13–23. The contrast with Samaria is sharp: Samaritans believed with no sign at all, on the word of a woman and then on his word; Galileans welcome him because of what they saw.

**Asks:** Is your welcome of Jesus built on what he can do for you, or on who he is? It is possible to receive him warmly and still not honour him.

#### John 4:46–50 · Go Thy Way; Thy Son Liveth
**Says:** "So Jesus came again into Cana of Galilee, where he made the water wine. And there was a certain nobleman, whose son was sick at Capernaum." (John 4:46) "When he heard that Jesus was come out of Judaea into Galilee, he went unto him, and besought him that he would come down, and heal his son: for he was at the point of death." (John 4:47) "Then said Jesus unto him, Except ye see signs and wonders, ye will not believe." (John 4:48) "The nobleman saith unto him, Sir, come down ere my child die." (John 4:49) "Jesus saith unto him, Go thy way; thy son liveth. And the man believed the word that Jesus had spoken unto him, and he went his way." (John 4:50) *Nobleman* means a royal official; *ere* means before.

**Means:** John reminds us that we are back 'where he made the water wine'. The nobleman is a *basilikos* (G937, John 4:46); the STEPBible lexicon explains the word, used of a person, as 'one in the service of a king, a courtier', and lists this verse. In Galilee that meant the court of Herod Antipas, whom the people called king. Whether he was a Jew or a Gentile John does not say. His son lies sick at Capernaum, down by the lake, and the father has come up to Cana to find Jesus. He wants Jesus to 'come down', *katabē* (G2597, John 4:47), for the boy is 'at the point of death'.

Jesus' first answer sounds harsh: 'Except ye see signs and wonders, ye will not believe.' The 'ye' is plural. Jesus is speaking to the Galileans around the man, and to the man as one of them: a people who needed to see before they would believe. 'Signs and wonders', *sēmeia* (G4592, John 4:48) and *terata* (G5059, John 4:48): the word 'wonders' occurs nowhere else in John. John's own word for Jesus' miracles is 'signs', things that point beyond themselves; 'wonders' names the spectacle. Paul would say, "the Jews require a sign" (1 Corinthians 1:22).

The father does not argue. He simply asks again, more urgently, and more tenderly: 'Sir, come down ere my child die.' The word for 'child' is *paidion* (G3813, John 4:49), little boy. He still thinks Jesus must be there in person. Jesus neither goes nor refuses. He gives a command and a promise: 'Go thy way; thy son liveth.' 'Liveth' is *zē* (G2198, John 4:50, V-PAI-3S), present tense: he lives, now. The same verb gave us 'living water' in verse 10. And 'the man believed the word that Jesus had spoken unto him, and he went his way.' He had nothing to take home but a sentence.

This is often compared with the centurion's servant in Matthew 8:5–13 and Luke 7:1–10. Both are healings at a distance involving Capernaum and an official. But the differences are many: a nobleman and a son here, a centurion and a servant there; a request that Jesus 'come down' here, a centurion who says he is 'not worthy' for Jesus to come there; a rebuke of sign-seeking here, Jesus marvelling at great faith there. Most interpreters conclude that they are two different events (verify the views of those who identify them).

**Asks:** Can you take Jesus at his word when you cannot see the result? Faith is not a feeling of certainty; it is going your way on the strength of what he has said.

#### John 4:51–54 · The Same Hour
**Says:** "And as he was now going down, his servants met him, and told him, saying, Thy son liveth." (John 4:51) "Then enquired he of them the hour when he began to amend. And they said unto him, Yesterday at the seventh hour the fever left him." (John 4:52) "So the father knew that it was at the same hour, in the which Jesus said unto him, Thy son liveth: and himself believed, and his whole house." (John 4:53) "This is again the second miracle that Jesus did, when he was come out of Judaea into Galilee." (John 4:54) *Amend* means get better.

**Means:** On the road home, his servants meet him with the very words Jesus had used: 'Thy son liveth.' The word for son is now *pais* (G3816, John 4:51), boy. The father asks one question: at what hour did he begin to improve? 'Began to amend' translates *kompsoteron eschen*, *kompsoteron* (G2866, John 4:52): the STEPBible lexicon compares the colloquial English 'to be doing finely'. The answer: 'Yesterday at the seventh hour', about one in the afternoon, 'the fever', *puretos* (G4446, John 4:52), 'left him'. The fever did not linger and fade; it left.

'Yesterday' tells us that the man did not reach home the same day. Some suggest this shows the calm of his faith: he did not rush home to check. That may be reading in; the distance was long, and a traveller setting out in the afternoon might well stop for the night. What the text does show is his care to connect the healing with the word: 'the father knew that it was at the same hour'. Jesus' word worked at the moment he spoke it, without his presence, without a touch. The psalmist had said of God, "He sent his word, and healed them" (Psalm 107:20).

His faith grows through three stages in the story. It begins as the faith of a desperate man who asks Jesus to come (John 4:47). It becomes faith in the bare word, when he goes his way (John 4:50). And it ends as settled faith, 'himself believed', and it spreads: 'and his whole house', *oikia* (G3614, John 4:53). The same pattern of a household believing together appears in Acts, when the Philippian jailer rejoiced, "believing in God with all his house" (Acts 16:34).

John closes with a numbering: 'This is again the second miracle', *deuteron sēmeion*, *sēmeion* (G4592, John 4:54). He numbers only the first two signs, both at Cana (John 2:11; John 4:54). Between them lie Nicodemus, the Baptist's last witness, and the Samaritans, a journey from Cana to Cana that many call the Cana cycle. It began with water turned to wine at a wedding and ends with a dying boy given life, and in between it shows every kind of response to Jesus: the disciples who believed, the temple authorities who demanded a sign, the crowds who believed on signs, the teacher who came by night, the Samaritans who believed his word, and the official who believed before he saw. John tells us why he records the signs: "that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name" (John 20:31). In this sign, a word from the Son gives life.

**Asks:** Faith that begins in need can grow into faith that rests on his word and then into faith that fills a household. Where are you on that road? Take the next step: believe the word he has spoken, and let those in your house see what he does.

### 🧨 Cultural Landmines and Misconceptions
**Samaritans as 'nice outsiders'.** Modern readers know 'Samaritan' mainly from the parable of the Good Samaritan and use it to mean a kind stranger. To Jesus' hearers the word carried centuries of hostility, suspicion of false religion, and contempt. Jesus' opponents would one day throw it at him as an insult: "Say we not well that thou art a Samaritan, and hast a devil?" (John 8:48). Ezra's leaders had refused Samaritan help in building the temple (Ezra 4:3). To feel the force of John 4, think of the group your community most distrusts.

**The noon hour as proof of disgrace.** It is often said that the woman came at noon because the other women shunned her. That may be so, but John does not say it. Hold it as a possibility and do not preach it as fact; the text's emphasis is on Jesus' weariness and the heat, not on her motives.

**Five husbands as five false gods.** Some ancient and modern readers have taken her five husbands as a picture of the five nations settled in Samaria, each with its gods (2 Kings 17:24, 29–31), and the sixth, 'not thy husband', as the Samaritans' incomplete knowledge of the LORD. It is an ingenious reading, but the gods of 2 Kings 17 are more than five, and nothing in John 4 signals a symbol. Jesus is speaking about her life.

**'The Jews' in John.** John often uses 'the Jews' for the religious authorities in Judaea who opposed Jesus, and some have used such verses to justify contempt for Jewish people. John 4 is a strong corrective: Jesus himself says 'we know what we worship: for salvation is of the Jews' (John 4:22). The Saviour of the world was a Jew, and the gospel came 'to the Jew first' (Romans 1:16).

**The nobleman and the centurion as the same story.** Because both involve Capernaum and a healing at a distance, they are often blended in memory. Read them side by side (John 4:46–54; Matthew 8:5–13; Luke 7:1–10) and let each keep its own details.

**'Living water' as a religious phrase.** In the ancient Near East, 'living water' was an everyday term for running water, the best water, needed for some purifications (Leviticus 14:5 has it in the Hebrew, verify the phrasing there). The woman's misunderstanding is natural, not foolish. Jesus uses a plain phrase to open a spiritual door.

## 🔴 Tier 3 — Expert
### 🏗️ Theological Framework
John 4 sits where Christology, the doctrine of worship, and the history of redemption meet. Read through the Reformed conviction that Scripture interprets Scripture, the chapter answers three questions: who Jesus is, what worship God accepts, and how God's saving purpose, given through Israel, reaches the world.

*The person of Christ.* No chapter in John sets the two natures of Christ side by side more simply. The same person is 'wearied with his journey' and thirsty (John 4:6–7), and knows 'all things that ever I did' (John 4:29) of a stranger; he is hungry at noon, and he is sustained by the Father's will (John 4:34); he sits by a well in Samaria, and his word gives life to a boy in Capernaum (John 4:50). The church's confession at Chalcedon (451) summarized the biblical witness this way (paraphrased here, not quoted): one Lord Jesus Christ, truly God and truly man, in two natures, without confusion or change, without division or separation, the properties of each nature preserved and concurring in one person. John 4 gives that confession its narrative texture. The weariness is not play-acting (against Docetism, which made his body an appearance), and the knowledge is not a lucky guess (against every reduction of Jesus to a gifted teacher). He 'must needs' go through Samaria as a man walks a road, and as the Son sent on the Father's errand.

*Worship and the temple.* The question of the place of worship runs through the whole Old Testament: the tabernacle, the place the LORD would choose (Deuteronomy 12:5), Solomon's temple, the rival altars of the northern kingdom and later of Gerizim. John has already shown that Jesus' body is the true temple (John 2:19–21). In John 4 Jesus announces the consequence: the hour has come when the Father will be worshipped neither on Gerizim nor at Jerusalem, but 'in spirit and in truth'. That is not a move from outward worship to formless inwardness, but from shadow to substance: the Spirit whom the Son gives, and the truth that has come in the Son (John 1:17). The Reformed tradition has drawn from this and other texts the conviction that God is to be worshipped only as he has revealed, and that worship offered in ignorance of him, however sincere, is 'ye know not what' (John 4:22). That conviction, called the regulative principle, is argued in the Debate Corner.

*Particular and universal.* 'Salvation is of the Jews' (John 4:22) and 'the Saviour of the world' (John 4:42) frame the second half of the conversation. God's saving purpose was particular in its channel: Abraham, Israel, David, the prophets, and the Messiah according to the flesh (Romans 9:4–5). It is universal in its reach: the promise to Abraham was that in him all families of the earth would be blessed (Genesis 12:3), and the Servant would be 'a light to the Gentiles' (Isaiah 49:6). John 4 shows the particular becoming universal in one afternoon, and the Samaritans are the first fruits of what Acts 1:8 and Acts 8 will show on a larger scale. Covenant theology reads this as the one covenant of grace, administered through Israel under the law and now extended to the nations in Christ.

*The Spirit and the word.* John tells us later that the living water is the Spirit (John 7:38–39). John 4 shows the Spirit's work joined to the word: the woman is brought to faith through Jesus' words, the Samaritans 'because of his own word' (John 4:41), the nobleman when he 'believed the word' (John 4:50). The Reformed churches have held that the Spirit ordinarily works faith through the hearing of the word (Romans 10:17), and the Westminster Shorter Catechism (Q. 89, verify the number) teaches that the Spirit makes the reading, and especially the preaching, of the word an effectual means of convincing and converting sinners. John 4 is that doctrine in narrative form.

*Mission under sovereignty.* The fields are 'white already to harvest' (John 4:35). The harvest is God's: the Father seeks worshippers (John 4:23), the Son sends reapers (John 4:38), and sowers and reapers rejoice together because neither is the source of the crop (1 Corinthians 3:6–8). Reformed theology has always held together the sovereignty of God in salvation and the urgency of mission; John 4 shows why they are not in tension. Because the harvest is God's, it is ready now, and no labourer's work is wasted.

### 📚 Scholarly Insights
- **Origen**, *Commentary on the Gospel of John* (book 13, verify): the earliest surviving extended commentary on John 4. Origen argues at length with an earlier Gnostic interpreter, Heracleon, and he reads the chapter on two levels: the woman's thirst and the well stand for the soul's search in lesser teachings, and Jesus' water for the higher knowledge he gives. He also allegorizes the five husbands (as the five senses, on one of his readings, verify). His allegories are not to be followed, but his instinct that the chapter is about the soul's thirst for God is sound.
- **Augustine**, *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (Tractate 15, verify): reads Jesus' weariness as the weakness of the incarnate Word, strong in his divinity and weak for our sake, and sees the woman as a figure of the church gathered from the Gentiles. He also reads 'call thy husband' as a call to her understanding (verify), a reading few would follow today.
- **John Chrysostom**, *Homilies on the Gospel of John* (Homilies 31 to 35, verify): contrasts the woman with Nicodemus to her advantage. He praises the way she grows from calling Jesus a Jew to a prophet to the Christ, and her zeal in leaving her waterpot to become, in his view, something like an apostle to her town (verify).
- **John Calvin**, *Commentary on the Gospel According to John*: on John 4:23–24, argues that worship 'in spirit and in truth' means worship that is inward and freed from the shadows and figures of the law, not worship without any outward form; he warns both against empty ceremony and against invented worship (verify). On John 4:48 he sees Jesus rebuking a faith that wants to prescribe how God must act (verify).
- **J. C. Ryle**, *Expository Thoughts on the Gospels* (John): draws from the weary Christ at the well the comfort that our Saviour knows our bodily weakness, and from the woman's conversion that no one is beyond his reach; he also stresses the Lord's sharp dealing with sin, 'Go, call thy husband', as a mark of true kindness (verify).
- **B. F. Westcott**, *The Gospel According to St. John* (1880): argues that John counts the hours from midnight, as he takes Roman civil practice to have done, which would make the sixth hour six o'clock (verify). Most later commentators think John counts from sunrise.
- **D. A. Carson**, *The Gospel According to John* (Pillar New Testament Commentary, 1991): takes the living water as the Spirit's life given by Jesus, in line with John 7:38–39, and reads 'in spirit and in truth' as eschatological worship made possible by the Spirit and centred on Jesus, the truth, rather than a call to mere sincerity (verify).
- **Raymond E. Brown**, *The Gospel According to John* (Anchor Bible, 1966): notes the likely Samaritan hope of a prophet like Moses, and suggests that the chapter reflects the early church's mission among Samaritans; he also surveys the view that the five husbands are the five peoples of 2 Kings 17 and finds it doubtful (verify).
- **David Daube**, a 1950 article on the phrase in John 4:9 in the *Journal of Biblical Literature* (verify the title and year): argues that the phrase means Jews do not use vessels in common with Samaritans, which explains the woman's surprise that Jesus would drink from her jar.

### ⚔️ Debate Corner
**The question:** What does worship 'in spirit and in truth' (John 4:23–24) require of the church's worship?

**Position 1 · Sincerity from the heart is what matters; forms are indifferent.** Many Christians, from Pietist and revivalist traditions to much of today's evangelicalism, read Jesus' words as a turn from outward form to inward reality. Its holders stress that Jesus sets aside the question of the mountain, that God looks on the heart (1 Samuel 16:7), and that David prayed for "truth in the inward parts" (Psalm 51:6). On this reading, any form of worship, ancient or contemporary, liturgical or spontaneous, is acceptable if offered sincerely to the true God, and the church is free to adapt its worship to its culture. They are right that Jesus exposes worship tied to a place and empty of the heart.

**Position 2 · The normative principle: whatever Scripture does not forbid may be used, if it edifies and is done in order.** The Lutheran and Anglican traditions have held that Christ's words free the church from the ceremonial law and leave it at liberty to order its worship with prudence, keeping what Scripture commands and adding only what is edifying and does not contradict it. Its holders point to "Let all things be done decently and in order" (1 Corinthians 14:40) and to Christian liberty. The Thirty-Nine Articles (Article 20, verify) give the church authority to decree rites and ceremonies so long as they are not contrary to God's word. They would say that 'in spirit and in truth' requires worship that is true to the gospel and from the heart, not a particular list of elements.

**Position 3 · The regulative principle: God is to be worshipped only in the ways he has appointed.** The Reformed and Presbyterian churches have held that God, who alone knows how he is to be approached, has revealed in Scripture how he is to be worshipped, and that the church may not add to it. The Westminster Confession (chapter 21, section 1, verify) teaches that the acceptable way of worshipping God is instituted by himself and limited by his revealed will, so that he may not be worshipped according to human imaginations or devices. Its holders point to Moses' command, "thou shalt not add thereto, nor diminish from it" (Deuteronomy 12:32); to Nadab and Abihu, who "offered strange fire before the LORD, which he commanded them not" (Leviticus 10:1); to Jesus' rebuke, "But in vain they do worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men." (Matthew 15:9); and to Paul's warning against 'will worship' (Colossians 2:23). From John 4 they draw the word 'truth', and Jesus' verdict on sincere Samaritan worship: 'Ye worship ye know not what' (John 4:22).

**Most consistent with Reformed hermeneutics.** Scripture interpreting Scripture tells against Position 1 in its simple form: the Samaritans were sincere, and Jesus still said they did not know what they worshipped, and he joined 'truth' to 'spirit' as one requirement (John 4:23–24). Worship must be true to what God has revealed, not only heartfelt. Between Positions 2 and 3, the regulative principle best honours the whole biblical pattern, in which God repeatedly shows that he, not the worshipper, sets the terms of approach, and in which the new covenant, far from loosening that rule, fulfils the shadows in Christ and gives the church his appointed means: the word read and preached, prayer, praise, the sacraments. Two cautions keep this honest. First, John 4:23–24 does not by itself settle the list of elements; the principle rests on the whole of Scripture, and Christians who hold Position 2 read the same texts with care. Second, Jesus' 'in spirit' cuts against every formalism, including a Reformed formalism that keeps the right elements with a cold heart. The Reformed position is right only if the worship it regulates is alive with the Spirit whom the Son gives.

**Pastoral Warning:** Few things divide congregations as quickly as worship, and the 'worship wars' over music and form have emptied churches and embittered friends. Notice what Jesus did with the woman's worship question: he took it seriously, he did not pretend all answers were equal, and he refused to let the argument replace the worship. Teach this passage so that a congregation asks first whether the Father, who seeks worshippers, is finding them among us, and whether we worship him as he has revealed himself in his Son. A church can win every argument about the mountain and still be full of people who worship 'ye know not what'.

### 🔤 Original Language Deep Dive
Every Greek and Hebrew definition below comes from one source: the brief lexicon published by STEPBible.org (Tyndale House, Cambridge), whose Greek entries are drawn largely from G. Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament*. It is called 'the STEPBible lexicon' below.

**Spring and well.** John uses two words for Jacob's well. The narrator calls it *pēgē* (G4077, John 4:6, N-NSF), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'a spring, fountain', noting this verse as 'a well fed by a spring' and listing John 4:14 among its metaphorical uses. The woman calls it *phrear* (G5421, John 4:11, N-NSN), 'a well', a shaft dug in the ground; the lexicon notes that in the Greek Old Testament it chiefly renders the Hebrew *beer* (H875), 'well, pit, spring'. The site fits both: a deep shaft fed by groundwater. The literary effect is that Jesus, promising a *pēgē* in the believer (John 4:14), takes up the narrator's word, not the woman's. Some press the contrast too far, as if John meant 'stagnant cistern' by *phrear*; the lexicon does not support that, and the woman's well was not stagnant. The point is simply that Jesus offers a spring of his own.

**Living water.** *Hudōr zōn*, with *zōn* (G2198, John 4:10, V-PAP-ASN), a present active participle of the verb 'to live'. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 4:10–11 under the metaphorical use of *hudōr*, 'of divine truth and grace', with Revelation's 'water of life'. The Hebrew adjective *chay* (H2416) is given in the lexicon with the sense 'flowing, fresh (of water)' among others. So the phrase had a plain, everyday meaning, running water, and a prophetic one, God himself as 'the fountain of living waters' (Jeremiah 2:13). The noun there is *maqor* (H4726), which the lexicon glosses 'spring, fountain', and marks as a figure for the source of life, joy, and purification. John's own interpretation is decisive for the Christian reading: "(But this spake he of the Spirit, which they that believe on him should receive: for the Holy Ghost was not yet given; because that Jesus was not yet glorified.)" (John 7:39).

**'Shall never thirst'.** John 4:13 has the future indicative *dipsēsei* (G1372, John 4:13, V-FAI-3S): the drinker of the well 'will thirst' again. John 4:14 has *dipsēsē* (G1372, John 4:14, V-AAS-3S), an aorist subjunctive after the double negative *ou mē*, the strongest way Greek can deny a future possibility, strengthened by 'unto the age', *aiōna* (G165, John 4:14, N-ASM). The same construction comes in the bread of life discourse: "he that believeth on me shall never thirst" (John 6:35). The springing water is *hallomenou* (G242, John 4:14, V-PNP-GSN), a present participle, continuous: springing, leaping up. The STEPBible lexicon defines *hallomai* as 'to leap', with Acts 3:8 and Acts 14:10 for lame men leaping, and 'of water, to spring up' for this verse; the verb occurs 3 times in the New Testament. It would be a stretch to say that John 'means' the joy of a healed cripple here; the lexicon lists the water sense separately. But the word is vivid, and it pictures life that moves.

**Worship.** *Proskuneō* (G4352, John 4:20, V-AAI-3P) appears 10 times in John, 8 of them in John 4:20–24 alone. The STEPBible lexicon defines it as 'to make obeisance, do reverence to, worship', and mentions the older derivation from a verb meaning 'to kiss'. That derivation tells us something about the word's history (a gesture of homage), but meaning is settled by use, not by roots: in John 4 it means worship offered to God. John 4:21 and John 4:23 have the verb with the dative ('worship the Father'), a construction the lexicon discusses under its uses with God and Christ. The noun *proskunētēs* (G4353, John 4:23, N-NPM), 'worshipper', occurs only here in the New Testament. When the healed blind man later 'worshipped him' (John 9:38), the same verb is used of worship given to Jesus.

**'God is a Spirit'.** In *pneuma ho theos*, *pneuma* (G4151, John 4:24, N-NSN) stands first and without the article, *theos* (G2316, John 4:24) has the article: 'God', the subject, 'is spirit', the predicate. Greek has no indefinite article, so English must decide between 'God is a Spirit' (KJV) and 'God is spirit'. The construction describes God's nature, as 'God is light' (1 John 1:5) and 'God is love' (1 John 4:8) do; it does not make God one spirit among many. The following phrase has one preposition governing both nouns, *en pneumati kai alētheia*, with *alētheia* (G225, John 4:24, N-DSF): a single manner of worship with two aspects. 'Must' is *dei* (G1163, John 4:24, V-PAI-3S).

**'I am he'.** In John 4:26 Jesus says *egō eimi*, with *egō* (G1473, John 4:26, P-1NS) and *eimi* (G1510, John 4:26, V-PAI-1S), followed by 'the one speaking to you'. The KJV supplies 'he', correctly: the predicate is understood from the woman's words about the Messiah. John uses the same two words with no predicate at all in the great sayings of John 8:24, 58 and John 18:5–6, where they echo God's name in Exodus 3:14. Whether John 4:26 carries that echo is debated. The safe claim is that it is the first *egō eimi* on Jesus' lips in John, that it is a claim to be the Messiah, and that the later sayings will show the Messiah to be more than the woman imagined.

**Two words for what was said.** The Samaritans believed 'because of his own word', *logon* (G3056, John 4:41, N-ASM), and told the woman that their faith no longer rested on her 'saying', *lalian* (G2981, John 4:42, N-ASF). The STEPBible lexicon gives *lalia* the senses 'talk, speech, conversation'. The change of word is real, but it should not be made into a slight; the same townsfolk first believed through her (John 4:39), where John uses *logon* (G3056, John 4:39) of her testimony. The contrast in verse 42 is between hearing about him and hearing him.

**Labour and finishing.** *Kopiaō* is used twice in this chapter: of Jesus, 'wearied', *kekopiakōs* (G2872, John 4:6, V-RAP-NSM), and of the sowers, 'laboured', *kekopiakasin* (G2872, John 4:38, V-RAI-3P). The STEPBible lexicon gives both senses, 'to grow weary' (listing John 4:6) and 'to work with effort, to toil' (listing John 4:38). The weariness of the Lord at the well is part of the labour into which the disciples enter. *Teleioō*, 'to finish', *teleiōsō* (G5048, John 4:34, V-AAS-1S), is listed by the lexicon under 'bring to an end, finish, accomplish', with John 17:4 and John 19:28.

**The official and his son.** *Basilikos* (G937, John 4:46, A-NSM) is an adjective, 'royal'; used of a person, the STEPBible lexicon explains, 'one in the service of a king, a courtier', and it lists John 4:46 and John 4:49. The boy is called *huios* (G5207, John 4:46), son; *paidion* (G3813, John 4:49), little child, on his father's lips; and *pais* (G3816, John 4:51), boy, on the servants'. 'Began to amend' is *kompsoteron* (G2866, John 4:52, A-ASN-C) with 'had': the lexicon compares the colloquial English 'to be doing finely'. 'Wonders' is *terata* (G5059, John 4:48, N-APN), which the lexicon notes is always plural in the New Testament and paired with 'signs'.

**Saviour.** *Sōtēr* (G4990, John 4:42, N-NSM) is defined by the STEPBible lexicon as 'saviour, deliverer, preserver', and it notes the word's frequent use as an epithet of kings in the Ptolemaic and Roman periods. In the Greek Old Testament it renders forms of the Hebrew verb *yasah* (H3467), 'to save, be delivered', and it is used of God (Luke 1:47; 1 Timothy 2:3) and of Christ (Luke 2:11). The phrase *ho sōtēr tou kosmou* joins it with *kosmou* (G2889, John 4:42, N-GSM), the world God loved (John 3:16).

**Textual notes.** Two small variants touch this chapter. In John 4:1 the KJV, with the Textus Receptus and many manuscripts, reads 'the Lord knew'; several early manuscripts read 'Jesus knew', and many modern editions follow them (verify the manuscripts). In John 4:42 the KJV reads 'the Christ, the Saviour of the world'; several early manuscripts lack 'the Christ', and many modern editions omit the words (verify). Neither variant changes the teaching of the passage: the woman has already asked whether he is the Christ (John 4:29), and Jesus has said he is (John 4:26). This study teaches the text the KJV prints, and notes the difference without scorn for either side.

### 🚷 Preaching Mistakes to Avoid
- **Information without life:** a sermon rich in Samaritan history, Gerizim, and the Greek of *pēgē* that never offers the living water to the thirsty people in the room, or asks them which well they keep going back to.
- **Heat without light:** a sermon that makes the woman's five husbands its centre, thunders against immorality, and never reaches the gift of God, the Messiah revealed, or the Father who seeks worshippers.
- **Mockery of the woman:** jokes about 'five husbands and counting' may get a laugh, but they wound the widowed and divorced in the pews and contradict the way Jesus spoke to her. He named her history once, privately, and without a sneer.
- **Evangelism as technique:** turning John 4 into a five-step method (ask for water, pivot to spiritual things, expose sin, handle objections, call for decision) forgets that it was the Father seeking and the Son speaking. Jesus' approach is a model of love, not a script.
- **Universalism from one phrase:** reading 'the Saviour of the world' (John 4:42) as a promise that everyone is saved regardless of faith. The Samaritans who confess it are those who 'believed' (John 4:39, 41).
- **Despising all prayer for healing:** using 'Except ye see signs and wonders' (John 4:48) to shame people who ask God for a sick child's life. Jesus rebuked sign-hunting, and then he healed the boy.

## 🟣 Tier 4 — Christocentric, Humility, Life & Light
### 👑 Every Answer in Jesus
John 4 demolishes every ground of self-glory. Religious pedigree is no help: the Jews have the true revelation, but salvation is of the Jews only because the Saviour is. Heritage is no help: 'our father Jacob' gave a well, but no one who drank from it stopped thirsting. Sincerity is no help on its own: the Samaritans worshipped sincerely, and did not know what they worshipped. Moral record is no help: the woman's history was known to the one she spoke with. Rank is no help: the royal official had to walk home with nothing but a word. Even the disciples, who had left everything to follow Jesus, came back from town with food and missed the harvest. Every human being in the chapter is shown to be thirsty, ignorant, or dependent, and the only one who gives anything is Jesus.

And every good thing the chapter holds is found in him. The gift of God is bound up with 'who it is that saith to thee' (John 4:10). The living water is the water that 'I shall give' (John 4:14). The knowledge of the Father comes from the one who says 'we know what we worship' (John 4:22) and then reveals the Father who seeks. The Messiah the woman waited for is speaking to her (John 4:26). The Saviour of the world stays two days in a Samaritan town (John 4:40, 42). The word that gives life to a dying boy is his (John 4:50). This is what Paul means when he says of Christ, "In whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." (Colossians 2:3) The chapter humbles us by showing that we come to Jesus with nothing but an empty waterpot, and it makes Christ glorious by showing that he gives a spring.

## 📚 Doctrine
### Theology Proper · God Is Spirit, and the Father Seeks Worshippers
**Proposition:** God is spirit: not a body, not confined to any place, not contained by any mountain or temple. The Father is not a distant being waiting to be found by the diligent; he is actively seeking true worshippers, and he makes himself known in his Son so that he may be worshipped as he truly is.

**Textual Anchor:** "But the hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him." (John 4:23) "God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth." (John 4:24) The verb *zētei* (G2212, John 4:23, V-PAI-3S) is present: the Father is seeking.

**Cross-References:** "But will God indeed dwell on the earth? behold, the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain thee; how much less this house that I have builded?" (1 Kings 8:27); "God that made the world and all things therein, seeing that he is Lord of heaven and earth, dwelleth not in temples made with hands;" (Acts 17:24); "For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost." (Luke 19:10)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *God is located in, or bound to, a sacred place.* Both Samaritans and Jews of Jesus' day could speak as though the right mountain guaranteed access to God; some religious practice today treats buildings, shrines, or lands the same way. Jesus says the hour has come when neither Gerizim nor Jerusalem is the place (John 4:21). (2) *God is a material or embodied being.* The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints teaches that the Father has a body of flesh and bones; its members would point to passages where God is described with hands or a face. Jesus says 'God is a Spirit' (John 4:24), and Scripture's bodily language about God is the language of accommodation. (3) *God is passive, and religion is our search for him.* Many describe the world's religions as human quests for the divine. John 4 reverses the direction: the Father 'seeketh' (John 4:23), and the Son came to a well to find a worshipper.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Freedom to worship anywhere: in a kitchen, a hospital, a prison, a field. Reverence, because the God we worship is not ours to contain. And comfort, because the Father is looking for us before we look for him.

### Christology · The Messiah Revealed, the Saviour of the World
**Proposition:** Jesus of Nazareth is the promised Messiah, the Christ, who revealed himself as such to a Samaritan woman, and he is the Saviour of the world: the one Saviour, sent by the Father, whose saving work reaches beyond Israel to every nation.

**Textual Anchor:** "Jesus saith unto her, I that speak unto thee am he." (John 4:26), with *egō* (G1473, John 4:26) and *eimi* (G1510, John 4:26); and the Samaritans' confession: "this is indeed the Christ, the Saviour of the world" (John 4:42), where 'Saviour' is *sōtēr* (G4990, John 4:42).

**Cross-References:** "And we have seen and do testify that the Father sent the Son to be the Saviour of the world." (1 John 4:14); "For unto you is born this day in the city of David a Saviour, which is Christ the Lord." (Luke 2:11); "I will also give thee for a light to the Gentiles, that thou mayest be my salvation unto the end of the earth." (Isaiah 49:6)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus never claimed to be the Messiah; the church made the claim for him.* Some modern scholars hold that the historical Jesus was a teacher or prophet and that messianic titles were added later. John 4:26 records Jesus' own claim, plainly spoken, and it fits the whole Gospel's witness (John 20:31). (2) *Jesus is a saviour for some peoples, and other religions are saviours for others.* Religious pluralism, held with sincerity by many, sees Christ as one path among many. The Samaritans, who had their own mountain and tradition, confessed him as the Saviour of 'the world', not of Jews only, and Jesus himself told them, 'Ye worship ye know not what' (John 4:22). (3) *Because he is the Saviour of the world, all people are saved whether they believe or not.* Universalists read the title as a guarantee. The Samaritans who say it are those who 'believed' (John 4:41–42), and the chapter's whole movement is toward believing his word.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Confidence in evangelism: no one lives where the Saviour's name does not reach. Humility: the first town in John to confess him as Saviour of the world was a Samaritan one. And worship, because the one who speaks to us is the Christ.

### Christology · Wearied at the Well: True Man and All-Knowing Lord
**Proposition:** The eternal Son became truly man, able to be tired, thirsty, and hungry, and remained truly God, knowing the hidden life of a stranger and giving life by his word at a distance. The two natures are united in one person without confusion and without division.

**Textual Anchor:** "Jesus therefore, being wearied with his journey, sat thus on the well" (John 4:6), with *kekopiakōs* (G2872, John 4:6, V-RAP-NSM); "Come, see a man, which told me all things that ever I did" (John 4:29); "Go thy way; thy son liveth" (John 4:50).

**Cross-References:** "Wherefore in all things it behoved him to be made like unto his brethren, that he might be a merciful and faithful high priest in things pertaining to God, to make reconciliation for the sins of the people." (Hebrews 2:17); "Neither is there any creature that is not manifest in his sight: but all things are naked and opened unto the eyes of him with whom we have to do." (Hebrews 4:13)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Docetism: Jesus only seemed to be human.* Some early teachers, whom John's first letter opposes (1 John 4:2–3), held that the divine Christ could not truly suffer or tire. John shows him wearied and thirsty at noon (John 4:6–7). (2) *Jesus was a man with unusual insight, not God.* Many admire him as a gifted reader of people. But his knowledge of the woman's five husbands was not a guess, and his word healed a boy in another town at the very hour he spoke (John 4:53). (3) *The kenotic view that the Son laid aside his divine attributes to become man.* Some nineteenth-century theologians, such as Gottfried Thomasius (verify), taught that the Son gave up attributes like omniscience in the incarnation; its holders want to take his real humanity seriously. John 4 shows the same person both tired and all-knowing, which fits the church's confession that he took humanity to himself without ceasing to be what he was.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Comfort for the weary: our Lord knows tiredness from the inside. Honesty before him: nothing about us surprises him. And worship, because the one who sat on the well is the one whose word gives life.

### Pneumatology · Living Water: The Spirit Given by the Son
**Proposition:** The living water Jesus promises is the gift of the Holy Spirit, given by the Son to all who ask and believe, dwelling within the believer as a spring, and rising into everlasting life. The Spirit's coming marks the new hour of worship 'in spirit and in truth'.

**Textual Anchor:** "If thou knewest the gift of God, and who it is that saith to thee, Give me to drink; thou wouldest have asked of him, and he would have given thee living water." (John 4:10) "the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life" (John 4:14). The gift is *dōrea* (G1431, John 4:10), the word Acts 2:38 uses for 'the gift of the Holy Ghost'.

**Cross-References:** "(But this spake he of the Spirit, which they that believe on him should receive: for the Holy Ghost was not yet given; because that Jesus was not yet glorified.)" (John 7:39); "For I will pour water upon him that is thirsty, and floods upon the dry ground: I will pour my spirit upon thy seed, and my blessing upon thine offspring:" (Isaiah 44:3); "and have been all made to drink into one Spirit." (1 Corinthians 12:13)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The Spirit is a later gift for an elite, after salvation.* Some teach that believers receive the Spirit in a second experience subsequent to conversion. Jesus offers the living water as the gift that ends the thirst of a sinner who asks, and Paul says all believers 'have been all made to drink into one Spirit' (1 Corinthians 12:13). Classical Pentecostal Christians would reply that they distinguish the Spirit's indwelling at conversion from a later empowering; the text here speaks of the first, and that is enough to show that no believer is without the Spirit. (2) *The Spirit is an impersonal force.* The Watchtower Society describes the holy spirit as God's active force. John 4 uses the picture of water, but John 7:39 names the Holy Ghost, and Jesus later speaks of the Spirit as one who teaches and testifies (John 14:26; John 15:26). (3) *The living water is merely good teaching.* Some reduce Jesus' offer to his moral wisdom. The water becomes a spring 'in him' (John 4:14), which describes a life given within, not only instruction received.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Assurance that the Spirit is not rationed to the spiritually advanced. Dependence: the spring is his, not ours. And joy, because the water 'springing up' is life that moves, not a stagnant religion.

### Anthropology & Sin · The Thirst That No Well Can Quench
**Proposition:** Every human being was made for God and is thirsty without him. Sin turns that thirst toward created things that cannot satisfy it, so that people go back again and again to the same wells, and it leads them to hide from the God who knows them, even behind religious argument.

**Textual Anchor:** "Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again" (John 4:13), the future *dipsēsei* (G1372, John 4:13, V-FAI-3S); "For thou hast had five husbands; and he whom thou now hast is not thy husband" (John 4:18); "Ye worship ye know not what" (John 4:22).

**Cross-References:** "For my people have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water." (Jeremiah 2:13); "Wherefore do ye spend money for that which is not bread? and your labour for that which satisfieth not?" (Isaiah 55:2); "For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God;" (Romans 3:23)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *People are basically content, and religion only invents a need.* Many secular people would say that human beings can be fully satisfied without God. Jesus' words, 'shall thirst again', describe what every human life shows: satisfaction does not last. (2) *The problem is only ignorance, not sin.* Some treat human need as a lack of information. The woman needed more than knowledge; Jesus named a life, and Jeremiah names 'two evils', forsaking God and digging broken cisterns. (3) *Religious people are exempt.* The Samaritans were devout, and their worship was 'ye know not what'; the disciples were followers of Jesus and still missed what he was doing. The thirst and the blindness run through religious and irreligious alike.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Compassion without contempt: the person on their fifth empty well is thirsty, as we were. Honesty about our own cisterns. And a clear direction for every thirst: to the fountain himself.

### Soteriology & Grace · The Gift of God, Promised Through Israel, Offered to All
**Proposition:** Salvation is the free gift of God, not drawn up by human effort or bought by merit. It came through Israel, since the Messiah is a Jew, and it is offered freely to every thirsty person of every nation, to be received simply by asking the one who gives it.

**Textual Anchor:** "If thou knewest the gift of God... thou wouldest have asked of him, and he would have given thee living water." (John 4:10); "for salvation is of the Jews" (John 4:22), *sōtēria* (G4991, John 4:22); "the Saviour of the world" (John 4:42).

**Cross-References:** "For the wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord." (Romans 6:23); "Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters, and he that hath no money; come ye, buy, and eat; yea, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price." (Isaiah 55:1); "And whosoever will, let him take the water of life freely." (Revelation 22:17)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Salvation is earned by religious effort or merit.* Many systems, and many hearts, hold that God rewards those who try hardest. The woman had 'nothing to draw with' (John 4:11), and Jesus did not ask her to find a bucket; he told her to ask. (2) *Salvation bypasses Israel, and the Old Testament is irrelevant.* Marcion in the second century rejected the Old Testament, and some Christians still treat it as a discarded first draft. Jesus says 'salvation is of the Jews' (John 4:22), and the Messiah the woman waited for is Israel's Messiah. (3) *Salvation is for Israel only, or for one nation or group.* Some in Jesus' day, and ethnic or national religion in every age, have assumed God's favour follows their people. The first town in John to confess him as Saviour was Samaritan.

**Pastoral Fruit:** The freedom of a gift. Gratitude to Israel and love for the Jewish people, through whom the Saviour came. And boldness to offer Christ to anyone, because the invitation says 'whosoever will'.

### Soteriology & Grace · Faith That Takes Jesus at His Word
**Proposition:** Saving faith rests on the word of Christ. It may begin with another person's testimony or with a desperate need, but it grows into trust in what Jesus himself has said, apart from sight, and it is confirmed as he keeps his word.

**Textual Anchor:** "And many more believed because of his own word;" (John 4:41); "for we have heard him ourselves" (John 4:42); "And the man believed the word that Jesus had spoken unto him, and he went his way." (John 4:50), with *episteusen* (G4100, John 4:50) and *logō* (G3056, John 4:50).

**Cross-References:** "So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God." (Romans 10:17); "Jesus saith unto him, Thomas, because thou hast seen me, thou hast believed: blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed." (John 20:29); "(For we walk by faith, not by sight:)" (2 Corinthians 5:7)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Faith must be based on signs and wonders.* Some ministries promise that miracles will produce faith, and some seekers say they will believe when they see. Jesus rebuked that demand (John 4:48), and the Samaritans believed with no sign at all. (2) *Faith is a leap without reasons.* Many sceptics describe faith as believing without evidence. The nobleman's faith rested on a word from a person, and it was confirmed by what happened 'at the same hour' (John 4:53); faith trusts a trustworthy word. (3) *Secondhand faith is enough.* Some rely on a parent's or a church's faith. The Samaritans moved from 'thy saying' to 'we have heard him ourselves' (John 4:42).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Stability in suffering, when we cannot see the outcome. Hunger for Scripture, where his word is heard. And patience with young faith, which often begins in need and grows.

### Ecclesiology · One People of True Worshippers from Every Nation
**Proposition:** In the hour that has come, the Father gathers one people of true worshippers, no longer defined by a mountain, a city, or an ethnic line, but by worship of the Father in spirit and in truth through the Son. The walls between Jew and Samaritan, and every such wall, are broken down in him.

**Textual Anchor:** "the hour cometh, when ye shall neither in this mountain, nor yet at Jerusalem, worship the Father" (John 4:21); "the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth" (John 4:23), with *proskunētai* (G4353, John 4:23); a Jewish Messiah staying two days with Samaritans (John 4:40).

**Cross-References:** "And other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring, and they shall hear my voice; and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd." (John 10:16); "For he is our peace, who hath made both one, and hath broken down the middle wall of partition between us;" (Ephesians 2:14); "In whom ye also are builded together for an habitation of God through the Spirit." (Ephesians 2:22)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The church is defined by a holy place or a single ethnic or national identity.* Churches in many ages have bound Christian identity to a nation or a people, and their members have often meant it as faithfulness to their heritage. Jesus says worship is no longer bound to Gerizim or Jerusalem (John 4:21). (2) *Christians can keep 'no dealings' with believers of other backgrounds.* Segregated churches, and quieter habits of avoidance, contradict a Lord who drank from a Samaritan's jar and stayed in a Samaritan town. (3) *The church is only an invisible fellowship of individuals with no gathered worship.* Some say that since God is spirit, meeting together does not matter. The 'true worshippers' are a plural people, and the Spirit builds them 'together' (Ephesians 2:22).

**Pastoral Fruit:** A congregation that welcomes the stranger as a fellow worshipper. Repentance for prejudice. And joy in a church that looks, even now, a little like the multitude of every nation before the throne.

### Eschatology · The Hour Cometh, and Now Is; the Harvest Unto Life Eternal
**Proposition:** The age to come has broken into the present in Jesus. The hour of true worship 'now is', the fields are already white, and the reaper is already gathering 'fruit unto life eternal', while the final harvest and the full gathering of God's people are still to come.

**Textual Anchor:** "But the hour cometh, and now is" (John 4:23); "Lift up your eyes, and look on the fields; for they are white already to harvest." (John 4:35); "And he that reapeth receiveth wages, and gathereth fruit unto life eternal: that both he that soweth and he that reapeth may rejoice together." (John 4:36)

**Cross-References:** "Verily, verily, I say unto you, The hour is coming, and now is, when the dead shall hear the voice of the Son of God: and they that hear shall live." (John 5:25); "Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that the plowman shall overtake the reaper" (Amos 9:13); "Thrust in thy sickle, and reap: for the time is come for thee to reap; for the harvest of the earth is ripe." (Revelation 14:15)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *God's kingdom is only future, so the church waits rather than reaps.* Some forms of piety, and some end-times teaching, treat the present age as a waiting room. Jesus says the hour 'now is' and the fields are white 'already'. (2) *The kingdom is only present, and there is no final harvest.* Some modern readings of John, associated with Rudolf Bultmann (verify), keep the present decision and drop the future consummation. John keeps both: the hour 'cometh, and now is', and the resurrection is still ahead (John 5:28–29). (3) *The harvest can wait for a better season.* Christians often say 'not yet' about evangelism, waiting for the right time. Jesus answers the proverb of four months with 'behold'.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Urgency without panic: the harvest is now, and it is God's. Hope: every sheaf gathered is fruit 'unto life eternal'. And joy shared between sowers and reapers, who will rejoice together at the end.

### Christian Walk · My Meat Is to Do His Will: Sowing, Reaping, and Witness
**Proposition:** The believer, like his Lord, is sustained by doing the will of the one who sent him. Every disciple is sent into God's harvest, some to sow and some to reap, none as the source of the crop, and each is called to bear witness to what Christ has done, pointing others to hear him for themselves.

**Textual Anchor:** "Jesus saith unto them, My meat is to do the will of him that sent me, and to finish his work." (John 4:34); "I sent you to reap that whereon ye bestowed no labour: other men laboured, and ye are entered into their labours." (John 4:38); the woman 'which testified' (John 4:39), *marturousēs* (G3140, John 4:39, V-PAP-GSF).

**Cross-References:** "Then saith he unto his disciples, The harvest truly is plenteous, but the labourers are few;" (Matthew 9:37); "Now he that planteth and he that watereth are one: and every man shall receive his own reward according to his own labour." (1 Corinthians 3:8); "And let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not." (Galatians 6:9)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Evangelism is only for specialists.* Many believers assume that witness belongs to pastors and trained evangelists. The first witness to a Samaritan town was a woman with a broken history and a few hours' knowledge of Jesus. (2) *Success in ministry is measured by what I reap.* Ministry culture can prize the reaper and forget the sower. Jesus says 'One soweth, and another reapeth', and both rejoice together (John 4:36–37). (3) *Obedience is a burden that drains us.* Some imagine the Christian life as duty endured. Jesus called the Father's will his food (John 4:34), the thing that fed him when he was tired.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Every member a witness. Contentment for the faithful sower who never sees fruit. And a way to be fed when weary: doing the next thing the Father gives.

### Assumed Here
- **Baptism:** The chapter mentions baptism only in passing (John 4:1–2), assuming a practice carried out by the disciples under Jesus' authority; the meaning of Christian baptism is taught plainly in Matthew 28:19, Romans 6:3–4, and Titus 3:5. Every doctrinal category is treated in a block above.

## ⚖️ The Council's Hard Questions
### ❓ Question 1 · Who Was the Woman at the Well, and What Did Jesus Expose
**Moderator:** The woman has been preached for centuries as a notorious sinner, an outcast who came to the well at noon to avoid the other women. Others now read her as a victim of men who divorced her. What does the text actually say about her, and what did Jesus expose when he said 'Go, call thy husband'?

**Historian:** Start with the facts John gives: five husbands, and a man now who is not her husband (John 4:18). In that world a woman could be widowed more than once; life expectancy was short. Divorce was generally in the husband's hands, as Deuteronomy 24:1 assumes, though Jewish sources debate the grounds, and we know little about Samaritan practice (verify). So some of her marriages may have ended by death, some by divorce, and we do not know which. The noon hour is suggestive, but people did draw water at noon, and John gives no motive.

**Philologist:** And the words Jesus uses are notable for what they lack. He says 'Thou hast well said' and 'in that saidst thou truly', *alēthes* (G227, John 4:18). There is no word for adultery, no word of condemnation. At the same time, 'he whom thou now hast is not thy husband' draws a clear line: Jesus does not call the present arrangement a marriage.

**Pastor:** So we should not preach her as a byword, and we should not preach her as blameless. The text shows a woman whose life has been a series of wells that ran dry, whatever mixture of her own sin and others' sin that involved, and a Lord who knew all of it. What Jesus exposed was not first a moral record but a thirst. He named her life so that she would know her need of the water, and so that she would know he knew and was still offering it.

**Church Historian:** The early church mostly praised her. Chrysostom (verify) admired her progress from 'a Jew' to 'a prophet' to 'the Christ', and her readiness to tell others. The picture of her as an especially scandalous woman grew later, often by blending her with other women in the Gospels. Some interpreters since have gone to the other extreme and treated the husbands as a symbol only. Both moves lose the woman John actually describes.

**Resolution:** The text states that the woman had had five husbands and was living with a man who was not her husband, and it gives no causes. Some losses may have been done to her; the present relationship Jesus distinguishes from marriage. What the passage stresses is that Jesus knew her life completely, named it privately and without contempt, commended her honesty, and offered her the gift of God in the same conversation. She became the first witness in her town: "Come, see a man, which told me all things that ever I did: is not this the Christ?" (John 4:29)

### ❓ Question 2 · What Is the Living Water
**Moderator:** Jesus offers 'living water' (John 4:10) that becomes 'a well of water springing up into everlasting life' (John 4:14). Is the living water the Holy Spirit, eternal life itself, or Jesus' teaching? And does it matter?

**Philologist:** The phrase meant running water in ordinary speech. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 4:10–11 under a metaphorical use, 'of divine truth and grace'. In the Old Testament the image points to God himself, 'the fountain of living waters' (Jeremiah 2:13), and to his Spirit poured out: "I will pour water upon him that is thirsty... I will pour my spirit upon thy seed" (Isaiah 44:3).

**Literary Scholar:** And John tells us how to read his water language. In John 7 Jesus cries, "If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink." (John 7:37), and the narrator explains that "this spake he of the Spirit" (John 7:39). John 4 and John 7 are written to be read together; the promise at the well and the cry at the feast are the same offer. Water in John also connects to cleansing (John 2:6; John 3:5) and to life (John 4:14). One symbol gathers several truths.

**Theologian:** So the answer is not either-or. The water is the life of God, eternal life, given by the Son and made ours by the Spirit who dwells within. The Reformed tradition, reading Scripture by Scripture, takes John 7:39 as the key: the living water is the Holy Spirit, who unites us to Christ and in whom eternal life springs up. Teaching is part of it, because the Spirit works through Christ's word (John 4:41; John 6:63), but the water is more than information; it becomes a spring 'in him'.

**Church Historian:** That has been the church's main reading from early on, though not the only one. Some early writers connected the water with baptism, as they did the water of John 3:5 (verify particular writers); Origen (verify) read it as the deeper knowledge Christ gives. The Spirit reading has been the common one among Protestant commentators, following John's own explanation.

**Resolution:** The living water is the life of God that Christ gives, and John himself identifies it with the Spirit whom believers receive (John 7:38–39). It is eternal life in its source (the Son), its agent (the Spirit), and its end ('into everlasting life'), and it comes through faith in Christ's word. It matters because it means the gift is not a program or a teaching to be mastered, but God himself dwelling in the believer: "and have been all made to drink into one Spirit" (1 Corinthians 12:13).

### ❓ Question 3 · His Own Country, and the Faith That Wants Signs
**Moderator:** John 4:44 says Jesus went to Galilee because 'a prophet hath no honour in his own country', and then says the Galileans received him. Which country is 'his own', and why is Jesus so sharp with a desperate father: 'Except ye see signs and wonders, ye will not believe' (John 4:48)?

**Historian:** The other Gospels use the saying of Nazareth (Matthew 13:57; Mark 6:4; Luke 4:24), which is in Galilee. But John has told us that Jesus was born of David's line and some expected the Messiah from Bethlehem in Judaea (John 7:42), and his ministry so far has centred on Jerusalem. So readers have proposed both. Origen (verify) and some modern scholars take 'his own country' as Judaea, where he has not been honoured, so he goes to Galilee. Others take it as Galilee, and read the welcome as shallow.

**Literary Scholar:** The flow of the passage favours the second reading, in my view. John says the Galileans received him 'having seen all the things that he did at Jerusalem at the feast' (John 4:45). That links them directly to the sign-believers of John 2:23–25, to whom Jesus 'did not commit himself'. Then comes John 4:48, spoken in the plural. The welcome is real but it is the wrong kind of honour: honour for a wonder-worker, not for the Word. And it stands in deliberate contrast to the Samaritans, who believed with no sign.

**Theologian:** Either way the theological point is the same: Jesus is not honoured by enthusiasm for his works that has no faith in his word. The rebuke in John 4:48 is not coldness toward the father's need. It is a test, and the man passes it. He asks again, and when Jesus gives him only a word, he believes the word and goes. Jesus rebukes sign-hunger and then gives a sign, but in a form that requires faith before sight.

**Teacher:** For our group this is the practical crux. Many of them have prayed for a sick child or parent. They need to hear that Jesus did not despise the father's request; he healed the boy. What he refused was a faith that says 'come and do it my way, where I can see it'. I would ask them to put the nobleman's two prayers side by side: 'Sir, come down' and then going home on a word.

**Resolution:** 'His own country' in John 4:44 may mean Judaea or Galilee, and faithful readers take both views; the context of John 4:45 and John 4:48 makes the Galilee reading, with a welcome based on signs, somewhat more likely. The rebuke of John 4:48 is aimed at a faith that insists on seeing, and the nobleman's story shows it overcome: "And the man believed the word that Jesus had spoken unto him, and he went his way." (John 4:50) John writes his Gospel for readers who will not see, "that ye might believe" (John 20:31), and Jesus pronounces them blessed (John 20:29).

### 🔦 The Life & Light Audit
**Pastor:** If a hearer obeyed this lesson exactly, they would, tonight, name to the Lord the well they keep returning to and ask him plainly for the living water. Tomorrow they would ask a small favour of, or start a real conversation with, someone from a group they usually avoid, and one person who is running from well to well would receive John 4:14 with a line of personal testimony. The dark place lit would be the one subject they least want Christ to name, brought to him in plain words and agreed with, as the woman agreed; and where a habit of changing the subject hides a struggle, one trusted believer would hear the truth.

**Theologian:** The practices keep source discipline. None asks the hearer to produce satisfaction, change, or light by effort. The Inward practices are acts of asking and confessing, the posture of the woman who said 'give me this water'. The Outward practices carry the hearer to people with a verse and an offer, and they point to Christ: the hearer's role is the woman's, 'Come, see', and the aim is that others believe 'because of his own word'. The Abundance Guard is kept by the lesson's refusal to promise changed circumstances.

**Teacher:** A Level 1 participant can do the Inward Life practice tonight: read two verses and pray one sentence. The Outward practices name the kind of person to reach (a neighbour of another background, a difficult coworker, someone with a reputation, someone ashamed of their past) and leave a blank for the name, so a newcomer knows exactly what to do.

**Fix:** The Audit found one risk and the unit has addressed it. John 4:16–18 could be taken as permission to 'expose' other people's sexual histories in the name of light. So the Outward Light practices ask the hearer to confess their own struggle and to receive someone else's confidence privately, never to reveal it; the Common Errors table corrects the misuse; and the Leader's Map below coaches the member who points the light at others. The Gather naming round reads only the second row of the Light Planner, never the private first row.

### ✅ Answer Key
**Q1.** The Pharisees had heard that Jesus was making and baptizing more disciples than John, though his disciples did the baptizing (John 4:1–2). He left Judaea for Galilee, and 'he must needs go through Samaria' (John 4:4).

**Q2.** He sat on Jacob's well near Sychar, 'being wearied with his journey', about the sixth hour, around noon (John 4:5–6). His disciples had gone into the city to buy food (John 4:8).

**Q3.** She was surprised that a Jew would ask a Samaritan woman for a drink, 'for the Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans' (John 4:9).

**Q4.** Whoever drinks the water of the well 'shall thirst again' (John 4:13). Whoever drinks the water Jesus gives 'shall never thirst', because it becomes in him 'a well of water springing up into everlasting life' (John 4:14).

**Q5.** 'A Jew' (John 4:9); 'Sir' (John 4:11, 15, 19); 'a prophet' (John 4:19); and to the town, 'is not this the Christ?' (John 4:29). Jesus says, "I that speak unto thee am he." (John 4:26)

**Q6.** She left her waterpot (John 4:28) and said, "Come, see a man, which told me all things that ever I did: is not this the Christ?" (John 4:29) The Samaritans told her, 'Now we believe, not because of thy saying: for we have heard him ourselves', and called him 'the Saviour of the world' (John 4:42).

**Q7.** He asked Jesus to come down and heal his son, who was at the point of death (John 4:47, 49). Jesus did not go; he said, 'Go thy way; thy son liveth' (John 4:50). The fever left the boy 'yesterday at the seventh hour', the same hour Jesus spoke (John 4:52–53).

**Q8.** Human beings are thirsty for God, and we try to satisfy that thirst with cisterns that cannot hold water (Jeremiah 2:13; Isaiah 55:2). The true water comes from God himself, and Jesus gives it; John explains that it is the Spirit (John 7:37–39). The invitation is to 'every one that thirsteth' (Isaiah 55:1) and 'whosoever will' (Revelation 22:17), including a mixed and despised people like the Samaritans (2 Kings 17:24–33).

**Q9.** Model answer, not the only answer: by asking, Jesus honoured her, put himself in her debt, and crossed the wall between Jew and Samaritan, man and woman, from his side first (John 4:7–9). A request opens a conversation that a rebuke would have closed. It shows that he comes to people humbly and patiently, and that the gift he offers is not a transaction between a superior and an inferior (John 4:10).

**Q10.** Her request shows she still thinks only of ordinary water (John 4:15). Jesus goes to the root of her thirst: her life of broken relationships (John 4:16–18). She cannot receive the living water until she knows her need, and she needs to know that the giver knows everything about her and still offers it.

**Q11.** Faith often begins with someone's testimony or with a desperate need (John 4:39, 47), but it grows as people hear Jesus' own word and trust it (John 4:41–42, 50), and it is confirmed and spreads to others (John 4:53). Faith rests finally on his word, not on another person's report or on seeing a sign (John 4:48).

**Q12.** Model answer, not the only answer: View B fits the text better, though View A holds part of the truth. The hour that 'now is' (John 4:23) brings something new, and in John the new thing is the Spirit given by Jesus (John 7:39) and the truth that 'came by Jesus Christ' (John 1:17). Sincere worship was possible before; worship through the Spirit and in the truth of Christ is what the new hour brings. The Spirit also makes worship inward and real, so View A's concern is included. Let members who hold View A state it at its strongest.

**Q13.** John had to learn the love of Christ for people he despised, and to see the Samaritans as a harvest rather than an enemy (Luke 9:54–55; Acts 8:25). The resurrection, the gift of the Spirit, and Jesus' command to be witnesses in Samaria changed him (Acts 1:8). Answers to the second part will vary.

**Q14.** Answers will vary: a neighbour, a relative, a coworker, someone of another background or religion. Small requests and kindnesses (borrowing something, asking advice, accepting hospitality) can open doors, as Jesus' request did (John 4:7).

**Q15.** Model answer: 'I was always thirsty for something more, and nothing lasted. Jesus gave me a life with God that keeps springing up, even on hard days' (John 4:14). Answers to the second part will vary.

**Q16.** Salvation is 'the gift of God' (John 4:10), not earned or drawn up by our own effort; it is life from God that becomes a spring within and rises into everlasting life (John 4:14). It comes from Jesus himself ('the water that I shall give'), and it is received by asking him (John 4:10).

**Q17.** Model answer: he does not despise me or keep his distance; he knows all my history and still offers his gift (John 4:18, 10). That makes it safe to come with honesty rather than hiding, because his knowledge of me is joined to his kindness.

**Q18.** Answers will vary: arguments about church styles, politics, other people's hypocrisy, theological debates. The Father is seeking worshippers (John 4:23); I do not have to win the argument before I can come. Jesus answered the woman's question honestly and then brought her to himself (John 4:26).

**Q19.** Answers will vary: a busy schedule, a fear of what people will think, a habit of silence, a task that can wait. Humbling ourselves means admitting that our fear or busyness has mattered more to us than Christ, and asking for courage to say 'Come, see' (John 4:29).

**Q20.** Model answer: In Jerusalem the crowds believed because of signs, and Jesus knew their hearts (John 2:23–25). The woman had no standing and no claim, and she was honestly thirsty; to her he revealed himself plainly (John 4:26). It humbles us because Christ is not impressed by rank or religious credentials, and it makes him glorious because he chooses to reveal himself to the lowly (Isaiah 66:2).

**Q21.** Model answers: a friend on a series of relationships, a relative who is always chasing the next success, a neighbour who fills every silence with noise or a screen. 'Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again' (John 4:13).

**Q22.** Model answers: the thief may have stolen their hope that anything could satisfy, their belief that God would want them, or the chance to hear because we have kept our distance. The thief comes "to steal, and to kill, and to destroy" (John 10:10).

**Q23.** Christ gives water that ends the deep thirst and becomes 'a well of water springing up into everlasting life' (John 4:14). It is enough because it depends on the giver, not the drinker's history; the woman with five husbands received the same offer as anyone else.

**Q24.** Model answers: a relative I keep meaning to talk to 'later'; a coworker from a group I avoid; a neighbour I have decided is not interested. Jesus says the fields are 'white already to harvest' (John 4:35).

**Q25.** Answers will vary: a relationship, an addiction, a resentment, a secret. The 'safer subject' might be doctrine, church politics, or someone else's faults. Jesus names things in order to give, not to shame (John 4:10, 16–18).

**Q26.** Model answer: telling others, where it is wise, 'I was once... and Christ found me and forgave me', so that the forgiven thing points to him rather than to me (John 4:39). This should be done with discretion, and never in a way that exposes others.

**Q27.** Model answer: a friend who stopped coming to church after a divorce, someone ashamed of a prison sentence, a young person who has had an abortion. Listen first; then show them that Jesus knew the woman's whole history and still offered her the gift of God (John 4:10, 18), and that he came to seek and to save the lost (Luke 19:10).

### 🗺️ Life & Light Leader's Map
**Worked Life Planner Row:** *Who:* Marcus, who works the next desk over, has been through two divorces and now spends every weekend at the bar with the same group, and who once joked that church is for people who have their lives together. *What the thief has stolen:* the hope that anything could last, and the belief that God would want anything to do with him. *What Christ gives instead:* living water that becomes a spring within (John 4:14), offered by one who knows everything (John 4:18). *What I will do this week:* on Tuesday, ask Marcus for his advice on something he knows well (he fixes old cars), and on Friday invite him for lunch and, if the door opens, tell him in one sentence what Christ has been to me. *Done?* A tick, and a note of what he said.

**Worked Light Planner Row:** *Where it is dark:* my own heart: whenever my small group asks how my marriage is, I talk about church or politics instead. *What is hidden there:* a long coldness between us, and my pride. *What light Christ shines:* he named the woman's hidden life and offered her the gift of God in the same breath (John 4:10, 16–18). *How I carry it:* tonight, tell the Lord plainly what I keep avoiding; on Thursday, tell one trusted friend in the group the truth and ask them to pray with me. This first row stays private; in the naming round members read their second row.

**When Someone Has No One to Name:** Do not accept 'no one' too quickly, and do not shame the person either. John 4 gives two kinds of people to think of: the 'Samaritan', someone from a group we avoid, and the thirsty, someone going from one well to another. Walk through their week with them: 'Who do you pass every day but never really speak to? Who seems to be chasing one thing after another?' Most people can name one of each. If they still cannot, suggest they ask the Lord for one name this week, since 'the Father seeketh such' (John 4:23) and the Lord of the harvest sends labourers (Matthew 9:38).

**When Someone Points the Light at Others:** John 4:16–18 can be misused: 'Jesus exposed her sin, so I should expose theirs.' When a member names a family member's or coworker's sexual history as their 'dark place', bring them gently back to the first row of their Light Planner. Remind the group that Jesus alone knew her life perfectly, that he spoke privately, that he offered the gift first, and that he never shamed her. We are not the all-knowing Messiah; we are the woman with the waterpot, pointing to him. If there is a real concern about another person's sin, point to the private, humble way of Matthew 18:15, after the beam has come out of our own eye (Matthew 7:5).

### 🐑 Shepherding Notes
This chapter touches several tender places. Handle them with care.

- **Divorce, widowhood, and remarriage.** Members who have been divorced, widowed, or married more than once may hear the five husbands as a verdict on them. Say clearly that the text gives no causes, that some of her losses may have been done to her, and that Jesus spoke to her with respect. Do not use the lesson to settle debates about divorce and remarriage; that is for another day and a pastor's care.
- **Living together.** Some in the group, or their children, may be living with a partner outside marriage. Jesus plainly distinguished the woman's present relationship from marriage (John 4:18), and he also kept talking with her and offered her the gift of God. Hold both.
- **Prejudice.** The chapter confronts ethnic, religious, and social hostility. Some members may have suffered it; others may discover it in themselves. Make room for confession without turning the session into a debate about current politics.
- **Sick children and unanswered prayer.** The nobleman's son lived. Some parents in the group may have prayed as hard for a child who did not recover. Do not promise that every child will be healed. The sign shows Jesus' power over death and the trustworthiness of his word; the final answer to every such grief is the resurrection life he gives (John 11:25). Grieve with those who grieve.
- **A shameful testimony.** Some members' stories include things they are ashamed of. The woman's 'all that ever I did' became part of her witness, but no one should be pressed to tell their history in public. Encourage them to tell it first to the Lord, then, if and when they are ready, to someone trusted.
- **The Light Planner's first row.** It is private: never ask anyone to read it aloud, and say so at the start of the naming round. If someone chooses to share, receive it quietly, pray, and keep it in the group.

### 🎯 Teaching Angles
**For a Tier 1 Group:** Bring a jug of water and a glass. Pour a drink, drink it, and ask, 'Will I be thirsty again tomorrow?' Then read John 4:13–14. Tell the story of the woman at the well simply, then the nobleman's son. Ask: What did Jesus offer the woman? How did Jesus know about her life? What did she do when she found out who he was? Use the Tier 1 section, and end by saying John 4:14 together.

**For a Tier 2 Group:** Trace three lines through the chapter. The line of titles: 'a Jew' (John 4:9), 'Sir' (John 4:11), 'greater than our father Jacob'? (John 4:12), 'a prophet' (John 4:19), 'Messias' (John 4:25), 'the Christ' (John 4:29), 'the Saviour of the world' (John 4:42). The line of water: well, living water, spring, waterpot left behind. The line of faith: believing for the woman's saying, because of his own word, and believing the word before seeing (John 4:39–53). Then set John 4 beside John 3: a named Jewish ruler at night and an unnamed Samaritan woman at noon, both needing the same gift.

**Tough Question 1:** *Did Jesus really treat women differently from other teachers of his day?* The disciples' astonishment (John 4:27) shows that a teacher speaking at length with a woman in public was unusual. Jesus taught her, revealed himself to her, and let her become the first witness to her town. That does not answer every modern question about men and women in the church, but it shows that Jesus honoured women as hearers, worshippers, and witnesses.

**Tough Question 2:** *If 'salvation is of the Jews', what does that mean for Jewish people today, and for everyone else?* It means that God's saving plan came through Israel, and that the Saviour himself is a Jew (John 4:22; Romans 9:5). Christians should never speak of Jewish people with contempt. It also means that Jesus is the Saviour of the world, Jew and Gentile alike, so the gospel is offered to all, 'to the Jew first, and also to the Greek' (Romans 1:16). Faithful Christians differ on some details of Israel's future; do not claim more than Scripture says.

**Tough Question 3:** *Does 'God is a Spirit' mean that church buildings, sacraments, and set forms of worship do not matter?* No. It means that God is not confined to any place and that worship must be from a heart made alive by his Spirit and according to his truth (John 4:24). Jesus did not abolish gathered worship; he made himself its centre. Christians differ on how closely Scripture regulates the forms of worship (see the Debate Corner), but all agree that the place no longer makes worship true; Christ and the Spirit do.
