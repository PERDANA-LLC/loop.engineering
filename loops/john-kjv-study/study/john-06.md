# John 6 · The Bread of Life (KJV)
> Unit john-06 · John 6:1–71 · loop john-kjv-study v2.0 · DRAFT for human doctrine review

## 🧭 At a Glance
### 🧵 Golden Thread
Jesus is the true bread from heaven: the Father gives, draws, and keeps his people, and all who come to Jesus and feed on him by faith have eternal life.

### 🌗 Life & Light Line
John 6 answers a hunger that loaves could fill for one afternoon and manna could not cure (the fathers 'did eat manna in the wilderness, and are dead', John 6:49) with the Son who is 'the living bread which came down from heaven' (John 6:51), so that whoever comes to him 'shall never hunger' (John 6:35). It exposes the darkness of following Jesus for what he gives rather than for who he is, and of murmuring when his words are hard, and it gives the light of the Son's own words, which 'are spirit, and they are life' (John 6:63), so that with Peter we confess there is nowhere else to go (John 6:68).

### 🎯 Objectives
- Explain from John 6:35–58 what it means to come to Jesus and feed on him, showing from John 6:35, 40, 47, and 54 that coming, believing, and eating describe one living trust in Christ crucified.
- Trace the Father's work in John 6:37–45 (he gives, he draws, he teaches, and the Son keeps and raises up) and say why this is a comfort to anyone who comes and a warning to anyone who stays away.
- **Doing:** Within the next day, take the bread of life to one hungry person: share food or a meal with someone in need, and in one sentence tell them that Jesus says 'he that cometh to me shall never hunger' (John 6:35).

## 🏛️ Core Foundations
### ✝️ Christological Root
**Gospel Foundation:** John 6 rests on what Jesus did beside the sea and what he said in the synagogue at Capernaum. He took a boy's loaves, gave thanks, and fed a multitude until twelve baskets of fragments were left (John 6:11–13). He came to his frightened disciples across the water in the dark: "But he saith unto them, It is I; be not afraid." (John 6:20) Then he told the crowd what the sign meant: "And Jesus said unto them, I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger; and he that believeth on me shall never thirst." (John 6:35) And he told them how that bread would be given: "I am the living bread which came down from heaven: if any man eat of this bread, he shall live for ever: and the bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world." (John 6:51)

**The Bridge:** The loaves were a sign, and Jesus himself interprets it. The bread that fed five thousand for one afternoon points to the bread that gives life for ever, and that bread is Jesus himself, come down from heaven (John 6:33, 38) and given up to death for the life of the world (John 6:51). The doctrines grounded here are the person of Christ, who came down from heaven and is the Lord of the sea; his sacrificial death, his flesh given and his blood poured out; and the sovereign grace of the Father, who gives a people to the Son, draws them, teaches them, and will not let one be lost (John 6:37–45). Faith is the hand that receives it: coming to him, believing on him, eating his flesh and drinking his blood (John 6:35, 47, 54).

**Canonical Echoes:** Jesus (John 6:35) → Promise (Exodus 16:4; Isaiah 55:1–2) → Doctrine (1 Corinthians 10:16; Galatians 2:20) → Life (Revelation 7:16)

**What Gets Lost:** Taught without Christ at the centre, John 6 becomes a lesson in sharing ('the boy shared his lunch, and everyone else shared theirs'), a lesson in faith under pressure ('Jesus will calm your storms'), or a battleground for arguments about the Mass. Each of those misses what Jesus himself said the sign meant. The crowd made the same mistake: they wanted the bread and not the Baker, a king who would keep the loaves coming, and Jesus walked away from them (John 6:15, 26). Keep him at the centre and the chapter becomes an invitation to the hungry: not to a better meal, but to a person who gives himself as food, and without whom 'ye have no life in you' (John 6:53).

### ⚔️ Spiritual Warfare
**The Lie:** Jesus is valuable for what he provides. Follow him while the loaves keep coming; when he gives you words instead of bread, or a hard saying instead of an easy one, you can take your hunger somewhere else. Its second form sounds more religious: tell me what to do, and I will do the works of God myself (John 6:28).

**The Truth:** The work God asks is to receive, not to achieve: "Jesus answered and said unto them, This is the work of God, that ye believe on him whom he hath sent." (John 6:29) And when every other voice is weighed, there is nowhere else to go: "Then Simon Peter answered him, Lord, to whom shall we go? thou hast the words of eternal life." (John 6:68)

**The Battleground:** Two places. The belly: the appetite that follows Jesus 'because ye did eat of the loaves, and were filled' (John 6:26), and that turns away when he does not feed it. And the offended mind: the heart that hears his words and murmurs, 'This is an hard saying; who can hear it?' (John 6:60).

**The Weapon:** The words of Christ, received by faith. 'The words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life' (John 6:63). The believer fights by feeding on Christ in his word, as Jesus fought the tempter in the wilderness with the lesson of the manna: "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God." (Matthew 4:4)

### 🌱 Life Pathway
**The Theft:** In John 6 the thief steals by filling the belly and starving the soul. The crowd has eaten its fill, and it spends the next day chasing another meal, labouring 'for the meat which perisheth' (John 6:27). Their fathers ate bread from heaven for forty years in the wilderness and still died there (John 6:49). The thief steals by offence as well: when the bread turns out to be a crucified man's flesh, 'many of his disciples went back, and walked no more with him' (John 6:66). And he steals from inside the circle, where a man eats with Jesus and handles the bread and is 'a devil' (John 6:70).

**The Source:** The Son, 'the bread of God' who 'cometh down from heaven, and giveth life unto the world' (John 6:33). He came "that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly" (John 10:10). His means in this chapter are his own flesh given 'for the life of the world' (John 6:51), the Father's drawing and teaching (John 6:44–45), the Spirit that quickeneth (John 6:63), and his words, which are spirit and life (John 6:63, 68).

**The Channel:** Watch the loaves travel. A lad hands over five barley loaves; Jesus gives thanks and gives them 'to the disciples, and the disciples to them that were set down' (John 6:11). The disciples did not make the bread, multiply it, or improve it; they carried what came from his hands. That is the believer's place in bringing life: we hand on the bread of life, Christ himself, in word and in deed, and he does the multiplying. "For without me ye can do nothing." (John 15:5)

**The Abundance Guard:** Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, not prosperity, ease, or freedom from suffering. The crowd wanted a bread-king who would keep them fed, and Jesus 'departed again into a mountain himself alone' (John 6:15). He promised not a full stomach every day but life for ever: 'he shall live for ever' (John 6:51, 58). The disciples who stayed with him got a storm that same night and a hard saying the next day, and the apostles were later beaten for his name (Acts 5:40–41). The bread of life is enough in hunger as in plenty.

### 💡 Light Pathway
**The Darkness:** John notes the darkness plainly: 'it was now dark, and Jesus was not come to them' (John 6:17). The deeper darkness is in the heart. The crowd looked straight at a sign and saw only lunch (John 6:26). The Jews murmured at him and said they knew his father and mother, so he could not have come from heaven (John 6:41–42). Some who called themselves his disciples did not believe, and Jesus 'knew from the beginning who they were that believed not, and who should betray him' (John 6:64). Unbelief can sit in the front row.

**The Source:** Christ, whose words 'are spirit, and they are life' (John 6:63), and who knows every heart (John 6:61, 64). He is "the light of the world" (John 8:12). "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." (2 Corinthians 4:6)

**The Lamp:** In John 6 the lamp is plain speech about motives and about Christ. Jesus told the crowd why they had come (John 6:26); he told the murmurers why they could not come (John 6:44, 65); he told the twelve that one of them was a devil (John 6:70). The lamp for us is the same: the Scriptures opened to show Christ as the bread from heaven, the truth told about why we follow him, hidden unbelief confessed, the unfruitful works of darkness reproved (Ephesians 5:11), beginning with our own, and good works that glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16), like the bread the disciples carried from his hands to the hungry.

**The Love Guard:** Light without love is glare. Jesus exposed the crowd's motive in the same breath as he offered them the bread of life (John 6:26–27). He named the unbelief among his disciples, and he still asked the twelve, gently, 'Will ye also go away?' (John 6:67). He knew Judas from the beginning and kept him at the table. We turn the lamp first on our own motives for following Christ, and when we speak to anyone else about theirs we do it as he did, to feed them, never to shame them.

### 🏺 Setting the Scene
John 6 moves from Jerusalem back to Galilee. Jesus crosses 'the sea of Galilee, which is the sea of Tiberias' (John 6:1). Tiberias was a new city on the western shore, founded by Herod Antipas around AD 20 and named for the emperor Tiberius, and the lake came to be called after it (verify the date). John tells us that 'the passover, a feast of the Jews, was nigh' (John 6:4). This is the second Passover in John (John 2:13; John 6:4; John 11:55), and the only one at which John places Jesus in Galilee rather than in Jerusalem (compare John 7:1). It was spring: Mark notes that the people sat on 'the green grass' (Mark 6:39). The barley harvest began at Passover time (Leviticus 23:10), and barley bread was the cheaper, coarser bread of the poor (verify). Everything about the season spoke of the Exodus: the lamb, the unleavened bread, and the manna in the wilderness.

The feeding of the five thousand is the only miracle of Jesus' ministry, apart from his resurrection, that all four Gospels record (Matthew 14:13–21; Mark 6:30–44; Luke 9:10–17). John alone names Philip and Andrew, mentions the lad and his barley loaves, tells us that the crowd tried to make Jesus king by force, and gives the long discourse in the synagogue at Capernaum the next day (John 6:59). Moses had promised that God would raise up a Prophet like him (Deuteronomy 18:15), and some Jewish writings from the first century and after expected that the manna would fall again when the Messiah came (for example *2 Baruch* 29:8, verify). A man who fed a crowd with bread in the wilderness at Passover time looked to them like that Prophet, and like a king who could drive out Rome (John 6:14–15).

The chapter has two halves. Beside the sea, Jesus feeds the crowd and walks on the water. In the synagogue, he explains what the bread means, and the crowd that wanted to crown him one evening is murmuring against him the next day. John 6 is where the Galilean crowds turn away (John 6:66). It is also the chapter of the first 'I am' saying with an image: 'I am the bread of life' (John 6:35). Jesus will say 'I am' with an image six more times in this Gospel.

**View A:** When Jesus speaks of eating his flesh and drinking his blood (John 6:53–56), he is speaking of the Lord's Supper. The words 'eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood' (John 6:54) are sacramental words, and they match what he said at the Last Supper: "Take, eat; this is my body." (Matthew 26:26) Paul speaks in the same way: "The bread which we break, is it not the communion of the body of Christ?" (1 Corinthians 10:16) Many Roman Catholic, Orthodox, and Lutheran readers, and some others, hold this view in different forms.

**View B:** Jesus is speaking of faith in him as the one who would give his flesh in death for the life of the world (John 6:51). Eating and drinking are pictures of believing: the same promise of eternal life is made to the one who believes (John 6:47) and to the one who eats (John 6:54), and Jesus explains, "It is the spirit that quickeneth; the flesh profiteth nothing: the words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life." (John 6:63) The Supper, instituted later, is a sign and seal of this feeding, but it is not what John 6 is about. Many Reformed, evangelical, and other readers hold this view.

### 👣 Case Study · Scripture in Action
Simon Peter spoke for the twelve when the crowds went home: 'Lord, to whom shall we go? thou hast the words of eternal life' (John 6:68). He meant it. Yet on the night Jesus was betrayed, beside another fire, he denied three times that he even knew him (John 18:17, 25–27). Jesus had seen it coming and had prayed for him: "But I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not: and when thou art converted, strengthen thy brethren." (Luke 22:32) After the resurrection, on the shore of the same sea of Tiberias (John 21:1), Peter came out of the water to find a meal already prepared: "As soon then as they were come to land, they saw a fire of coals there, and fish laid thereon, and bread." (John 21:9) The Lord who once fed five thousand with bread and fish gave bread and fish to the man who had failed him (John 21:13), and then asked him three times whether he loved him, and told him, 'Feed my sheep' (John 21:17). Peter's confidence in John 6 did not keep him from falling. What kept him was the Lord who had chosen him (John 6:70), who lost none the Father had given him (John 6:39), and who fed him again.

### 🏙️ Modern Example
Renee had followed Jesus since college, and for years the following was easy. Prayers were answered: a good job, a husband, two healthy children, a church she loved. ### 📜 Voices from Church History
In 1745 the Welsh preacher and hymn-writer William Williams of Pantycelyn wrote a hymn in Welsh that was later put into English (1771, partly by Peter Williams and partly by William Williams himself, verify) and is sung today as *Guide Me, O Thou Great Jehovah* (in some books, *Guide Me, O Thou Great Redeemer*). Summarized in plain words (not quoted): the singer is a pilgrim walking through a barren land, weak and needing to be held by God's strong hand. The whole hymn is the Exodus retold as the Christian life, and its centre is the manna of John 6: Christ, the true bread from heaven, fed to pilgrims day by day until they reach home.

### 👑 Christocentric & Humility · Level 4
Every problem in John 6 is answered in Jesus. A multitude is hungry in a lonely place with no shops, and he feeds them from a boy's lunch. The disciples are counting money they do not have, and he already 'knew what he would do' (John 6:6). The crowd wants to seize him and make him a king of bread, and he withdraws alone, because his kingdom will come by a cross and not by a mob. The disciples are rowing in the dark against a great wind, and he comes to them on the water and says, 'It is I; be not afraid'. The people want a sign like the manna, and he tells them that he himself is the bread the manna pointed to. The Jews murmur that no one can come down from heaven, and he tells them that no one can even come to him unless the Father draws him. People are dead, and he gives his flesh for the life of the world. Many disciples leave, and he does not chase them with easier words; he turns to the twelve and asks if they will go too, and Peter finds there is nowhere else to go.

Every truth in the chapter is found in him too. The Father's gift is found in him (John 6:32). Life is found in him (John 6:35, 51). The Father's will is done by him, and none of his people is lost (John 6:38–39). Resurrection is found in him (John 6:40). The words of eternal life are his (John 6:68). The chapter humbles us from every side. It humbles the hungry, who must be fed and cannot feed themselves. It humbles the religious, who want to do the works of God and are told to believe. It humbles the confident, because no one can come unless the Father draws him, and because a devil sat among the twelve. And it makes Christ glorious, because the one who fed thousands and walked on the sea came down from heaven 'not to do mine own will' (John 6:38), and gave his own flesh as bread for people who would murmur at him.

- Today, when you are tempted to push to be noticed or put in charge, choose to step back and let the matter go, remembering that Jesus refused a crown offered by force and 'departed again into a mountain himself alone' (John 6:15).

**Why this verse:** It is the chapter's centre, the first of the seven 'I am' sayings with an image in John, and the Lesson Map's choice for this lesson. The verse speaks to the hungry heart of the unbeliever and the tired heart of the believer alike, and it states in small what John says his whole book is for: that believing we 'might have life through his name' (John 20:31).

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy
### 📌 Core Concept
One spring day, near the time of the Passover feast, thousands of people followed Jesus out to a grassy hillside by a lake. They were hungry, and there was nowhere to buy food. A boy had five small barley loaves and two little fish. Jesus thanked God for them and gave them to his disciples to hand out, and everyone ate as much as they wanted. There were twelve baskets of leftovers! That night Jesus walked across the lake to his disciples in a storm and told them not to be afraid, because it was him. The next day the people found him again, but Jesus knew they mostly wanted more free bread. He told them that he is the real bread from heaven. The bread God gave Israel in the desert, called manna, fed them for a day, but Jesus gives a life that never ends. Anyone who comes to him and trusts him will never be hungry inside again. Many people did not like what he said and went home. But Peter told Jesus that there was no one else to go to, because only Jesus has the words that give life for ever.

### 📖 Key Verses
"But he saith unto them, It is I; be not afraid." (John 6:20) When Jesus is with us, even a storm in the dark does not need to frighten us.

"And Jesus said unto them, I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger; and he that believeth on me shall never thirst." (John 6:35) Jesus is what our hearts are hungry for, and he fills us up for ever when we come to him.

"All that the Father giveth me shall come to me; and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out." (John 6:37) Jesus will never send away anyone who comes to him.

"Then Simon Peter answered him, Lord, to whom shall we go? thou hast the words of eternal life." (John 6:68) Nobody else can give us what Jesus gives, so we stay with him even when things are hard.

### 🪞 Analogy
Imagine a girl who is starving hungry and is given a picture of a sandwich. It is a very good picture, with every crumb and lettuce leaf drawn perfectly. She can look at it, but she cannot eat it. The next day someone gives her the real sandwich, the very one in the picture. The picture was not a trick; it was there to make her ready for the real thing. The manna in the desert was a little like that picture. It was real food, and it kept God's people alive for a while, but it was pointing to something better. Jesus is the real bread. He does not just fill you up until lunchtime; he gives you life with God that lasts for ever.

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium
### 📜 Walkthrough
#### John 6:1–4 · Over the Sea, near the Passover
**Says:** "After these things Jesus went over the sea of Galilee, which is the sea of Tiberias." (John 6:1) "And a great multitude followed him, because they saw his miracles which he did on them that were diseased." (John 6:2) "And Jesus went up into a mountain, and there he sat with his disciples." (John 6:3) "And the passover, a feast of the Jews, was nigh." (John 6:4) *Nigh* means near.

**Means:** 'After these things' is John's loose link between episodes; he does not tell us how much time has passed since the feast in Jerusalem (John 5:1). The other Gospels place this feeding after the death of John the Baptist and the return of the twelve from their mission (Mark 6:30–44). Jesus crosses to the far side of the lake, and the crowd follows 'because they saw his miracles'. The word is *sēmeia* (G4592, John 6:2), signs, John's usual word for Jesus' miracles: acts that point beyond themselves. The crowd has seen the signs, but the rest of the chapter will show that they have not read them (John 6:26).

Jesus 'went up into a mountain, and there he sat'. Teachers sat to teach, and Moses went up the mountain to receive the law; John does not press the comparison, but the hints of the Exodus gather quickly. The strongest is verse 4: 'the passover, a feast of the Jews, was nigh', *pascha* (G3957, John 6:4). John rarely gives a date for its own sake. Passover remembered the lamb, the night of deliverance, and the journey that followed, when God fed his people with bread from heaven. Everything Jesus does and says in this chapter should be heard with that feast in the background, and the next Passover in John will be the one at which he dies (John 11:55; John 19:14).

**Asks:** Why do you follow Jesus: because of what you have seen him do for others, or because you have understood who he is? Read the rest of the chapter with the Passover in mind, and ask what the Lamb of God (John 1:29) has to do with the bread of life.

#### John 6:5–9 · Whence Shall We Buy Bread?
**Says:** "When Jesus then lifted up his eyes, and saw a great company come unto him, he saith unto Philip, Whence shall we buy bread, that these may eat?" (John 6:5) "And this he said to prove him: for he himself knew what he would do." (John 6:6) "Philip answered him, Two hundred pennyworth of bread is not sufficient for them, that every one of them may take a little." (John 6:7) "One of his disciples, Andrew, Simon Peter's brother, saith unto him," (John 6:8) "There is a lad here, which hath five barley loaves, and two small fishes: but what are they among so many?" (John 6:9) *Whence* means from where; *prove* means test; *pennyworth* means the worth of a penny, here the Roman *denarius*; a *lad* is a boy.

**Means:** Jesus sees the crowd coming and takes the initiative. He asks Philip, who came from Bethsaida nearby (John 1:44), where they can buy bread. John immediately tells us why: 'this he said to prove him', *peirazōn* (G3985, John 6:6, V-PAP-NSM), testing him. It is not a trap; it is a teacher's question. And John adds, 'for he himself knew what he would do'. Jesus is never at a loss in this Gospel.

Philip does the arithmetic. The coin is the *dēnariōn* (G1220, John 6:7), the Roman denarius, which the KJV calls a penny; in Jesus' parable it was a labourer's wage for a day (Matthew 20:2). Two hundred of them, most of a year's wages for a working man, would not buy enough for everyone to have 'a little'. Philip is right, and his answer is the answer of every committee that has ever looked at a need and counted what it has.

Andrew, who once brought his brother Simon to Jesus (John 1:41–42), now brings a boy. The *paidarion* (G3808, John 6:9) is a little boy, a lad, though the STEPBible lexicon notes that in everyday Greek of the time the word could lose its sense of smallness. He has five loaves made of barley, *krithinous* (G2916, John 6:9), and two small fish, *opsaria* (G3795, John 6:9), probably dried or pickled fish eaten with bread. Barley loaves were the cheaper bread of the poor (verify). Only John mentions the barley, and it recalls another feeding: a man brought the prophet Elisha 'twenty loaves of barley' (2 Kings 4:42), and when the servant protested that it could not feed a hundred men, the LORD's word came true: "So he set it before them, and they did eat, and left thereof, according to the word of the LORD." (2 Kings 4:44) Andrew's question is the servant's question: 'but what are they among so many?'

**Asks:** When you face a need far bigger than your resources, do you calculate like Philip or bring what little there is like Andrew? Jesus already knows what he will do. Bring him what you have, even if it looks ridiculous, and let him be the one who multiplies it.

#### John 6:10–13 · Twelve Baskets over and Above
**Says:** "And Jesus said, Make the men sit down. Now there was much grass in the place. So the men sat down, in number about five thousand." (John 6:10) "And Jesus took the loaves; and when he had given thanks, he distributed to the disciples, and the disciples to them that were set down; and likewise of the fishes as much as they would." (John 6:11) "When they were filled, he said unto his disciples, Gather up the fragments that remain, that nothing be lost." (John 6:12) "Therefore they gathered them together, and filled twelve baskets with the fragments of the five barley loaves, which remained over and above unto them that had eaten." (John 6:13) *Fragments* means broken pieces; *over and above* means left over.

**Means:** Jesus gives orders like a host preparing a banquet: 'Make the men sit down'. The word for 'sit down' means to recline, the posture of a meal. 'Now there was much grass in the place', a detail from an eyewitness, and a reminder that it was spring. John counts 'the men', about five thousand; Matthew adds that there were women and children besides (Matthew 14:21).

Then the heart of the sign, in four verbs: he 'took', 'had given thanks', and 'distributed', and the people ate 'as much as they would'. 'Given thanks' is *eucharistēsas* (G2168, John 6:11, V-AAP-NSM), from *eucharisteō*. The STEPBible lexicon lists this verse among the places where the word is used of giving thanks before a meal, beside the accounts of the Last Supper (Luke 22:17, 19) and Paul's account of the Supper (1 Corinthians 11:24). Jesus is the host and thanks the Father for the bread, as any Jewish head of a household would; John will mention the thanksgiving again when he names the place (John 6:23). Later readers have heard in the words an echo of the Lord's Supper, and the Council's first question weighs how far that echo goes.

Jesus 'distributed to the disciples, and the disciples to them that were set down'. The bread passes through Jesus' hands to the disciples' hands to the crowd. Nobody sees the moment of multiplication. And the people ate until they 'were filled'. This is not a token meal; it is abundance.

Then the command: 'Gather up the fragments that remain, that nothing be lost.' The word for 'lost', *apolētai* (G622, John 6:12), is the verb Jesus will use of his people in John 6:39: 'of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing'. The one who will not waste bread will not lose a single soul. The fragments, *klasmata* (G2801, John 6:12), filled twelve baskets, *kophinous* (G2894, John 6:13). The STEPBible lexicon describes the *kophinos* as a basket, probably of wicker, of the kind Jews carried for food. The word occurs 6 times in the New Testament, all in the accounts of this feeding or in the Lord's later reminders of it (Matthew 16:9; Mark 8:19). Twelve baskets for twelve disciples: each of those who doubted went home carrying more than they started with.

**Asks:** Do you give thanks before you eat, as Jesus did? Do you trust that he can fill you, and not only give you a taste? And do you believe that the Lord who would not let a crumb be wasted will not let you be lost?

#### John 6:14–15 · A King by Force
**Says:** "Then those men, when they had seen the miracle that Jesus did, said, This is of a truth that prophet that should come into the world." (John 6:14) "When Jesus therefore perceived that they would come and take him by force, to make him a king, he departed again into a mountain himself alone." (John 6:15) *Of a truth* means truly; *perceived* means realized.

**Means:** The crowd draws the right conclusion by the wrong road. 'That prophet that should come into the world' is the Prophet Moses promised: "The LORD thy God will raise up unto thee a Prophet from the midst of thee, of thy brethren, like unto me; unto him ye shall hearken;" (Deuteronomy 18:15) Moses fed Israel with bread in the wilderness; here is a man who has done the same. The *prophētēs* (G4396, John 6:14) is the one who speaks for God; the crowd has seen the sign, *sēmeion* (G4592, John 6:14), and names him rightly as far as the name goes.

But they want the Prophet to be a king, and a king on their terms. They were about to 'take him by force', *harpazein* (G726, John 6:15), to seize, snatch, carry off. The STEPBible lexicon lists this verse beside John 10:28–29, where the same verb is used for plucking the sheep out of the Shepherd's hand. In a land ruled by Rome, a king who could feed an army from a basket was exactly what many wanted. Jesus 'perceived', and 'departed again into a mountain himself alone'. He will be a king (John 18:37; John 19:19), but his throne will be a cross, and his crown will be given by the Father and not seized by a crowd. John's irony is quiet and sharp: the people who want to make him king will soon walk away from him (John 6:66).

**Asks:** Do you want Jesus as king because he will do what you want, or because he is who he is? What would you do if he refused the crown you offered him and gave you himself instead?

#### John 6:16–21 · It Is I; Be Not Afraid
**Says:** "And when even was now come, his disciples went down unto the sea," (John 6:16) "And entered into a ship, and went over the sea toward Capernaum. And it was now dark, and Jesus was not come to them." (John 6:17) "And the sea arose by reason of a great wind that blew." (John 6:18) "So when they had rowed about five and twenty or thirty furlongs, they see Jesus walking on the sea, and drawing nigh unto the ship: and they were afraid." (John 6:19) "But he saith unto them, It is I; be not afraid." (John 6:20) "Then they willingly received him into the ship: and immediately the ship was at the land whither they went." (John 6:21) *Even* means evening; *furlongs* translates the Greek measure of a stadium, about an eighth of a Roman mile; *whither* means to which.

**Means:** Evening comes and the disciples set out across the lake without Jesus. John sets three sentences side by side: 'it was now dark', 'Jesus was not come to them', and 'the sea arose by reason of a great wind'. In John darkness is never only a matter of the clock (John 1:5; John 3:2; John 13:30). The disciples are in the dark, in a storm, and alone.

They had rowed 'about five and twenty or thirty furlongs'. The *stadious* (G4712, John 6:19) were stadia; the STEPBible lexicon gives the stadium as six hundred Greek feet, an eighth of a Roman mile, so they had rowed three or four miles and were well out on the lake. Then 'they see Jesus walking on the sea', *peripatounta* (G4043, John 6:19, V-PAP-ASM). The Old Testament gives this power to God alone. Job speaks of God 'Which alone spreadeth out the heavens, and treadeth upon the waves of the sea' (Job 9:8), and the psalmist says, "Thy way is in the sea, and thy path in the great waters, and thy footsteps are not known." (Psalm 77:19) At the Exodus, God made a way through the sea for his people. Now, at Passover time, the Son walks on it.

'And they were afraid.' Jesus answers with two words in Greek: *egō* (G1473, John 6:20) *eimi* (G1510, John 6:20, V-PAI-1S), 'I am', which the KJV renders 'It is I'. In ordinary speech the words simply identify the speaker: 'It's me'. But in John they carry more weight. The same words stand without a predicate in John 8:58 and John 18:5–6, where they echo the name God gave Moses: "I AM THAT I AM" (Exodus 3:14). Whether John means his readers to hear that echo here is debated (see the Council's third question). What is not debated is that the one who says 'It is I' is walking on the water, and that his presence ends their fear: 'be not afraid', with *mē* (G3361, John 6:20) and a present-tense command, *phobeisthe* (G5399, John 6:20, V-PNM-2P): do not go on being afraid.

'Then they willingly received him into the ship: and immediately the ship was at the land'. The storm and the distance are simply over. Mark tells us that the wind ceased and that the disciples were amazed, because they had not understood the loaves (Mark 6:51–52). John tells it in fewer words and lets the arrival speak: when Jesus is received, the journey is finished. The psalmist sang of those whom the LORD rescued at sea: "Then are they glad because they be quiet; so he bringeth them unto their desired haven." (Psalm 107:30)

**Asks:** Where are you rowing in the dark, against the wind, as if Jesus has not come? Listen for his voice, 'It is I; be not afraid'. Receive him into the boat, and trust him to bring you to the shore.

#### John 6:22–25 · Seeking for Jesus
**Says:** "The day following, when the people which stood on the other side of the sea saw that there was none other boat there, save that one whereinto his disciples were entered, and that Jesus went not with his disciples into the boat, but that his disciples were gone away alone;" (John 6:22) "(Howbeit there came other boats from Tiberias nigh unto the place where they did eat bread, after that the Lord had given thanks:)" (John 6:23) "When the people therefore saw that Jesus was not there, neither his disciples, they also took shipping, and came to Capernaum, seeking for Jesus." (John 6:24) "And when they had found him on the other side of the sea, they said unto him, Rabbi, when camest thou hither?" (John 6:25) *Save* means except; *howbeit* means however; *took shipping* means got into boats; *hither* means here.

**Means:** These verses are a puzzle about boats, told carefully. The crowd knew that only one boat had been there, that the disciples left in it, and that Jesus did not go with them. So where was he? Boats from Tiberias arrive, and the crowd uses them to cross to Capernaum, 'seeking for Jesus'. The detail shows that the crowd could not explain how Jesus had crossed. They had seen the sign of the loaves; they had not seen the sign on the sea, which was for the disciples alone.

Notice the narrator's reminder in verse 23: the place was 'where they did eat bread, after that the Lord had given thanks'. John calls Jesus 'the Lord', *kuriou* (G2962, John 6:23), and repeats the thanksgiving, *eucharistēsantos* (G2168, John 6:23). He wants the reader to remember the bread and the giver.

'Rabbi, when camest thou hither?' They call him teacher, and they ask 'when', not 'how'. Jesus does not answer their question. He answers the question they should have asked.

**Asks:** People can take a lot of trouble to find Jesus and still be looking for the wrong thing. When you come to him, whether in worship, in prayer, or in this study, what are you really looking for?

#### John 6:26–29 · The Work of God
**Says:** "Jesus answered them and said, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Ye seek me, not because ye saw the miracles, but because ye did eat of the loaves, and were filled." (John 6:26) "Labour not for the meat which perisheth, but for that meat which endureth unto everlasting life, which the Son of man shall give unto you: for him hath God the Father sealed." (John 6:27) "Then said they unto him, What shall we do, that we might work the works of God?" (John 6:28) "Jesus answered and said unto them, This is the work of God, that ye believe on him whom he hath sent." (John 6:29) *Meat* in the KJV means food of any kind; *perisheth* means spoils, passes away; *sealed* means marked with a seal of approval.

**Means:** 'Verily, verily' opens the first of 4 solemn sayings in this discourse (John 6:26, 32, 47, 53). Jesus exposes their motive: 'Ye seek me, not because ye saw the miracles', that is, not because they understood the signs, 'but because ye did eat of the loaves, and were filled'. The verb for 'were filled', *echortasthēte* (G5526, John 6:26, V-API-2P), is from *chortazō*; the STEPBible lexicon notes that it was used first of feeding animals and in later Greek of filling or satisfying people with food, and it lists the same verb for the crowds filled at the feedings in the other Gospels (Matthew 14:20; Mark 6:42). They had been satisfied, and they came back for more bread.

So Jesus redirects them: 'Labour not for the meat which perisheth'. The verb is *ergazesthe* (G2038, John 6:27, V-PNM-2P), a present command: do not go on working for food, *brōsin* (G1035, John 6:27), that spoils. The manna itself spoiled overnight if it was kept (Exodus 16:20). Instead, work for food 'which endureth unto everlasting life'. And at once Jesus says that this food is not earned; it is what 'the Son of man shall give unto you'. The reason he can give it: 'for him hath God the Father sealed', *esphragisen* (G4972, John 6:27, V-AAI-3S). The STEPBible lexicon lists this verse under sealing 'for authentication', beside John 3:33. A seal on a document or a sack guaranteed that it was genuine and belonged to its owner. The Father has put his mark on the Son; the signs, and above all the Spirit who came upon him (John 1:32–33), are that seal.

The crowd hears the word 'labour' and asks the natural religious question: 'What shall we do, that we might work the works of God?' They use the plural, *erga* (G2041, John 6:28): works, deeds, a list. Jesus answers with a singular, *ergon* (G2041, John 6:29): 'This is the work of God, that ye believe on him whom he hath sent'. The one thing God requires is faith in his Son. Believing is called a 'work' because the crowd asked about work, not because faith earns anything; it is the opposite of earning, the empty hand that receives what 'the Son of man shall give'. Whether 'the work of God' means the work God requires or the work God performs in us, the grammar allows either, and John 6:44 and 65 will show that faith is also God's gift.

**Asks:** What are you labouring for? Where have you asked 'What must I do?' when Jesus is saying 'Believe on me'? Rest from working for God's favour, and receive the food the Son of man gives.

#### John 6:30–33 · The True Bread from Heaven
**Says:** "They said therefore unto him, What sign shewest thou then, that we may see, and believe thee? what dost thou work?" (John 6:30) "Our fathers did eat manna in the desert; as it is written, He gave them bread from heaven to eat." (John 6:31) "Then Jesus said unto them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Moses gave you not that bread from heaven; but my Father giveth you the true bread from heaven." (John 6:32) "For the bread of God is he which cometh down from heaven, and giveth life unto the world." (John 6:33) *Shewest* means show.

**Means:** The request is astonishing. The day after the feeding, the crowd asks for a sign, *sēmeion* (G4592, John 6:30), so that they can believe. They have a particular sign in mind. Moses gave 'our fathers' manna, *manna* (G3131, John 6:31), for forty years; Jesus has given them one meal. If he is the Prophet like Moses, let him do what Moses did. They quote Scripture: 'He gave them bread from heaven to eat', words drawn from the story of the manna (Exodus 16:4, 15) and from the psalms that remembered it: "And had rained down manna upon them to eat, and had given them of the corn of heaven." (Psalm 78:24) Nehemiah's prayer says the same (Nehemiah 9:15).

Jesus corrects them three times in one sentence. First, the giver was not Moses but God: 'Moses gave you not that bread from heaven'. Second, the tense: Moses 'gave', *dedōken* (G1325, John 6:32, V-RAI-3S), in the past, but 'my Father giveth', *didōsin* (G1325, John 6:32, V-PAI-3S), now. Third, the bread: the manna fell from the sky, but the Father now gives 'the true bread from heaven'. 'True' is *alēthinon* (G228, John 6:32), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as true 'in the sense of real, ideal, genuine', listing this verse beside 'the true light' (John 1:9) and 'the true vine' (John 15:1). The manna was not false; it was real food from God. But it was a shadow, and the true bread is the reality it pointed to.

Then Jesus defines the bread in a way that is still, for the moment, veiled: 'the bread of God is he which cometh down from heaven, and giveth life unto the world'. The participle *katabainōn* (G2597, John 6:33, V-PAP-NSM) can be read 'that which comes down' or 'he who comes down'; the KJV chooses 'he', and the next verses prove it right. The manna fed one nation in one wilderness and only kept them alive; this bread gives life, and it is for 'the world'.

**Asks:** Do you keep asking God for signs while ignoring the gift he has already given? The Father is giving the true bread now. Where have you settled for shadows (comfort, success, religion) when the Father offers his Son?

#### John 6:34–36 · I Am the Bread of Life
**Says:** "Then said they unto him, Lord, evermore give us this bread." (John 6:34) "And Jesus said unto them, I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger; and he that believeth on me shall never thirst." (John 6:35) "But I said unto you, That ye also have seen me, and believe not." (John 6:36) *Evermore* means always.

**Means:** 'Lord, evermore give us this bread' sounds like the Samaritan woman's 'Sir, give me this water' (John 4:15). Both are sincere and both are misunderstandings: they want a supply of something, and Jesus is offering himself.

So he says it plainly: 'I am the bread of life'. It is the first of John's 'I am' sayings with an image, and it begins with the emphatic *egō* (G1473, John 6:35) *eimi* (G1510, John 6:35), 'I, I am'. Jesus does not say he gives the bread, or points to the bread, or teaches about the bread. He is the bread: the life he gives is himself. 'Of life' means the bread that gives and sustains life, as the STEPBible lexicon explains *artos* (G740, John 6:35) in its metaphorical use of Christ, 'the bread of God, the bread of life', listing John 6:33 and 35.

Then the promise, in two matching lines. 'He that cometh to me', *erchomenos* (G2064, John 6:35, V-PNP-NSM), 'shall never hunger'; 'he that believeth on me', *pisteuōn* (G4100, John 6:35, V-PAP-NSM), 'shall never thirst'. The two lines explain each other: coming to Jesus is believing on him. Both participles are present, the one who keeps coming, the one who keeps believing. The negatives are the strongest Greek has: *ou mē*, with *mē* (G3361, John 6:35), 'shall never, by no means'. Isaiah had promised a day when God's people 'shall not hunger nor thirst' (Isaiah 49:10), and the book of Revelation sees it fulfilled: "They shall hunger no more, neither thirst any more; neither shall the sun light on them, nor any heat." (Revelation 7:16) Jesus says it begins with coming to him. He adds thirst to hunger, although the talk has been of bread, because he is thinking of the whole need of the soul (compare John 4:14; John 7:37).

Then the sorrowful line: 'ye also have seen me, and believe not'. Sight is not faith. They saw the loaves, they saw the man, and they did not come.

**Asks:** Have you come to Jesus himself, or only asked him for bread? He does not promise that you will never feel hunger again in this life; he promises that the hunger of your soul will be met in him, and that whoever keeps coming will never be sent away empty.

#### John 6:37–40 · The Father's Will
**Says:** "All that the Father giveth me shall come to me; and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out." (John 6:37) "For I came down from heaven, not to do mine own will, but the will of him that sent me." (John 6:38) "And this is the Father's will which hath sent me, that of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing, but should raise it up again at the last day." (John 6:39) "And this is the will of him that sent me, that every one which seeth the Son, and believeth on him, may have everlasting life: and I will raise him up at the last day." (John 6:40) *In no wise* means in no way, never.

**Means:** Here Jesus explains why some believe and some do not, and why those who believe are safe. Verse 37 has two halves. The first is about the Father: 'All that the Father giveth me shall come to me'. 'All that' is neuter, *pan* (G3956, John 6:37), 'everything', a whole people seen as one gift. The Father gives them, and they 'shall come', *hēxei* (G2240, John 6:37, V-FAI-3S), a plain future: not 'may come' but 'will'. The second half is about the one who comes: 'him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out'. The verb is *ekbalō* (G1544, John 6:37, V-2AAS-1S), to drive or cast out, with the strong negative *mē* (G3361, John 6:37) and *exō* (G1854, John 6:37), 'out': I will certainly never throw him out. The STEPBible lexicon lists this verse under *ekballō* with 'out', the word used of driving people out of a place. The first half guarantees that the Father's gift will arrive; the second guarantees that no one who arrives will be turned away. No one who comes need wonder whether he was given.

Verse 38 grounds it in the Son's obedience. 'I came down from heaven', *katabebēka* (G2597, John 6:38, V-RAI-1S), a perfect: I have come down and am here. He came not to do his own will but 'the will of him that sent me', *thelēma* (G2307, John 6:38). Verses 39 and 40 say what that will is, in two parallel statements. From the Father's side: 'of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing', where 'lose' is *apolesō* (G622, John 6:39, V-AAS-1S), the verb of the gathered fragments (John 6:12). From the human side: 'every one which seeth the Son, and believeth on him, may have everlasting life'. 'Seeth' is *theōrōn* (G2334, John 6:40, V-PAP-NSM), beholding with attention, the seeing that becomes faith, unlike the seeing of verse 36. And both statements end with the same promise: 'I will raise him up at the last day'. The phrase 'the last day' occurs 4 times in this chapter (John 6:39, 40, 44, 54), and the Son's keeping does not stop at death.

These verses hold together two things that the rest of Scripture also holds together: God's sovereign giving and keeping, and the free invitation to 'every one' who sees and believes. Jesus does not set them against each other, and neither should we.

**Asks:** If you have come to Christ, rest: you were given to him, you will not be cast out, and you will not be lost. If you are hesitating, hear the promise: 'him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out'. The only way to know you were given is to come.

#### John 6:41–46 · No Man Can Come, except the Father Draw Him
**Says:** "The Jews then murmured at him, because he said, I am the bread which came down from heaven." (John 6:41) "And they said, Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know? how is it then that he saith, I came down from heaven?" (John 6:42) "Jesus therefore answered and said unto them, Murmur not among yourselves." (John 6:43) "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him: and I will raise him up at the last day." (John 6:44) "It is written in the prophets, And they shall be all taught of God. Every man therefore that hath heard, and hath learned of the Father, cometh unto me." (John 6:45) "Not that any man hath seen the Father, save he which is of God, he hath seen the Father." (John 6:46) *Murmured* means grumbled, complained in low voices.

**Means:** Now 'the Jews', here the Galilean hearers in the synagogue who object to him, 'murmured'. Jesus answers, 'Murmur not among yourselves', *gonguzete* (G1111, John 6:43, V-PAM-2P). The STEPBible lexicon notes that in the Greek Old Testament the word chiefly translates the Hebrew verb for Israel's murmuring in the wilderness. Their fathers murmured against God over the manna: "for that the LORD heareth your murmurings which ye murmur against him" (Exodus 16:8). The sons murmur against the true bread. Paul later warns the church not to repeat it (1 Corinthians 10:10).

Their objection is familiarity: 'Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know?' They know where he comes from, or think they do; John's readers know better (John 1:14). The irony is that the one thing they are sure of is the thing they have most wrong.

Jesus does not argue about his origin. He goes to the root: 'No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him'. 'Can' is *dunatai* (G1410, John 6:44, V-PNI-3S), is able: this is a statement about ability, not only about permission. 'Draw' is *helkusē* (G1670, John 6:44, V-AAS-3S). The STEPBible lexicon defines *helkuō* as 'to draw', used literally with things (John 18:10, of a sword; John 21:6, of a net) and with persons (Acts 16:19; James 2:6, of dragging people before the courts), and metaphorically 'to draw, lead, impel' in John 6:44 and John 12:32. The word occurs 8 times in the New Testament. How this drawing works, and whether it is given to all or effective in some, is the question of the Debate Corner. What the verse says plainly is that without it no one comes, and that everyone it brings the Son will 'raise him up at the last day', *anastēsō* (G450, John 6:44, V-FAI-1S).

Then Jesus shows how the Father draws: by teaching. 'It is written in the prophets, And they shall be all taught of God'. He quotes Isaiah's promise of the new covenant: "And all thy children shall be taught of the LORD; and great shall be the peace of thy children." (Isaiah 54:13) 'Taught' is *didaktoi* (G1318, John 6:45), which the STEPBible lexicon notes is used of persons taught by God here, with the genitive of the source of the teaching, and in the Greek Old Testament at Isaiah 54:13. The drawing is not force applied to an unwilling body; it is the Father teaching the heart, so that 'every man therefore that hath heard, and hath learned of the Father, cometh unto me'. 'Hath learned' is *mathōn* (G3129, John 6:45, V-2AAP-NSM). All who are taught come; none who are taught stay away.

Verse 46 guards against a misunderstanding. Being taught by the Father does not mean seeing him in a private vision. Only 'he which is of God', the Son, 'hath seen the Father' (compare John 1:18). The Father teaches by pointing to the Son.

**Asks:** Do you murmur when Jesus says what you did not expect, or argue that you know all about him already? If you have come to him, thank the Father who drew you; you did not find your own way. And if you long for someone to come, pray that the Father would teach them, and tell them about his Son.

#### John 6:47–51 · The Living Bread
**Says:** "Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that believeth on me hath everlasting life." (John 6:47) "I am that bread of life." (John 6:48) "Your fathers did eat manna in the wilderness, and are dead." (John 6:49) "This is the bread which cometh down from heaven, that a man may eat thereof, and not die." (John 6:50) "I am the living bread which came down from heaven: if any man eat of this bread, he shall live for ever: and the bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world." (John 6:51) *Thereof* means of it.

**Means:** Verse 47 is the plainest sentence in the discourse, and the key to what follows: 'He that believeth on me hath everlasting life'. The one who believes, *pisteuōn* (G4100, John 6:47), has, *echei* (G2192, John 6:47, V-PAI-3S), present tense, everlasting life. Everything Jesus says next about eating must be read in the light of this sentence.

'I am that bread of life' repeats verse 35. Then the contrast with the manna: 'Your fathers did eat manna in the wilderness, and are dead'. The verb *ephagon* (G5315, John 6:49, V-AAI-3P) is the ordinary aorist of 'eat'. The manna kept a generation alive in the wilderness, and every one of them died, most of them in the wilderness itself. This bread is different: a man may 'eat thereof, and not die', *phagē* (G5315, John 6:50, V-AAS-3S).

Verse 51 is the turning point of the chapter. 'I am the living bread', *zōn* (G2198, John 6:51, V-PAP-NSM), bread that is alive and gives life. Then comes the promise: 'if any man eat of this bread, he shall live for ever'. And now, for the first time, Jesus says what the bread is: 'the bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world'. 'Flesh' is *sarx* (G4561, John 6:51), the word John used for the incarnation: 'the Word was made flesh' (John 1:14). The future 'I will give' looks ahead to the cross. And 'for the life of the world' uses *huper* (G5228, John 6:51), 'on behalf of', the preposition of sacrifice that John uses again of the good shepherd laying down his life 'for the sheep' (John 10:11) and of Caiaphas's prophecy that one man should die 'for the people' (John 11:50). The bread is Jesus' own body given up to death. That is what the manna could never be.

**Asks:** Do you believe on the Son? Then you have everlasting life now. And do you see what it cost? The bread of life is not a teaching but a body given for you. Eat, that is, believe, and live.

#### John 6:52–55 · Except Ye Eat the Flesh of the Son of Man
**Says:** "The Jews therefore strove among themselves, saying, How can this man give us his flesh to eat?" (John 6:52) "Then Jesus said unto them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you." (John 6:53) "Whoso eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood, hath eternal life; and I will raise him up at the last day." (John 6:54) "For my flesh is meat indeed, and my blood is drink indeed." (John 6:55) *Strove* means argued fiercely; *whoso* means whoever; *indeed* means truly.

**Means:** The murmuring becomes a fight: the Jews 'strove among themselves'. 'How can this man give us his flesh to eat?' Like Nicodemus with 'How can a man be born when he is old?' (John 3:4), they take Jesus' words at the crudest level, and John lets the misunderstanding stand so that Jesus can go further.

Jesus does not soften the words; he sharpens them. 'Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you.' Now there is blood as well as flesh, and to a Jewish ear this was shocking: the law forbade eating blood, because 'the life of the flesh is in the blood' (Leviticus 17:11). Flesh and blood separated speak of death, a sacrifice. The title 'the Son of man' points to the one who must be 'lifted up' (John 3:14), who will give his life. The verbs are *phagēte* (G5315, John 6:53, V-AAS-2P) and *piēte* (G4095, John 6:53), 'eat' and 'drink', in the aorist, and the warning is absolute: without this, 'ye have no life in you'.

Then the promise, and a change of verb. 'Whoso eateth my flesh', *trōgōn* (G5176, John 6:54, V-PAP-NSM). The STEPBible lexicon notes that *trōgō* was used first of animals gnawing or munching, then of people eating raw food, and in the everyday Greek of the time 'simply to eat'. Some teachers make much of the change, reading it as crunching or chewing and pressing a literal sense; others note that John uses the same verb in John 13:18 where Psalm 41:9 has the ordinary word for eating. The present participle, the one who keeps eating, matches the present participle for believing in John 6:35, 40, and 47. The word occurs 6 times in the New Testament, four of them in John 6:54–58. The promise is the one already made to faith: 'hath eternal life; and I will raise him up at the last day' (compare John 6:40, 47).

'For my flesh is meat indeed, and my blood is drink indeed.' 'Indeed' is *alēthōs* (G230, John 6:55), truly, really. Ordinary food keeps a body alive for a day. His flesh and blood, given in death, are real food and real drink because they give eternal life.

**Asks:** Jesus did not make his words easier when people were offended. Have you been offended by the cross, by the idea that you need a sacrifice, that your life depends on someone else's death? There is no life apart from his flesh and blood. Receive him, and keep receiving him.

#### John 6:56–59 · Dwelleth in Me, and I in Him
**Says:** "He that eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood, dwelleth in me, and I in him." (John 6:56) "As the living Father hath sent me, and I live by the Father: so he that eateth me, even he shall live by me." (John 6:57) "This is that bread which came down from heaven: not as your fathers did eat manna, and are dead: he that eateth of this bread shall live for ever." (John 6:58) "These things said he in the synagogue, as he taught in Capernaum." (John 6:59) *Dwelleth* means abides, remains.

**Means:** Eating his flesh and drinking his blood brings union: the one who feeds on him 'dwelleth in me, and I in him'. The verb is *menei* (G3306, John 6:56, V-PAI-3S), from *menō*, to abide or remain, one of John's great words. The STEPBible lexicon lists this verse, with John 15:4, for Christ abiding in the believer and the believer in Christ. John 15 will develop it: 'Abide in me, and I in you' (John 15:4). Food becomes part of the one who eats it; Christ received by faith makes his home in the believer, and the believer in him.

Verse 57 describes a chain of life. 'The living Father', *zōn* (G2198, John 6:57), sent the Son; the Son lives 'by the Father', *zō* (G2198, John 6:57, V-PAI-1S), because of him; and 'he that eateth me, even he shall live by me', *zēsetai* (G2198, John 6:57, V-FDI-3S). As the Son draws his life from the Father, so the believer draws his life from the Son. Notice that Jesus now says simply 'he that eateth me'. The flesh and blood are not something separate from the person; to feed on them is to feed on him.

Verse 58 sums up the discourse, returning to where it began: the bread from heaven, the manna, the fathers who died, and the bread that gives life 'for ever'. And John closes the scene with a note of place: 'These things said he in the synagogue, as he taught in Capernaum'. The *synagōgē* (G4864, John 6:59) was the place where Moses was read every sabbath (Acts 15:21). The ruins of a later synagogue can be seen at Capernaum today, and some archaeologists think that the basalt foundations beneath it may belong to the synagogue of Jesus' day (verify).

**Asks:** Are you living by Christ as he lived by the Father, drawing on him daily as a body draws on bread? Abiding is not an extra for advanced Christians; it is what eating means. Come to him today, and stay.

#### John 6:60–65 · An Hard Saying
**Says:** "Many therefore of his disciples, when they had heard this, said, This is an hard saying; who can hear it?" (John 6:60) "When Jesus knew in himself that his disciples murmured at it, he said unto them, Doth this offend you?" (John 6:61) "What and if ye shall see the Son of man ascend up where he was before?" (John 6:62) "It is the spirit that quickeneth; the flesh profiteth nothing: the words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life." (John 6:63) "But there are some of you that believe not. For Jesus knew from the beginning who they were that believed not, and who should betray him." (John 6:64) "And he said, Therefore said I unto you, that no man can come unto me, except it were given unto him of my Father." (John 6:65) *Offend* means cause to stumble; *quickeneth* means gives life; *profiteth* means benefits, helps.

**Means:** Now the objection comes from inside: 'many therefore of his disciples'. 'This is an hard saying', *sklēros* (G4642, John 6:60, A-NSM). The STEPBible lexicon defines the word as 'hard to the touch, rough, harsh', and lists this verse for a hard word. The problem was not that the saying was hard to understand but that it was hard to accept: 'who can hear it?' means 'who can listen to this?'

Jesus knows 'in himself' that they are murmuring, *gonguzousin* (G1111, John 6:61), the wilderness word again, and asks, 'Doth this offend you?' The verb is *skandalizei* (G4624, John 6:61, V-PAI-3S), which the STEPBible lexicon explains as 'to put a snare or stumbling-block in the way', used in the New Testament always of what hinders right conduct or thought. Paul will call Christ crucified 'unto the Jews a stumblingblock' (1 Corinthians 1:23).

'What and if ye shall see the Son of man ascend up where he was before?' The sentence is unfinished in Greek, and it can be read two ways. If they stumble at his coming down from heaven and giving his flesh, will they stumble more when they see him go back up, *anabainonta* (G305, John 6:62)? Or: when they see him ascend, will they then understand that he was speaking of something heavenly, not of cannibalism? Either way, it confirms his origin: he will go 'where he was before', *proteron* (G4387, John 6:62), and it points to his death, resurrection, and ascension as the key to the discourse.

Then the explanation: 'It is the spirit that quickeneth', *zōopoioun* (G2227, John 6:63, V-PAP-NSN); 'the flesh profiteth nothing', *ōphelei* (G5623, John 6:63). Jesus is not contradicting what he has just said about his own flesh, which he will give for the life of the world (John 6:51). He is saying that flesh understood fleshly, flesh chewed with the teeth, or human nature left to itself, gives no life. Life comes by the Spirit, and 'the words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life'. The *rhēmata* (G4487, John 6:63), spoken words, are the means by which the Spirit gives life; they must be received by faith.

'But there are some of you that believe not.' Jesus 'knew from the beginning' who did not believe, and 'who should betray him', *paradōsōn* (G3860, John 6:64, V-FAP-NSM), the one who would hand him over. And he repeats verse 44 in another form: 'no man can come unto me, except it were given unto him of my Father', *dedomenon* (G1325, John 6:65, V-RPP-NSN). Coming to Christ is given.

**Asks:** Which of Jesus' sayings do you find hard, and why? Is it that you cannot understand it, or that you do not want to accept it? Ask the Spirit who gives life to make his words life to you, and do not treat your offence as the final word.

#### John 6:66–69 · To Whom Shall We Go?
**Says:** "From that time many of his disciples went back, and walked no more with him." (John 6:66) "Then said Jesus unto the twelve, Will ye also go away?" (John 6:67) "Then Simon Peter answered him, Lord, to whom shall we go? thou hast the words of eternal life." (John 6:68) "And we believe and are sure that thou art that Christ, the Son of the living God." (John 6:69) *Are sure* means have come to know.

**Means:** 'From that time', or 'for this reason' (the Greek can mean either), 'many of his disciples went back, and walked no more with him'. 'Walked', *periepatoun* (G4043, John 6:66, V-IAI-3P), is the ordinary word for going about with a teacher; they stopped following. The crowd that wanted to make him king has shrunk to a handful.

Jesus turns to 'the twelve', named as a group here for the first time in John, and asks, 'Will ye also go away?' The question is framed with *mē* (G3361, John 6:67), which in Greek expects the answer 'no': 'You do not want to go away too, do you?' It is not a threat but an invitation to declare themselves. 'Will' is *thelete* (G2309, John 6:67, V-PAI-2P), do you wish.

Peter answers for all of them, and his answer is the chapter's climax. 'Lord, to whom shall we go?' It is not a boast of understanding; Peter does not claim that the saying was easy. It is a confession that there is no alternative: 'thou hast the words of eternal life'. Peter has heard exactly what Jesus said in verse 63: the *rhēmata* (G4487, John 6:68) of *zōēs* (G2222, John 6:68), words of life. Then the confession: 'we believe and are sure', *pepisteukamen* (G4100, John 6:69, V-RAI-1P) and *egnōkamen* (G1097, John 6:69, V-RAI-1P), both perfects: we have come to believe and have come to know, and we stand there now. 'That thou art that Christ, the Son of the living God': the KJV here follows the Textus Receptus; many modern editions read a shorter title (see the Deep Dive). Either reading confesses him as the one sent from the living God, the Messiah.

This is John's equivalent of Peter's confession at Caesarea Philippi in the other Gospels (Matthew 16:16). In John it comes at the moment when faith costs something, when the crowds have gone.

**Asks:** When others walk away from Jesus, and when you are tempted to, ask Peter's question honestly: to whom else would you go? Name the alternatives, and weigh them against the one who has the words of eternal life. Then say, with Peter, what you have come to believe and know.

#### John 6:70–71 · One of You Is a Devil
**Says:** "Jesus answered them, Have not I chosen you twelve, and one of you is a devil?" (John 6:70) "He spake of Judas Iscariot the son of Simon: for he it was that should betray him, being one of the twelve." (John 6:71)

**Means:** Jesus answers Peter's 'we' with a warning. 'Have not I chosen you twelve', *exelexamēn* (G1586, John 6:70, V-AMI-1S), I chose for myself. The twelve did not choose him; he chose them (John 15:16). 'And one of you is a devil', *diabolos* (G1228, John 6:70). The STEPBible lexicon gives the basic sense 'slanderous, accusing falsely', and, as a noun, 'a slanderer', and in most New Testament uses the Accuser, the Devil himself. Here Jesus uses it of a man: Judas is not Satan, but he is doing Satan's work, and later 'Satan entered into him' (John 13:27).

John names him: 'Judas Iscariot the son of Simon', and gives the reason: 'he it was that should betray him, being one of the twelve'. The last words of the chapter are the saddest: 'one of the twelve'. Judas had eaten the multiplied bread, carried a basket of fragments, heard the bread of life discourse, and stayed when others left. He was chosen for the twelve, and he was not a believer (John 6:64). His presence does not contradict John 6:37–39. Jesus later prays that none of those the Father gave him is lost 'but the son of perdition; that the scripture might be fulfilled' (John 17:12), and the Scripture he has in mind is Psalm 41:9, the friend 'which did eat of my bread'. Judas had a place among the twelve; he never came to Christ in faith.

**Asks:** It is possible to be very near Jesus and not belong to him. Do not rest on your place in the group, your service, or your history; examine whether you have come to him and feed on him. And take comfort: Jesus knew Judas from the beginning, and nothing about his people surprises him or defeats his keeping.

### 🧨 Cultural Landmines and Misconceptions
**'Bread' as a side dish.** For many modern readers bread is one food among many, and often one to avoid. In Jesus' world bread was the staple of life; to 'eat bread' meant to have a meal, and for the poor, bread was most of what they ate. When Jesus says 'I am the bread of life', he is not claiming to be an extra in life but the thing without which life cannot go on: "Give us this day our daily bread." (Matthew 6:11)

**Cannibalism.** Early Christians were accused by some pagans of eating human flesh, a slander that seems to have grown from misunderstanding talk of eating Christ's body (verify the ancient sources, such as the charges answered by the second-century apologists). The crowd in John 6:52 makes the same mistake. Jesus' words are not about chewing his literal flesh, which 'profiteth nothing' taken that way (John 6:63); they are about receiving him, crucified and risen, by faith, and living by him.

**'The Jews' as a people.** John 6:41 and 52 say that 'the Jews' murmured and strove. Here the phrase means Galileans in the synagogue at Capernaum who objected to Jesus, not the Jewish people as a whole; Jesus, the twelve, and the lad with the loaves were all Jews, and many of those who turned back in John 6:66 were his own disciples. The sins the chapter exposes (following for loaves, murmuring, stumbling at the cross) belong to every people and every church.

**'God will never give you more than you can handle.'** Many people think this saying is in the Bible. The disciples were given a storm they could not handle, in the dark, without Jesus in the boat (John 6:17–19). What the chapter shows is not that we can always cope, but that he comes to us when we cannot, and says 'It is I; be not afraid'.

**The miracle as magic.** Some modern retellings present Jesus as a wonder-worker who could produce food on demand, and some prosperity preaching presents him as a supplier. John calls the feeding a sign: it points past itself to the one who gives it, and Jesus refuses to repeat it on request (John 6:30–32).

**'Calvinism' and 'Arminianism' as labels.** John 6:37 and 6:44 are often used as weapons in arguments between Christians who hold different views of grace and free will. Both sides take the verses seriously and appeal to them; the Debate Corner sets out the positions fairly. Neither side should use John 6 to question the faith of the other, and both should hear Jesus' own invitation in the same chapter: 'him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out' (John 6:37).

## 🔴 Tier 3 — Expert
### 🏗️ Theological Framework
John 6 is one of the densest chapters in the New Testament for systematic theology. Read through the Reformed conviction that Scripture interprets Scripture, it bears on at least six areas.

*The person of Christ: descent, incarnation, and deity.* The phrase 'down from heaven' occurs 7 times in this chapter (John 6:33, 38, 41, 42, 50, 51, 58), always of Jesus or of the bread that he is, twice in his hearers' report of his claim; and he adds that he will 'ascend up where he was before' (John 6:62). Those are words of personal pre-existence: the one speaking in the synagogue existed before his birth, with the Father, and 'came down'. The murmurers' objection ('whose father and mother we know', John 6:42) is precisely the objection the doctrine of the incarnation answers: the eternal Son took flesh (John 1:14) without ceasing to be what he was. The walking on the sea adds a sign of deity to the words: the one who treads the waves (Job 9:8) and says *egō* (G1473, John 6:20) *eimi* (G1510, John 6:20) to frightened men does what the Old Testament reserves to God.

*The atonement.* 'The bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world' (John 6:51). Three features mark this as a statement about the cross: the future tense (the giving is still to come), the preposition *huper* (G5228, John 6:51), which the STEPBible lexicon lists under 'Christ giving his life for man's redemption' with John 10:15, and the separation of flesh and blood (John 6:53–56), which is the language of sacrifice (Leviticus 17:11). The Reformed tradition has read the verse as teaching that Christ's death is the substance on which faith feeds: not his example, nor his teaching alone, but his body given and his blood shed as a sacrifice for sin (Westminster Larger Catechism, questions 168 and 170, verify).

*Union with Christ.* 'He that eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood, dwelleth in me, and I in him' (John 6:56), and 'he that eateth me, even he shall live by me' (John 6:57). Calvin made union with Christ the hinge of the application of redemption (*Institutes* 3.1.1, verify): everything Christ has done for us remains useless to us as long as we are outside him. John 6 is one of the texts on which that doctrine rests. Faith does not merely believe facts about Christ; it receives Christ himself, and the life that flows from the Father to the Son flows from the Son to the believer (John 6:57).

*Effectual calling and perseverance.* John 6:37–45 and 6:65 are the classic texts for what the Reformed confessions call effectual calling: the Father gives a people to the Son, draws them by teaching them inwardly through his word, so that they come freely, and the Son keeps them and raises them up (Westminster Confession, chapter 10, section 1, and chapter 17; Canons of Dort, third and fourth heads of doctrine, verify each). The chapter also shows the other side: no one is kept out who comes, and those who do not come are responsible for their unbelief (John 6:36, 64). The Debate Corner sets out the alternatives.

*The sacraments.* Whether or not John 6:51–58 speaks directly of the Lord's Supper (the Council's first question), the chapter describes the reality the Supper signifies. The Reformed confessions teach that worthy receivers of the Supper feed on Christ crucified spiritually, by faith, really and truly, though not carnally (Westminster Confession, chapter 29, section 7, paraphrased, verify); the Heidelberg Catechism (question 76, verify) explains eating the crucified body of Christ as embracing his suffering and death with a believing heart and being united more and more to his body by the Spirit. John 6 is therefore rightly read at the Table, though it is not primarily a text about the Table.

*Eschatology: life now and the last day.* The believer 'hath everlasting life' now (John 6:47, 54), and Christ 'will raise him up at the last day' (John 6:39, 40, 44, 54). As in John 5:24–29, present possession and future resurrection belong together; neither swallows the other.

### 📚 Scholarly Insights
- **Augustine**, *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (Tractates 24–27, verify): reads the five loaves as the five books of Moses and the barley as the coarse husk of the old covenant that hides its food (verify the particulars). On John 6:29 he makes the famous point, in substance, that to believe in Christ is already to eat the living bread (Tractate 25, verify). On John 6:44 he argues that the Father draws not by compulsion but by delight, so that the will is drawn by what it loves (Tractate 26, verify).
- **John Chrysostom**, *Homilies on the Gospel of John* (Homilies 42–47, verify): presses the crowd's fickleness and their love of the belly as a warning to his hearers, and reads the discourse on flesh and blood with reference to the mysteries, the church's name for the sacraments (verify).
- **Thomas Aquinas**, *Commentary on the Gospel of John* (chapter 6, verify): distinguishes a spiritual eating of Christ by faith and love from a sacramental eating, and holds that the passage speaks of both (verify).
- **Martin Luther**: in his controversy with Zwingli over the Supper, Luther denied that John 6 speaks of the sacrament, while Zwingli used 'the flesh profiteth nothing' (John 6:63) against a bodily presence; Luther answered that the verse refers to our flesh, not Christ's (see Luther, *That These Words of Christ, This Is My Body, Still Stand Firm*, 1527, verify).
- **John Calvin**, *Commentary on the Gospel According to John*: holds that the discourse does not speak of the Supper as such but of the continual communication of Christ's life that we receive by faith, which the Supper later seals; he distinguishes eating from believing as effect from cause, so that we eat Christ by believing (verify). On John 6:44 he argues that the drawing is effectual, not a mere offer that the will may take or leave (verify).
- **J. C. Ryle**, *Expository Thoughts on the Gospels* (John): argues at length that John 6:51–58 does not refer to the Lord's Supper, pointing out that the Supper had not yet been instituted and that the verses would then shut out of life every believer who never received it (verify). He treats John 6:37 as one of the sweetest invitations in the Bible (verify).
- **B. F. Westcott**, *The Gospel According to St. John* (1880): holds that the discourse speaks of the truth that the Eucharist embodies rather than of the Eucharist itself (verify).
- **Leon Morris**, *The Gospel According to John* (New International Commentary, 1971): argues that the sacramental reading is not the primary one and that eating and drinking in John 6 are vivid ways of speaking of faith in the crucified Christ (verify).
- **D. A. Carson**, *The Gospel According to John* (Pillar New Testament Commentary, 1991): reads John 6:53–54 in parallel with John 6:40, concluding that eating and drinking are metaphors for believing; allows that John's first readers would have heard eucharistic overtones; and defends the effectual reading of John 6:37–44 (verify each).
- **Raymond E. Brown**, *The Gospel According to John* (Anchor Bible, 1966): sees John 6:35–50 as sapiential, about Jesus' revelation, and John 6:51–58 as a eucharistic section, possibly a later addition by the Gospel's editor drawn from Johannine eucharistic teaching (verify).
- **Rudolf Bultmann**, *The Gospel of John* (German original 1941): assigned John 6:51c–58 to an 'ecclesiastical redactor' who added sacramental teaching to the evangelist's work (verify). No manuscript lacks the verses, and the discourse reads as a unity.
- **John Wesley**, *Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament* (1755), on John 6:44: reads the Father's drawing as universal and resistible, the grace that goes before every person's response (verify).

### ⚔️ Debate Corner
**The question:** What does Jesus mean when he says 'No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him' (John 6:44)?

**Position 1 · Universal, enabling, resistible drawing.** Jacob Arminius and the Remonstrants (1610, verify), John Wesley (verify), and many evangelicals today hold that the Father draws every person, or every person who hears the gospel, by a grace that goes before and enables faith (often called prevenient grace), but that this grace can be resisted. They point to John 12:32, where Jesus uses the same verb: 'I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me'. They point to the universal invitations in this chapter ('every one which seeth the Son, and believeth on him', John 6:40; 'if any man eat of this bread', John 6:51), to God's love for the world (John 3:16), to his will that 'all men' be saved (1 Timothy 2:4), and to Jesus' lament that Jerusalem 'would not' (Matthew 23:37) and Stephen's charge, 'ye do always resist the Holy Ghost' (Acts 7:51). On this view, those whom the Father 'giveth' the Son (John 6:37) are those he foreknows will believe. They want to guard God's universal love and human responsibility, and they are right that no one in John 6 is kept out against his will.

**Position 2 · Drawing as persuasion by the word.** Pelagius in the fifth century (verify), and in a different form the nineteenth-century revivalist Charles Finney (*Lectures on Systematic Theology*, verify), held that human beings retain the natural ability to turn to God, so that God's drawing is his moral persuasion: the truth presented, the gospel preached, the motives set before the mind. They note that the STEPBible lexicon's short gloss for *helkuō* includes 'persuade', and that John 6:45 speaks of hearing and learning. They want to leave no excuse for unbelief and to press every hearer to decide now.

**Position 3 · Effectual drawing: the Father's inward teaching that brings all it draws.** Augustine (verify), Calvin (verify), the Canons of Dort (third and fourth heads, verify), and the Westminster Confession (chapter 10, verify) read the verse as describing the Father's sovereign, inward work, by his word and Spirit, which makes the unwilling willing, so that everyone he draws comes, freely and gladly, and is raised up at the last day.

**Most consistent with Reformed hermeneutics.** Scripture interpreting Scripture, beginning with the verse itself, favours Position 3. (1) The person drawn in John 6:44 is the person raised: 'except the Father which hath sent me draw him: and I will raise him up at the last day'. The pronoun is the same, and in this chapter being raised up at the last day is the promise to believers (John 6:39–40, 54). If the drawing were given to all, all would be raised to life, which John 5:29 denies. (2) The context says the same three times: 'All that the Father giveth me shall come to me' (John 6:37); 'Every man therefore that hath heard, and hath learned of the Father, cometh unto me' (John 6:45); 'no man can come unto me, except it were given unto him of my Father' (John 6:65). Every one taught comes; the giving and the coming have the same extent. (3) Jesus says these things to explain why his hearers do not believe (John 6:36, 41–43, 64–65); a drawing given equally to believers and unbelievers would explain nothing. (4) The word itself, in the STEPBible lexicon's full entry, means 'to draw, lead, impel', and its other uses in John describe a sword drawn and a net dragged to shore full of fish (John 18:10; John 21:6, 11): drawing that arrives. The short gloss 'persuade' does not carry Position 2; and John 6:45 describes not persuasion that may fail but teaching that always results in coming. (5) John 12:32 is the strongest text for Position 1, but its context is the arrival of Greeks (John 12:20–23), and 'all men' there is best read as all kinds of people, Gentile as well as Jew, drawn by the lifted-up Christ, as Jesus says elsewhere that he has 'other sheep' who 'shall hear my voice' (John 10:16); read as every individual without exception, it would prove universal salvation, which none of the positions hold.

Position 1 has a real insight, and the Reformed position keeps it: the invitation of John 6 is sincere and universal, and no one is turned away who comes (John 6:37). Unbelief is never excused by the doctrine of drawing: the crowd saw and did not believe (John 6:36), and it was their own fault (John 5:40). The Reformed tradition holds both God's sovereign drawing and man's full responsibility, as Jesus does in this chapter, without claiming to explain everything about how they fit.

**Pastoral Warning:** This debate has divided churches, and it can harm a congregation from either side. A church that holds Position 3 coldly can slide into fatalism ('if they are elect they will come'), stop evangelising, and breed anxious believers who search their feelings for proof that they were drawn. A church that holds Position 1 or 2 carelessly can slide into techniques that manipulate decisions, and into a quiet pride that credits the believer's own choice. Teach John 6:44 as Jesus taught it: in the same breath as John 6:37. Let it humble every believer ('I came because I was drawn'), fuel prayer for the lost ('Father, draw them'), send the church out to preach (the Father draws by teaching through the word, John 6:45), and assure every trembling seeker that 'him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out'. And do not use the verse to question the salvation of brothers and sisters who read it differently while trusting the same Christ.

### 🔤 Original Language Deep Dive
Every Greek and Hebrew definition below comes from one source: the brief lexicon published by STEPBible.org (Tyndale House, Cambridge), whose Greek entries are drawn largely from G. Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament*. It is called 'the STEPBible lexicon' below.

**Bread.** *Artos* (G740, John 6:35, N-NSM). The STEPBible lexicon defines it as 'bread, a loaf', notes that in the Greek Old Testament it chiefly translates the Hebrew for bread, and lists a metaphorical use, 'the bread of God, the bread of life', referring to Christ, at John 6:33 and 35. It also notes the idiom 'to eat bread' for 'to have a meal'. The word 'bread' occurs 17 times in the KJV of this chapter.

**True.** *Alēthinon* (G228, John 6:32, A-ASM), from *alēthinos*, which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'true, in the sense of real, ideal, genuine', distinct from *alēthēs*, true as opposed to false. Its list of occurrences reads like a summary of John's Christology: the true light (John 1:9), the true worshippers (John 4:23), the true bread (John 6:32), the true vine (John 15:1), the only true God (John 17:3). The manna was not a lie; it was the copy, and Christ is the original.

**Sealed.** *Esphragisen* (G4972, John 6:27, V-AAI-3S), aorist of *sphragizō*. The STEPBible lexicon lists four uses of sealing: for security, for concealment, for distinction, and for authentication, and places John 6:27 with John 3:33 under authentication. The aorist points to a definite act; many interpreters take it of the Spirit descending and remaining on him, as John the Baptist bore witness (John 1:32–34), though John does not say so here.

**Come and believe.** *Erchomenos* (G2064, John 6:35, V-PNP-NSM) and *pisteuōn* (G4100, John 6:35, V-PAP-NSM) are present participles, and so is *theōrōn* (G2334, John 6:40, V-PAP-NSM); the future *hēxei* (G2240, John 6:37, V-FAI-3S), 'shall come', states what will certainly happen. The negatives with *hunger* and *thirst* in John 6:35 and with *cast out* in John 6:37 are the double negative *ou mē*, with *mē* (G3361, John 6:37), the strongest way Greek can deny a future possibility.

**Cast out.** *Ekbalō* (G1544, John 6:37, V-2AAS-1S), from *ekballō*, 'to drive, cast or send out, to expel'. The STEPBible lexicon lists this verse where the verb is followed by *exō* (G1854, John 6:37), 'out'. John uses the same verb for the healed blind man who was 'cast out' by the authorities (John 9:34–35). The world may cast out those who come to Christ; he never will.

**Draw.** *Helkusē* (G1670, John 6:44, V-AAS-3S), from *helkuō* (the STEPBible lexicon lists *helkō* and the Hellenistic form *helkuō*). Its full entry gives 'to draw', with things (John 18:10, a sword; John 21:6, a net) and with persons, 'before' magistrates (Acts 16:19; James 2:6), and metaphorically 'to draw, lead, impel' at John 6:44 and John 12:32. In the Greek Old Testament it renders the Hebrew verb for drawing out or pulling, among others. The Septuagint uses the verb in Jeremiah 31:3 (verify the Greek there), where the KJV has 'with lovingkindness have I drawn thee'. The word occurs 8 times in the New Testament, five of them in John.

**Taught of God.** *Didaktoi* (G1318, John 6:45, A-NPM), from *didaktos*, 'taught', which the STEPBible lexicon lists with a genitive of the source of teaching, 'taught of God', at John 6:45, noting its use in the Greek of Isaiah 54:13. The drawing of verse 44 is explained by the teaching of verse 45, and the result of both is *erchetai* (G2064, John 6:45, V-PNI-3S), 'cometh'.

**Eat: two verbs.** From John 6:49 to 6:53 the verb is *phagein* (G5315, John 6:52, V-AAN), the aorist of the ordinary verb 'to eat', which the STEPBible lexicon traces to classical *esthiō*. From John 6:54 to 6:58 it is *trōgōn* (G5176, John 6:54, V-PAP-NSM), from *trōgō*. The STEPBible lexicon gives its history: first 'to gnaw, munch, crunch', of animals; then 'to eat raw food, as vegetables, nuts', of people; and 'in late vernacular, simply to eat', listing Matthew 24:38 ('eating and drinking' before the flood), John 6:54–58, and John 13:18, where it notes that the Greek Old Testament of Psalm 41:9 has the ordinary verb. The verb appears 6 times in the New Testament. Two observations follow. First, the switch is real, and some interpreters argue it stresses a physical, even realistic, eating. Second, the lexicon's evidence that *trōgō* had become an everyday word for eating, and John's own use of it in John 13:18 for the betrayer's ordinary meal, cautions against building a doctrine on the change. A likely reason for it is John's preference for the present tense in 6:54–58, since the aorist of *trōgō* was not in common use (verify). Either way, the present participle 'he that eateth' matches 'he that believeth' (John 6:35, 40, 47).

**Flesh.** *Sarx* (G4561, John 6:51, N-NSF). The STEPBible lexicon's first sense is 'the soft substance of the animal body', and under that sense it lists, as a mystical use, eating the flesh of the Son of man in John 6:52–56. The same word marks the incarnation (John 1:14) and, in a different sense, 'the flesh' that 'profiteth nothing' (John 6:63). John's point turns on the difference: Christ's flesh given in death gives life; flesh as mere human nature, or as bodily eating without the Spirit, gives none.

**Murmur.** *Gonguzete* (G1111, John 6:43, V-PAM-2P), from *gonguzō*, 'to mutter, murmur'. The STEPBible lexicon notes that in the Greek Old Testament it chiefly translates the Hebrew *lun* (H3885), which the lexicon glosses 'to grumble, complain, murmur', the verb of Israel's complaining in the wilderness (Exodus 16:7–8; Numbers 14:27). The same Greek verb returns at John 6:61 for the disciples. The manna story and the bread of life discourse share the same sin.

**Manna.** *Manna* (G3131, John 6:31, N-ASN-HEB), an indeclinable word of Hebrew origin. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 6:31 and 6:49, Hebrews 9:4, and the 'hidden manna' of Revelation 2:17. Behind it stands the Hebrew *man* (H4478), for which the lexicon gives two entries: the name of the bread from heaven that fed Israel for forty years, and an interrogative, 'What?', 'What is it?'. That is the wordplay of Exodus 16:15, where Israel asked what the substance was; the KJV renders 'It is manna', taking the word as a name, and many modern versions render the question. The bread from heaven began as a question, and in John 6 the question is answered.

**Hard, offend.** *Sklēros* (G4642, John 6:60, A-NSM), 'hard to the touch, rough, harsh', and metaphorically of a hard saying; *skandalizei* (G4624, John 6:61, V-PAI-3S), 'to put a snare or stumbling-block in the way', in the New Testament always of what hinders right conduct or belief. The disciples' problem was not the saying's obscurity but its offence.

**Devil.** *Diabolos* (G1228, John 6:70, A-NSM), an adjective used as a noun. The STEPBible lexicon gives 'slanderous, accusing falsely', then 'a slanderer', and most often 'Satan, the Accuser, the Devil', noting that in the Greek Old Testament it chiefly translates the Hebrew *satan*. Jesus calls Judas by the name of the one whose work he would do (John 13:2, 27).

**Textual notes.** Two places in this chapter read differently in many modern editions. (1) *John 6:69.* The KJV, following the Textus Receptus and the majority of later manuscripts, reads 'that Christ, the Son of the living God'. Several of the earliest manuscripts read a shorter confession, 'the Holy One of God' (verify the witnesses), and many modern editions follow them. Those who prefer the shorter reading suggest that scribes brought Peter's words into line with Matthew 16:16; those who defend the longer point to its wide attestation. (2) *John 6:47.* The KJV reads 'He that believeth on me'; some early manuscripts lack 'on me', reading 'He that believeth' (verify). The sense is the same, since the whole discourse is about believing on the Son (John 6:29, 35, 40). Neither difference touches any doctrine. This study teaches the text the KJV prints, and the differences should be explained calmly, without scorn for either side.

### 🚷 Preaching Mistakes to Avoid
- **Information without life:** a sermon rich in Passover background, the *trōgō* debate, and the history of eucharistic controversy that never invites the hungry in the room to come to Christ and eat.
- **Heat without light:** a sermon that thunders against the crowd's shallow motives, or against 'consumer Christians' in other churches, and never offers the bread of life, or asks the hearers why they follow Jesus.
- **The sharing sermon:** preaching the feeding as a lesson in generosity (the boy shared, so everyone shared) instead of a sign of who Jesus is. Sharing is good, but the text says he fed them.
- **Storm-calming as self-help:** preaching John 6:16–21 as 'Jesus will calm every storm in your life' without the Christ who walks on the sea and says 'It is I'. The comfort is his presence and his identity, not a promise of smooth water.
- **Using John 6:44 to discourage seekers:** preaching the drawing so that hearers wonder whether they are allowed to come. Jesus put John 6:37 before John 6:44; so should we.
- **Using John 6:53 as a club in sacramental debates:** turning the discourse into a proof text against other traditions, and missing its call to every hearer, of every tradition, to feed on Christ crucified by faith.
- **Preaching the manna without the Christ:** treating the wilderness story as a lesson in God's daily provision of groceries, and missing Jesus' own point that the manna fed people who died, and that he is the bread that gives life for ever.

## 🟣 Tier 4 — Christocentric, Humility, Life & Light
### 👑 Every Answer in Jesus
John 6 demolishes every ground of self-glory. Human resources are useless: two hundred pennyworth would not buy a mouthful each, and five barley loaves are nothing 'among so many' (John 6:7, 9). Human strength is useless: the disciples row for hours in the dark and get nowhere near the shore until Jesus comes. Human religion is useless on its own: the crowd wants to do the works of God and is told that the only work is to believe (John 6:28–29). Human ability is useless: 'No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him' (John 6:44), and 'the flesh profiteth nothing' (John 6:63). Even closeness to Jesus is no guarantee: one of the twelve is a devil (John 6:70).

And every good thing the chapter holds is found in him. Bread for the hungry is found in his hands (John 6:11). Safety in the storm is found in his presence (John 6:20). The true bread from heaven is found in his person (John 6:32–35). Life that never ends is found in his flesh given for the world (John 6:51). The Father's will is done by him, and none of his people is lost (John 6:38–39). Resurrection is in his voice (John 6:40, 54). The words of eternal life are his (John 6:68). This is what Paul means when he says of Christ, "In whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." (Colossians 2:3) The chapter humbles us by showing that we come to him as the crowd came, hungry and with nothing to pay, and as the disciples came, afraid in the dark; and it makes Christ glorious by showing that he is himself the bread, the Lord of the sea, and the one who keeps all whom the Father gives him.

## 📚 Doctrine
### Theology Proper · The Living Father Who Gives the True Bread
**Proposition:** God the Father is 'the living Father' (John 6:57), the source of life and the giver of every good gift, who fed Israel with manna and now gives his Son as the true bread from heaven. He has sealed the Son as his authentic messenger, sent him into the world, and wills that everyone who sees the Son and believes on him should have everlasting life.

**Textual Anchor:** "Then Jesus said unto them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Moses gave you not that bread from heaven; but my Father giveth you the true bread from heaven." (John 6:32), with *didōsin* (G1325, John 6:32, V-PAI-3S), a present, 'giveth'; "for him hath God the Father sealed" (John 6:27); "As the living Father hath sent me, and I live by the Father" (John 6:57).

**Cross-References:** "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." (John 3:16); "And he humbled thee, and suffered thee to hunger, and fed thee with manna, which thou knewest not, neither did thy fathers know; that he might make thee know that man doth not live by bread only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the LORD doth man live." (Deuteronomy 8:3); "For with thee is the fountain of life: in thy light shall we see light." (Psalm 36:9)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The God of the Old Testament is a stern God, and the Father of Jesus a different, kinder one.* Some since Marcion in the second century have set the two Testaments against each other; they want to honour the love shown in Christ. Jesus says the manna and the true bread come from the same giver: 'my Father giveth you the true bread from heaven' (John 6:32). (2) *God gives good things, but not himself.* Many think of God as a benefactor who sends blessings while remaining distant. The Father's great gift is his own Son (John 6:32–33; John 3:16). (3) *Moses and the law are the source of life.* The crowd honoured Moses as the giver of the manna. Jesus says Moses did not give it, and that the manna kept no one alive for ever (John 6:32, 49).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Gratitude for daily bread as the gift of a Father who gives better gifts still. Confidence in God's generosity, since he has already given his Son. And worship that looks past the gifts to the Giver.

### Christology · The Bread Who Came Down from Heaven
**Proposition:** Jesus Christ existed with the Father before his birth, and came down from heaven to do the Father's will. The man whose father and mother were known in Galilee is the eternal Son made flesh, who will ascend where he was before. He is not only the giver of the bread of life; he is the bread.

**Textual Anchor:** "For I came down from heaven, not to do mine own will, but the will of him that sent me." (John 6:38), with *katabebēka* (G2597, John 6:38, V-RAI-1S), a perfect; "What and if ye shall see the Son of man ascend up where he was before?" (John 6:62); "I am the bread of life" (John 6:35).

**Cross-References:** "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth." (John 1:14); "And had rained down manna upon them to eat, and had given them of the corn of heaven." (Psalm 78:24); "And did all drink the same spiritual drink: for they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them: and that Rock was Christ." (1 Corinthians 10:4)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus did not exist before his human conception; 'came down from heaven' means only that his mission was from God.* Socinians in the sixteenth century, and some modern unitarian and liberal theologians, read the phrase this way; they want to protect the unity of God and Jesus' real humanity. But Jesus says he will 'ascend up where he was before' (John 6:62), and the objectors in John 6:42 understood him to claim a heavenly origin that his known parentage seemed to deny. (2) *Jesus was a heavenly being only appearing to be human.* Docetists in the early church, embarrassed by a God who eats and suffers, denied his real flesh. Jesus says the bread he gives 'is my flesh' (John 6:51), the flesh that the Word 'was made' (John 1:14). (3) *Jesus is one bread among many.* Religious pluralism honours Jesus as one source of spiritual nourishment. He says he is 'the true bread' and that without eating his flesh 'ye have no life in you' (John 6:32, 53).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Wonder at the incarnation, that the eternal Son came down to be eaten, as it were, by sinners. Humility, because he came 'not to do mine own will'. And satisfaction: no other bread need be sought.

### Christology · It Is I: The Lord of the Sea
**Proposition:** Jesus is the Lord of creation, who walks on the sea as only God does, and who reveals himself to his frightened people with the words 'It is I'. His presence, not the absence of storms, is their safety, and when he is received the journey is ended.

**Textual Anchor:** "they see Jesus walking on the sea, and drawing nigh unto the ship: and they were afraid." (John 6:19), with *peripatounta* (G4043, John 6:19, V-PAP-ASM); "But he saith unto them, It is I; be not afraid." (John 6:20), with *egō* (G1473, John 6:20) *eimi* (G1510, John 6:20, V-PAI-1S).

**Cross-References:** "Which alone spreadeth out the heavens, and treadeth upon the waves of the sea." (Job 9:8); "Thy way is in the sea, and thy path in the great waters, and thy footsteps are not known." (Psalm 77:19); "And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM: and he said, Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, I AM hath sent me unto you." (Exodus 3:14)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The story is a misunderstanding: Jesus was walking on the shore, or on a sandbar.* Some rationalist interpreters since the eighteenth century have suggested this, wanting to keep Jesus within ordinary nature. John says the disciples had rowed twenty-five or thirty furlongs, well out on the lake, that they were afraid, and that they received him 'into the ship' (John 6:19, 21). (2) *Jesus is a great prophet but not divine.* Many admire Jesus without worshipping him. Walking on the sea is what Scripture reserves to God (Job 9:8), and Matthew records that the disciples worshipped him after it (Matthew 14:33). (3) *If Jesus is with us, there will be no storms.* Popular piety sometimes promises calm seas. The disciples were in the storm because they obeyed him (compare Mark 6:45); what he promised was himself.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Courage in fear, because the one who comes over the waves is the Lord. Worship, because he does what only God does. And patience in the dark, because 'Jesus was not come to them' was only the first half of the story.

### Pneumatology · The Spirit That Quickeneth
**Proposition:** The Holy Spirit is the giver of life. Human flesh, whether human nature left to itself or a merely bodily reception of holy things, profits nothing for eternal life; life comes by the Spirit, who works through the words of Christ, so that his words are spirit and life to those who believe.

**Textual Anchor:** "It is the spirit that quickeneth; the flesh profiteth nothing: the words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life." (John 6:63), with *zōopoioun* (G2227, John 6:63, V-PAP-NSN).

**Cross-References:** "That which is born of the flesh is flesh; and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit." (John 3:6); "Who also hath made us able ministers of the new testament; not of the letter, but of the spirit: for the letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life." (2 Corinthians 3:6); "And shall put my spirit in you, and ye shall live, and I shall place you in your own land: then shall ye know that I the LORD have spoken it, and performed it, saith the LORD." (Ezekiel 37:14)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Grace is conveyed by the outward act alone, whatever the state of the heart.* Some forms of popular sacramentalism, in many traditions, treat the physical reception of holy things as automatically life-giving; they want to honour the objectivity of God's gifts. Jesus says 'the flesh profiteth nothing' without the Spirit (John 6:63), and Judas ate the bread and was 'a devil' (John 6:70). (2) *The Spirit works apart from, or against, the written and spoken word of Christ.* Some enthusiasts in every age have sought the Spirit in private impressions rather than in Christ's words. Jesus joins them: 'the words that I speak unto you, they are spirit' (John 6:63). (3) *Spiritual life can be produced by human effort, technique, or willpower.* Many self-help spiritualities assume it. Jesus says it is the Spirit who quickens (John 6:63), and that coming to him is given (John 6:65).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Dependence on the Spirit in every reading of Scripture and every approach to the Table. Confidence in the preached and read word as the Spirit's instrument. And humility, since our own flesh profits nothing.

### Anthropology & Sin · No Man Can Come
**Proposition:** Fallen human beings cannot come to Christ by their own power. Left to themselves they seek from God only what satisfies the body, murmur against his word, take offence at the cross, and turn away. Their inability is not a lack of opportunity but a bondage of the will, for which they remain responsible, and which only the Father's drawing overcomes.

**Textual Anchor:** "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him: and I will raise him up at the last day." (John 6:44), with *dunatai* (G1410, John 6:44, V-PNI-3S), 'is able'; "Ye seek me, not because ye saw the miracles, but because ye did eat of the loaves" (John 6:26); "But there are some of you that believe not." (John 6:64)

**Cross-References:** "Because the carnal mind is enmity against God: for it is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be." (Romans 8:7); "But the natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God: for they are foolishness unto him: neither can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned." (1 Corinthians 2:14); "And ye will not come to me, that ye might have life." (John 5:40)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Pelagianism: people are fully able to turn to God without inward grace.* Its holders want to leave no excuse for sin. Jesus says 'No man can come' unless drawn (John 6:44) and repeats it (John 6:65). (2) *People reject Christ only because they lack information or evidence.* Many sceptics and some apologists assume this. The crowd had just eaten the multiplied bread and still asked for a sign (John 6:26, 30), and many disciples heard the clearest teaching and left (John 6:66). (3) *Inability cancels responsibility: if I cannot come, I cannot be blamed.* Some who hear John 6:44 draw this conclusion. Jesus charges the unbelieving with their unbelief (John 6:36, 64), and the inability he describes is an unwillingness (John 5:40), not a desire to come that God frustrates.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Humility, because no believer came by his own strength. Patience and prayer for the unbelieving, since argument alone will not bring them. And honest self-examination of our own motives for following Christ.

### Soteriology & Grace · Given, Drawn, and Kept
**Proposition:** Salvation is the work of the triune God from beginning to end. The Father gives a people to the Son; he draws them by inwardly teaching them through his word, so that they come to Christ freely; the Son receives every one who comes, will never cast any out, and will lose none of them, but raise them up at the last day. The invitation is sincere and universal: every one who sees the Son and believes has everlasting life.

**Textual Anchor:** "All that the Father giveth me shall come to me; and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out." (John 6:37), with *ekbalō* (G1544, John 6:37, V-2AAS-1S); "that of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing" (John 6:39); "Every man therefore that hath heard, and hath learned of the Father, cometh unto me." (John 6:45)

**Cross-References:** "My Father, which gave them me, is greater than all; and no man is able to pluck them out of my Father's hand." (John 10:29); "Moreover whom he did predestinate, them he also called: and whom he called, them he also justified: and whom he justified, them he also glorified." (Romans 8:30); "Being confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ:" (Philippians 1:6)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *God gives grace to all equally, and the difference between believer and unbeliever lies finally in the human will.* Arminian and Wesleyan theology holds this, wanting to guard God's universal love and human responsibility (see the Debate Corner). Jesus says all whom the Father gives do come, and all whom he draws are raised up (John 6:37, 44). (2) *True believers can finally fall away and be lost.* Many sincere Christians, reading warnings such as Hebrews 6:4–6, hold that salvation can be forfeited. Jesus says it is the Father's will that he 'should lose nothing' of all given to him (John 6:39), and he does the Father's will (John 6:38). Those who 'went back' (John 6:66) show, in John's own later words, 'that they were not all of us' (1 John 2:19). (3) *Election makes the gospel invitation insincere.* Some critics of the Reformed faith, and some hyper-Calvinists, draw this conclusion from opposite sides. Jesus holds John 6:37a and 6:37b together, and says 'every one which seeth the Son, and believeth on him, may have everlasting life' (John 6:40).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Assurance for the weak believer, who is kept by the Son's promise and not by the strength of his own grip. Freedom for the anxious seeker, who is told to come and will not be cast out. And bold evangelism, because the Father draws through the word we speak.

### Soteriology & Grace · His Flesh for the Life of the World, Received by Faith
**Proposition:** Christ gave his flesh and poured out his blood in death as a sacrifice on behalf of the world, and that sacrifice is the food of eternal life. Sinners receive it by faith: to believe on Christ crucified is to eat his flesh and drink his blood, and whoever so feeds on him has eternal life now, dwells in him, and lives by him.

**Textual Anchor:** "I am the living bread which came down from heaven: if any man eat of this bread, he shall live for ever: and the bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world." (John 6:51), with *huper* (G5228, John 6:51), 'on behalf of'; "Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that believeth on me hath everlasting life." (John 6:47); "He that eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood, dwelleth in me, and I in him." (John 6:56)

**Cross-References:** "Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed." (1 Peter 2:24); "I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me: and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself for me." (Galatians 2:20); "For the life of the flesh is in the blood: and I have given it to you upon the altar to make an atonement for your souls: for it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul." (Leviticus 17:11)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Christ's death was an example of love, not a sacrifice for sin.* Moral influence theories, associated with Peter Abelard in the twelfth century and many modern liberal theologians, want to stress the love the cross reveals. Jesus speaks of flesh and blood separated, given 'for the life of the world' (John 6:51, 53), the language of sacrifice (Leviticus 17:11). (2) *Eternal life is received by participating in a rite, apart from faith.* Some read John 6:53 as teaching that everyone who receives the sacrament has life, and everyone who does not is lost. The same promise is made to believing (John 6:40, 47), and the penitent thief, who never received the Supper, was promised paradise (Luke 23:43). (3) *Faith is a merely intellectual assent.* Some treat believing as agreeing with a set of facts. Jesus describes faith as coming, eating, drinking, and abiding (John 6:35, 54, 56): receiving Christ himself and living by him.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Thankfulness at the cost of our life. Daily dependence on Christ as the soul's food, not an occasional religious snack. And joy at the Lord's Table, where the Supper signs and seals the feeding faith already enjoys.

### Eschatology · Raised Up at the Last Day
**Proposition:** Those who come to Christ have eternal life now, and at the last day Christ himself will raise their bodies from the dead. The resurrection of believers is the completion of his keeping: none whom the Father gave him will be lost, not even to death.

**Textual Anchor:** "and I will raise him up at the last day" (John 6:40, 44), with *anastēsō* (G450, John 6:44, V-FAI-1S); "Whoso eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood, hath eternal life; and I will raise him up at the last day." (John 6:54)

**Cross-References:** "Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:" (John 11:25); "But if the Spirit of him that raised up Jesus from the dead dwell in you, he that raised up Christ from the dead shall also quicken your mortal bodies by his Spirit that dwelleth in you." (Romans 8:11)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Eternal life is only a future hope.* Some treat salvation as something no one can possess until death. Jesus says the believer 'hath everlasting life' now (John 6:47, 54). (2) *Eternal life is only a present spiritual experience, with no bodily resurrection.* Some modern theologies reduce resurrection to a metaphor for new life. Jesus repeats that he will raise his people 'at the last day' (John 6:39, 40, 44, 54), and Martha understood that phrase of the bodily resurrection (John 11:24). (3) *The dead are lost to Christ until they are raised.* Some fear that death breaks the believer's union with him. Jesus says he will lose 'nothing' of all the Father gave him, and raise it up (John 6:39).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Hope at the graveside, because the Lord who gathered every fragment will gather every one of his people. Freedom from the fear of death. And a patient life, lived toward 'the last day'.

### Christian Walk · Labour for the Meat That Endureth; To Whom Shall We Go?
**Proposition:** Believers are to set their labour and their longing on Christ, the food that endures, rather than on bread that perishes. When his sayings are hard and others turn back, they stay with him, confessing that he alone has the words of eternal life, and they live by him from day to day.

**Textual Anchor:** "Labour not for the meat which perisheth, but for that meat which endureth unto everlasting life" (John 6:27), with *ergazesthe* (G2038, John 6:27, V-PNM-2P), a present command; "Then Simon Peter answered him, Lord, to whom shall we go? thou hast the words of eternal life." (John 6:68)

**Cross-References:** "But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you." (Matthew 6:33); "Then said Jesus to those Jews which believed on him, If ye continue in my word, then are ye my disciples indeed;" (John 8:31); "Take heed, brethren, lest there be in any of you an evil heart of unbelief, in departing from the living God." (Hebrews 3:12)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Following Jesus is worthwhile because it brings material blessing.* The prosperity gospel, and the crowd in John 6, follow for the loaves; they want to honour God's goodness in provision. Jesus refused to be a bread-king and told them not to labour for food that perishes (John 6:15, 27). (2) *A disciple may walk away when a teaching is too hard.* Many today treat Jesus' teaching as a menu to choose from. Those who 'went back' are presented as a warning, and Peter's answer as the way of faith (John 6:66–68). (3) *Continuing in faith is achieved by our own resolve.* Peter's later denial (John 18:25–27) shows that the disciple's grip is not what keeps him; Jesus had chosen him (John 6:70) and prayed for him (Luke 22:32).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Contentment with less, because the soul's food is secure. Steadiness when faith is costly. And a daily habit of coming to Christ in his word and at prayer, as the body comes to the table.

### Assumed Here
- **Ecclesiology:** John 6 assumes a gathered people (the twelve, the disciples, the synagogue) but does not teach the nature of the church; and while its language of eating and drinking stands behind the church's reading of the Lord's Supper, the chapter does not institute the Supper. Scripture teaches the church and its Supper plainly in Matthew 16:18, Matthew 26:26–28, Acts 2:42, and 1 Corinthians 11:23–26.

## ⚖️ The Council's Hard Questions
### ❓ Question 1 · Does John 6 Speak of the Lord's Supper
**Moderator:** Jesus says, 'Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you' (John 6:53). Christians have fought over these words for a thousand years. Is Jesus speaking of the Lord's Supper, and if not, why does he use such language?

**Church Historian:** The main positions are old and well defined. The Roman Catholic Church, at the Council of Trent (thirteenth session, 1551, verify), taught that in the Eucharist the bread and wine are changed in substance into the body and blood of Christ, and it appealed to John 6:51 among other texts; yet the same council, in its twenty-first session (verify), acknowledged that John 6 has been interpreted in various ways by the fathers. The Eastern Orthodox churches likewise read the passage of the Eucharist. Luther taught that Christ's body and blood are truly present in, with, and under the bread and wine, but he denied that John 6 speaks of the sacrament (verify). Calvin and the Reformed confessions taught that believers truly feed on Christ's body and blood in the Supper, spiritually and by faith, through the Spirit, and read John 6 as describing that feeding by faith, which the Supper later signs and seals. Zwingli stressed the Supper as a memorial and leaned hard on 'the flesh profiteth nothing' (John 6:63). So even those who agree that Christ is really present at the Table disagree about whether John 6 is the text that proves it.

**Philologist:** Three details in the Greek matter. First, the promise of verse 54, 'hath eternal life; and I will raise him up at the last day', is word for word the promise made to the believer in verse 40, and verse 47 promises the same life to 'He that believeth on me'. Eating in verses 53–58 stands where believing stood in verses 35–47. Second, John writes 'flesh', *sarx* (G4561, John 6:51), whereas the accounts of the Last Supper and Paul's account write 'body': "Take, eat: this is my body" (1 Corinthians 11:24). If John meant his readers simply to think of the Supper, 'body' would have been the obvious word. Third, the change to *trōgōn* (G5176, John 6:54) is not strong evidence either way, since the word had become an everyday verb for eating (see the Deep Dive).

**Historian:** Consider the setting. Jesus is speaking in a synagogue in Capernaum, a year before the Last Supper, to a Galilean crowd and to disciples many of whom leave that day (John 6:59, 66). They could not have understood his words as instructions about a rite that did not yet exist, and he holds them responsible for not believing what he said (John 6:64). John's first readers, decades later, did know the Supper, and they would surely have heard its echo in 'my flesh' and 'my blood', and in the thanksgiving over the loaves (John 6:11, 23). John may well intend that echo.

**Theologian:** And the theology settles the primary reference. Verse 53 is absolute: whoever does not eat and drink 'have no life in you', and verse 54 says whoever does eat and drink 'hath eternal life'. If these words were simply about receiving the sacrament, then every communicant, Judas included (John 13:26–27), would have eternal life, and every believer who died before receiving it, like the thief on the cross (Luke 23:43), would be lost. No Christian tradition teaches either. Read as feeding on Christ crucified by faith, both verses are true without exception. And verse 63 explains how the feeding happens: by the Spirit, through Christ's words, received in faith. The Supper is where the church eats and drinks what John 6 describes; John 6 is not a description of the Supper.

**Pastor:** In our group we will have people from many backgrounds, some who were taught that the Mass is the only way to receive Christ, some who treat the Lord's Supper as an optional memorial, and some who have never taken it at all. John 6 speaks to all of them. It tells the first that Christ gives himself to faith, and it tells the second that feeding on Christ is not optional or symbolic but the very life of the soul.

**Resolution:** The best reading, and the one most consistent with Scripture interpreting Scripture, is that John 6:51–58 speaks directly of feeding on Christ crucified by faith, which is the reality the Lord's Supper later signifies and seals. The verses cannot mean that the rite itself gives life to all who receive it and withholds it from all who do not, because the same promise is made to believing (John 6:40, 47), and because 'the flesh profiteth nothing' apart from the Spirit and the word (John 6:63). John's readers rightly hear the Table in these words, and believers who come to the Table in faith feed on the same Christ. Other traditions read the verses more directly of the sacrament, and their concern for Christ's real self-giving is one the text shares: "He that eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood, dwelleth in me, and I in him." (John 6:56)

### ❓ Question 2 · None Lost, yet Many Went Back
**Moderator:** Jesus says it is the Father's will 'that of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing' (John 6:39). Yet by the end of the chapter 'many of his disciples went back' (John 6:66), and one of the twelve is 'a devil' (John 6:70). Were these people given to him and then lost?

**Literary Scholar:** Watch how John uses the word 'disciples'. At the beginning of the Gospel, many 'believed in his name' in Jerusalem, 'But Jesus did not commit himself unto them' (John 2:23–24). In chapter 8, of those who 'believed on him' (John 8:30), Jesus says that true discipleship is proved by continuing (John 8:31), and within the same chapter they want to stone him (John 8:59). John uses 'disciple' in a broad sense for those who followed Jesus about, and he shows again and again that following and believing are not the same. John 6 itself says so before the departure: 'there are some of you that believe not' (John 6:64).

**Theologian:** And John tells us that Jesus 'knew from the beginning who they were that believed not, and who should betray him' (John 6:64). Those who went back had never come in the sense of John 6:35 and 37. They had followed the loaves. The promise of verse 39 is for all whom the Father gave, and verse 37 says all those come to him; nothing in the chapter says these did. John's first letter says of those who left the church in his day, 'They went out from us, but they were not of us' (1 John 2:19). As for Judas, Jesus prays in John 17:12 that he kept all the Father gave him and none was lost 'but the son of perdition; that the scripture might be fulfilled'. Judas was chosen to be one of the twelve (John 6:70), with a place in the story Scripture had foretold (Psalm 41:9; John 13:18), but he was never a believer.

**Church Historian:** The church has divided on how to state this. The Reformed confessions teach that those whom God has effectually called can neither totally nor finally fall away from grace, but will persevere to the end (Westminster Confession, chapter 17, section 1, paraphrased, verify). Arminian and Wesleyan churches, and the Roman Catholic Church, teach that a true believer can lose grace and be lost, and they point to warnings such as Hebrews 6:4–6 and John 15:6. The Remonstrants in 1610 left the question open and asked for further study (verify); later Arminians generally affirmed that believers can fall.

**Pastor:** Two kinds of people in our group need this passage. One is the tender believer who is afraid of being among those who go back. To them I say: the promise is not 'you will hold on' but 'I will lose nothing'; if you have come to Christ, you are kept by him. The other is the person who is comfortable in the group but has never come to Christ at all. To them John 6:64 and 6:70 are a loving warning: you can eat the bread, carry the basket, and stay when others leave, and still not believe.

**Teacher:** In the session I would draw two columns on a whiteboard: 'followed' and 'came'. The crowd, the many disciples, and Judas all followed; Peter and the eleven came. Then I would ask the group which column John 6:37 and 39 speak about. It keeps the discussion on the text and away from speculation about particular people we know.

**Resolution:** John 6 does not teach that those given to the Son can be lost. It distinguishes those who followed Jesus from those who came to him in faith. Those who went back had followed for bread and stumbled at his words (John 6:26, 60–66); Jesus knew they did not believe (John 6:64), and John's later word is that 'they were not all of us' (1 John 2:19). The promise stands for every one the Father gives and every one who comes: "And this is the Father's will which hath sent me, that of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing, but should raise it up again at the last day." (John 6:39) The departure of the many is a warning to examine ourselves; the keeping of the Son is the comfort of all who have come.

### ❓ Question 3 · It Is I, or I Am
**Moderator:** On the sea Jesus says, 'It is I; be not afraid' (John 6:20). In Greek the words are *egō* (G1473, John 6:20) *eimi* (G1510, John 6:20), 'I am'. Is this simply 'It's me', or is Jesus using the divine name of Exodus 3:14?

**Philologist:** Grammatically, the words are an ordinary way of identifying oneself. In John 9:9 the healed blind man uses exactly the same two words, *egō* (G1473, John 9:9) *eimi* (G1510, John 9:9), and the KJV renders them 'I am he'; no one thinks he is claiming deity. Matthew and Mark report the same saying on the sea (Matthew 14:27; Mark 6:50). So we must not say that *egō eimi* always means 'I am God'. The question is what the words mean in this setting.

**Literary Scholar:** And the setting is loaded. John has just told us that the Passover was near (John 6:4). A man has fed a multitude in the wilderness with bread; now he walks on the sea at night, as the God of the Exodus made 'thy way is in the sea' (Psalm 77:19). He speaks to men terrified at what they see, and his words end their fear. In Isaiah, God says to Israel, "When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee; and through the rivers, they shall not overflow thee" (Isaiah 43:2), and a few verses later, 'that ye may know and believe me, and understand that I am he' (Isaiah 43:10), where the Greek Old Testament uses the same two words (verify). John's Gospel later uses the words with no predicate at all (John 8:24, 28, 58) and at the arrest, where the soldiers 'went backward, and fell to the ground' (John 18:6). John is building toward those moments, and this is the first step.

**Historian:** The Old Testament's language for walking on the sea belongs to God alone: he 'treadeth upon the waves of the sea' (Job 9:8). No Jewish reader would have thought of a prophet doing it. Mark adds that the disciples 'were sore amazed' and did not understand (Mark 6:51–52), and Matthew that they worshipped him and called him 'the Son of God' (Matthew 14:33). The first reaction of those who saw it was not 'It was only him' but worship.

**Theologian:** So both levels are true, and they do not compete. On the surface Jesus says, 'It's me; don't be afraid'. Beneath the surface, the one who says it is doing what God alone does, and using the words God used to reveal himself. John often writes this way: ordinary words carry a second meaning that the reader understands better than the characters do. Jesus' deity does not rest on this verse alone, but the verse is one of the lines that lead to 'Before Abraham was, I am' (John 8:58).

**Teacher:** For a Tier 1 group, I would not start with Greek. I would ask, 'Who walks on water?' and read Job 9:8. Then I would read John 6:20 again and let them feel the double meaning: 'It is I', the friend they know; and 'It is I', the Lord of the sea. Both are comforting.

**Resolution:** 'It is I' in John 6:20 is, on the surface, an ordinary self-identification, as the same words are in John 9:9. But in its setting, at Passover time, on the sea, in the dark, spoken by one who walks where only God walks (Job 9:8; Psalm 77:19), it carries the echo of God's self-revelation that John will make explicit later in the Gospel. The comfort for the disciples, and for us, is not only that a friend has come but that the Lord has come: "But he saith unto them, It is I; be not afraid." (John 6:20)

### 🔦 The Life & Light Audit
**Pastor:** If a hearer obeyed this lesson exactly, then tonight they would read John 6:35 and 6:51 slowly and pray, 'Lord, evermore give us this bread', thanking Christ by name for his body given for them; and they would name to the Lord one thing they have wanted from him more than him. Tomorrow they would share a meal or groceries with one person who is struggling to make ends meet, and tell them in one sentence that Jesus is the bread of life; they would call a friend who has drifted after a hard season and offer to read John 6:66–69 together; and they would tell a struggling believer which hard saying of Jesus they are learning to trust. The dark place lit would be their own motives for following Jesus, the loaves they have loved more than the Lord, and, for some, a hard teaching they have been avoiding.

**Theologian:** The practices keep source discipline. None asks the hearer to produce life or to draw anyone; the Father draws (John 6:44), the Son gives his flesh (John 6:51), and the Spirit quickens (John 6:63). The hearer's part is the disciples' part in the feeding: to carry from Christ's hands what only he supplies. The Inward practices are acts of receiving and confessing. The Abundance Guard is kept by the lesson's refusal to promise loaves: the practices give food to the hungry as mercy and as a sign, and point beyond it to the bread that gives life for ever.

**Teacher:** A Level 1 participant can do the Inward Life practice tonight: read two verses and pray one sentence. The Outward practices name the kind of person to reach (someone struggling to make ends meet; a friend who has drifted from church after a hard season; a fellow believer struggling with a hard teaching; a friend weighing whether to keep following after a disappointment) and leave a blank for the name. The Light practices are concrete and small: one confession tonight, one honest conversation tomorrow.

**Fix:** The Audit found three risks, and the unit has addressed them. First, a lesson about following Jesus 'for the loaves' could shame members who came to church through a food pantry or in a time of need; so the Light questions turn the lamp on the participant's own heart first, the Modern Example shows a comfortable believer rather than a poor one, and the Shepherding Notes tell the leader to guard those members. Second, John 6:44 could frighten seekers into thinking they may not be allowed to come; so the Walkthrough, the Common Errors, the Debate Corner, and the Preaching Mistakes all set John 6:44 beside John 6:37. Third, the Supper question could turn the session into a denominational argument; so the participant pages present it as a fair View A and View B, the Council gives each tradition its strongest form, and the Session Plan keeps it to one question. The naming round reads only the second row of the Light Planner, never the private first row.

### ✅ Answer Key
**Q1.** Jesus went over the sea of Galilee, also called the sea of Tiberias (John 6:1). A great multitude followed him because they saw the signs he did on the sick (John 6:2). He went up a mountain and sat with his disciples, and the Passover was near (John 6:3–4).

**Q2.** Jesus asked Philip, 'Whence shall we buy bread, that these may eat?' (John 6:5), to test him, since he already knew what he would do (John 6:6). Philip said two hundred pennyworth of bread would not give everyone even a little (John 6:7). Andrew pointed to a lad with five barley loaves and two small fishes, but asked what they were among so many (John 6:8–9).

**Q3.** Jesus had the men sit down on the grass, about five thousand of them (John 6:10); he took the loaves, gave thanks, and distributed them to the disciples, who gave them to the people, and the same with the fishes, 'as much as they would' (John 6:11). When all were filled he had the fragments gathered so that nothing would be lost, and they filled twelve baskets from the five barley loaves (John 6:12–13).

**Q4.** They said, 'This is of a truth that prophet that should come into the world' (John 6:14), the Prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15). They meant to take him by force and make him king; he withdrew alone into a mountain (John 6:15).

**Q5.** At evening the disciples set out by boat for Capernaum; it was dark and Jesus had not come to them (John 6:16–17). A great wind stirred the sea (John 6:18). After rowing twenty-five or thirty furlongs, three or four miles, they saw Jesus walking on the sea and were afraid (John 6:19). He said, 'It is I; be not afraid' (John 6:20). They gladly took him in, and immediately the boat was at the shore (John 6:21).

**Q6.** They sought him not because they understood the signs but because they ate the loaves and were filled (John 6:26). He told them to labour not for food that perishes but for food that endures to everlasting life, which the Son of man will give them (John 6:27).

**Q7.** The one who comes will never hunger; the one who believes will never thirst (John 6:35); all the Father gives will come, and whoever comes will never be cast out (John 6:37); Jesus will lose none of those given him and will raise them up at the last day (John 6:39); every one who sees the Son and believes will have everlasting life and be raised up at the last day (John 6:40).

**Q8.** God fed Israel with bread from heaven (Exodus 16:4, 15) to teach them that man lives by every word from his mouth (Deuteronomy 8:3). Elisha's feeding showed God's power to multiply a little bread (2 Kings 4:42–44). God's path is in the sea (Psalm 77:19). God invites the hungry to come and eat freely (Isaiah 55:1–2), and Paul says the wilderness food and drink pointed to Christ (1 Corinthians 10:3–4). Together they show that Jesus does what God did for Israel, that he is the true bread the manna pointed to, and that the hunger God means to satisfy is the soul's hunger for himself.

**Q9.** The crowd thought of many works to earn God's favour (John 6:28); Jesus names one 'work', believing on the one God sent (John 6:29). It is called a work only because they asked about works; it is not earning but receiving what 'the Son of man shall give' (John 6:27). His answer turns their question from what we can do for God to whom we must trust.

**Q10.** The first half of John 6:37 and John 6:44 explain why anyone comes at all: the Father gives and draws. The second half of John 6:37 and John 6:40 promise that everyone who comes and believes is welcomed and kept. Together they comfort the believer, who is kept by the Father's gift and the Son's promise (John 6:39), and they warn the one holding back that he will not come by his own strength, while assuring him that if he comes he will not be cast out (John 6:37). They never give anyone a reason to stay away.

**Q11.** Moses was the channel of the manna, but God gave it; the Father now gives the true bread (John 6:32). The manna fell from the sky; the true bread 'cometh down from heaven' as a person (John 6:33). The manna kept Israel alive for a time, and they died (John 6:49); the true bread gives life to the world, and whoever eats it will live for ever (John 6:50–51, 58).

**Q12.** Model answer, not the only answer: View B fits the text better, because the promise to the one who eats (John 6:54) repeats the promise to the one who believes (John 6:40, 47), because the crowd in Capernaum could not have known of a Supper not yet instituted, and because Jesus explains that his words are spirit and life (John 6:63). But View A rightly hears the Supper's echo, and the Supper does picture this feeding (1 Corinthians 10:16). Let members from sacramental traditions state View A at its strongest.

**Q13.** Peter rightly confessed that Jesus alone has the words of eternal life and is the Christ (John 6:68–69), but he did not know his own weakness; he later denied Jesus three times (John 18:17, 25–27). At the breakfast by the sea Jesus fed him and restored him, asking him three times whether he loved him and giving him his work again (John 21:9–17). Jesus did not cast him out (John 6:37); he had prayed that his faith would not fail (Luke 22:32).

**Q14.** Answers will vary. Encourage members to be specific about the 'loaves' they have loved (health, success, family blessing, answered prayer), and to see that Peter stayed not because following was easy but because there was nowhere else to go (John 6:26, 68).

**Q15.** The bread of heaven is Christ himself, who came down from heaven (John 6:33, 35). Unlike the manna, which fed Israel only in the wilderness and could not keep them from death (John 6:49), he gives life for ever (John 6:51). To be fed by him through a long journey is to come to him daily in faith, to live by him as he lives by the Father (John 6:57), and to trust him to bring us safely home and raise us up at the last day (John 6:40).

**Q16.** Coming, believing, and eating are three pictures of one act: receiving Christ himself by faith (John 6:35, 47, 54). Each receives the same gift, everlasting life. It is not by doing the works of God ourselves, because the one work God requires is to believe on the one he sent (John 6:29), and the food is what the Son gives (John 6:27).

**Q17.** Answers will vary. Point members to the strength of the promise: a double negative, 'in no wise' (John 6:37), and to the Son's purpose to lose nothing of what the Father gave (John 6:39). Past sin, doubt, and failure are not reasons he turns anyone away; they are what he came down from heaven to deal with (John 6:51).

**Q18.** Answers will vary. Peter's answer shows the path: weigh the alternatives honestly, and see that only Christ has 'the words of eternal life' (John 6:68). Encourage members to name the Spirit's work in keeping them (John 6:63).

**Q19.** Answers will vary, but the step should be concrete and small, for example setting aside time to read Scripture before checking work email, or giving away something that competes with Christ for our labour (John 6:27; Matthew 6:33).

**Q20.** Model answer, not the only answer: Jesus, who came down from heaven, came to do the Father's will and not his own (John 6:38), and he refused a crown the crowd tried to force on him (John 6:15). Our hunger for recognition and control is rebuked by the one who had every right to both and gave up both. It makes him more glorious because his humility was chosen, not forced, and because it led him to give his flesh for us (John 6:51).

**Q21.** Model answer, not the only answer: coworkers, relatives, or neighbours who are chasing money, success, pleasure, approval, or comfort, and who are full one day and empty the next (John 6:27). Encourage a specific name for the Life Planner.

**Q22.** Model answer, not the only answer: the thief has stolen lasting satisfaction, and often the ability to see Jesus as anything more than a possible helper with problems (John 6:26; John 10:10).

**Q23.** Model answer, not the only answer: Christ gives himself, the bread of life, so that whoever comes to him will never hunger, and whoever eats will live for ever (John 6:35, 51). It matters that he gives himself because gifts run out, and he does not.

**Q24.** Model answer, not the only answer: someone who is going without enough food, or eats alone; what stops us is often busyness, fear of awkwardness, or thinking we have too little to give, the same thought as 'what are they among so many?' (John 6:9, 11).

**Q25.** Model answer, not the only answer: health, a family's welfare, success, the feeling of being blessed, answered prayers (John 6:26). The honest answer belongs in the private first row of the Light Planner.

**Q26.** Model answer, not the only answer: sayings about self-denial, sexual holiness, forgiveness of enemies, judgment, money, or God's sovereignty in salvation (John 6:44, 60). The cost may be approval, comfort, or the sense of being in control.

**Q27.** Model answer, not the only answer: someone who attends church, serves, or grew up in a Christian home but shows no sign of trusting Christ (John 6:64, 70). Carry light in love by praying for them first, being honest about your own need of Christ, and inviting them to read John 6 with you, never by accusing them (Ephesians 4:15).

### 🗺️ Life & Light Leader's Map
**Worked Life Planner Row:** *Who:* 'Luis, the neighbour who lost his job in the spring'. *What the thief has stolen:* his sense of worth, his routine, and his hope that anything will get better; he is anxious about money and ashamed to ask for help. *What Christ gives instead:* the bread that endures and a Father who gives (John 6:27, 32, 35). *What I will do:* on Thursday, bring over a bag of groceries without making a fuss, stay for coffee if he invites me, and if the moment comes, tell him that Jesus once fed a hungry crowd and then said he himself is the bread of life. *Done?* Tick it when done, and write one line about how it went.

**Worked Light Planner Row:** *Where is it dark (second row):* 'our small group's habit of complaining about the pastor's sermons'. *What is hidden there:* murmuring like the crowd's (John 6:41, 61), and my own part in it. *What light Christ shines:* 'Murmur not among yourselves' (John 6:43). *How I carry it there:* on Sunday, instead of joining the complaint, say one thing the sermon showed me about Christ, and ask the others what they found; if I have complained, admit it first.

**When Someone Has No One to Name:** Ask them to picture the crowd on the grass: who in their week is hungry? Walk through their day with them: the person at the checkout they see every week, the colleague who eats lunch alone, the elderly neighbour, the family member who has gone quiet, the man at the bus stop. Remind them that the lad did not have much to give either (John 6:9). If they still cannot name someone, ask them to pray tonight, 'Father, show me one hungry person tomorrow', and to bring the name next week.

**When Someone Points the Light at Others:** When a member names another person's or another church's shallow motives, 'they're only in it for the blessings', as their dark place, bring them gently back to the first row of their Light Planner and to John 6:26. Say something like: 'Jesus could see the crowd's hearts; we can see our own. Let's start there.' Remind the group that Jesus exposed the crowd's motive in order to offer them himself (John 6:26–27), and that he kept Judas at the table. If the member persists, speak with them privately after the session.

### 🐑 Shepherding Notes
- **Members who have been hungry.** Some in the group may have depended on food banks, church pantries, or the kindness of others, or may do so now. A careless word about people who 'follow Jesus for the loaves' can wound them deeply. Make clear that Jesus fed the crowd gladly and in abundance, that receiving help is not the sin John 6 exposes, and that the question of motive is for each of us to ask of ourselves.
- **Members who fear they are not chosen.** John 6:44 and 6:65 can frighten a tender conscience. Anyone who is worried about whether they are drawn is already showing signs of the Father's work. Point them to John 6:37: the way to know you were given is to come, and whoever comes is never cast out.
- **Members who have walked away before.** Some have left church in the past, perhaps over a hard teaching or a hard season, and have come back. John 6:66 may sting. Point them to Peter, who confessed Christ, then denied him, and was fed and restored by the same sea (John 21:9–17).
- **Members in a storm.** Illness, grief, or anxiety may make the disciples' night on the sea very real. Do not promise that every storm will calm. Point them to the one who comes in the dark and says 'It is I', and pray with them.
- **Members from sacramental traditions.** Catholic, Orthodox, and Lutheran members, or those raised in them, may feel that the Reformed reading of John 6:53 dismisses what they hold dear. Present the views fairly, and stress what all agree on: that Christ gives himself, and that there is no life apart from him.
- **The Light Planner's first row.** It is private. Never ask anyone to read it aloud or to share it. If someone chooses to speak about their own heart, receive it with gentleness and pray for them; do not probe for details.

### 🎯 Teaching Angles
**For a Tier 1 Group:** Bring a loaf of bread and, if possible, share it at the start. Tell the story of the feeding through the lad's eyes: his small lunch, Jesus' thanks, the baskets left over. Then tell the night on the sea through Peter's eyes. Then ask one question again and again through the discourse: 'What is Jesus saying he is?' Keep to the Memory Verse and John 6:37 and 6:68, and let the loaf make the point: bread is not something you look at; it is something you eat, and Jesus is bread for the soul.

**For a Tier 2 Group:** Trace three lines through the chapter. The line of bread: the barley loaves, the manna, the true bread, the bread of life, the living bread that is his flesh; 'bread' occurs 17 times in the chapter. The line of faith: 'believ*' occurs 9 times, and 'life' 11 times; set coming, believing, seeing, and eating side by side (John 6:35, 40, 47, 54). The line of divine initiative: the Father gives, sends, seals, draws, teaches, and wills that none be lost (John 6:27, 32, 37–45, 65), and the Son raises up at the last day, a phrase that occurs 4 times. Then set the crowd beside the twelve: many who followed for bread, and a few who stayed because there was nowhere else to go.

**Tough Question 1:** *If no one can come to Jesus unless the Father draws him, is it fair for God to condemn those who do not come?* Jesus holds both truths in the same breath: no one comes unless drawn (John 6:44), and those who do not believe are responsible for their unbelief (John 6:36, 64). The inability he describes is not a longing to come that God blocks, but an unwillingness to come (John 5:40). No one in John 6 wanted Christ and was refused; the crowd wanted bread. And the invitation stands for everyone: 'him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out' (John 6:37).

**Tough Question 2:** *Is John 6 about the Lord's Supper? My church says the bread becomes Christ's body.* Christians disagree here, and the question deserves a respectful answer. The strongest reading of John 6 is that Jesus speaks of feeding on him by faith, because the same promise is made to believing (John 6:40, 47) and to eating (John 6:54), and because his words are spirit and life (John 6:63). The Lord's Supper, instituted later, is where the church feeds on Christ in this way at his Table. Every tradition agrees on the heart of the matter: there is no life apart from Christ's body given and his blood shed (John 6:53).

**Tough Question 3:** *Why did Jesus let so many people leave? Why not explain himself more gently?* Jesus did explain: his words are spirit and life (John 6:63), and the flesh without the Spirit profits nothing. But he did not change the message to keep the crowd, because the crowd wanted a king who would feed them, not a Saviour who would give his flesh for them (John 6:15, 26). He loved them enough to tell the truth. And he turned at once to the twelve with a gentle question, 'Will ye also go away?' (John 6:67): he does not chase people with easier words, and he does not stop inviting.
