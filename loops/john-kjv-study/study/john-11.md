# John 11 · The Resurrection and the Life (KJV)
> Unit john-11 · John 11:1–57 · loop john-kjv-study v2.0 · DRAFT for human doctrine review

## 🧭 At a Glance
### 🧵 Golden Thread
Jesus delays, weeps, and raises Lazarus to show that he himself is the resurrection and the life, while his enemies plot the death that gathers God's scattered children.

### 🌗 Life & Light Line
John 11 answers the death that comes even to a man Jesus loves: a sickness reported with 'he whom thou lovest is sick' (John 11:3), a body four days in a cave behind a stone (John 11:17, 38–39), two sisters saying the same broken sentence, 'Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died' (John 11:21, 32), and a council that answers the gift of life by planning a killing (John 11:53). Christ gives instead himself, 'the resurrection, and the life' (John 11:25), a voice that calls the dead out by name (John 11:43), and a death of his own, died 'for the people' so that God's scattered children are gathered into one (John 11:50–52). The chapter exposes the darkness of those who walk 'in the night' with 'no light in him' (John 11:10): disciples who hear 'sleep' and think of rest, mourners who ask why he did not prevent this, and rulers who see a dead man walk out of his tomb and reason only about their 'place and nation' (John 11:48). The light Christ gives instead is the light of the day he walks in (John 11:9), the plain word 'Lazarus is dead' (John 11:14), his prayer spoken aloud 'that they may believe' (John 11:42), and 'the glory of God' shown to those who believe (John 11:40).

### 🎯 Objectives
- Explain John 11:25–26 in your own words: what Jesus says he is, what he promises the believer who dies and the believer who lives, and why Martha's true faith in 'the resurrection at the last day' (John 11:24) was not yet the whole truth.
- Show from John 11:35 and John 11:43 that the one who wept at the grave as a true man is the one who called the dead out as the Lord of life, and say why both matter to someone who grieves.
- **Doing:** Within the next day, contact one grieving person, sit or speak with them without trying to explain their loss, and, if they are willing, read them John 11:25–26 and pray for them by name.

## 🏛️ Core Foundations
### ✝️ Christological Root
**Gospel Foundation:** John 11 rests on a claim Jesus made at a graveside and on a word he spoke into a tomb. To a grieving sister who believed in a resurrection far off, he said, "Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:" (John 11:25) "And whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this?" (John 11:26) Then, having wept with those who wept, "Jesus wept." (John 11:35), he stood before the cave and made the claim good: "And when he thus had spoken, he cried with a loud voice, Lazarus, come forth." (John 11:43) And the chapter ends with the other half of the gospel, spoken by an enemy who did not know what he was saying: "Nor consider that it is expedient for us, that one man should die for the people, and that the whole nation perish not." (John 11:50)

**The Bridge:** In John 10 Jesus said he would lay down his life for the sheep and take it again (John 10:17–18). In John 11 he walks back into Judaea, where men had just tried to stone him (John 11:8), to call one of his own out of death, and that act of life sets the council's plan of death in motion (John 11:53). The raising of Lazarus is the last and greatest sign of the ministry before the Passion, and it is the hinge of the whole Gospel: the life Jesus gives Lazarus costs Jesus his own. The doctrines grounded here are Christ's identity as the resurrection and the life, the one in whom resurrection is present now and not only at the last day (John 11:24–26); his true humanity and true deity, seen side by side in his tears and his command (John 11:35, 43); God's sovereign purpose to be glorified through sickness, delay, and death (John 11:4, 40); the effectual voice of the Son that gives life to the dead (John 11:43; 5:25); the substitutionary death of one man for the people (John 11:50–51); and the gathering of the scattered children of God into one people (John 11:52).

**Canonical Echoes:** Jesus (John 11:25) → Promise (Isaiah 25:8; Ezekiel 37:12–13; Daniel 12:2) → Doctrine (1 Corinthians 15:20–26; 2 Timothy 1:10) → Life (1 Thessalonians 4:13–14; Revelation 21:4)

**What Gets Lost:** Taught without Christ at the centre, John 11 becomes a story about a miracle long ago, a lesson in the stages of grief, or a proof that prayer gets results if you believe hard enough. 'Jesus wept' becomes a sentimental line for sympathy cards, and 'I am the resurrection' becomes a sentence read at funerals and forgotten at the graveside. With Christ at the centre, every piece holds together. His delay is love, because he is the Lord of the outcome (John 11:5–6). His tears are real, because he is truly man, and his command works, because he is truly God (John 11:35, 43). And the chapter's end shows why believers may face death with hope: the one who called Lazarus out went into death himself, 'for the people' (John 11:50), so that death could not keep any of his own.

### ⚔️ Spiritual Warfare
**The Lie:** Death has the last word, and if God really loved you he would have come sooner. The lie speaks in two voices in this chapter. To the grieving it says, *he could have stopped this and didn't, so he must not care*, which is why some of the mourners ask whether 'this man, which opened the eyes of the blind' could not have prevented this death (John 11:37). To the powerful it says, *secure your place; life is a threat to be managed*, which is why the council meets a resurrection with a murder plot (John 11:47–48, 53).

**The Truth:** "Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:" (John 11:25) "Forasmuch then as the children are partakers of flesh and blood, he also himself likewise took part of the same; that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil;" (Hebrews 2:14)

**The Battleground:** The grieving heart, the graveside, and the council room. The heart is the first ground: the long days of waiting when the answer seems late, and the sentence that begins 'Lord, if thou hadst been here' (John 11:21). The graveside is the second: the place where hope is tested against the smell of death (John 11:39). And the council room is the third: every place where people who have seen God's work decide to protect their position rather than bow to it (John 11:47–50).

**The Weapon:** The word of Christ, believed in the dark. Jesus answered Martha's grief not with an explanation but with himself and a question: 'Believest thou this' (John 11:26). At the stone he answered her doubt by recalling his promise: 'Said I not unto thee' (John 11:40). The weapon against the lie is not a technique for feeling better; it is the promise of the one who is the resurrection, held on to in the delay, prayed back to the Father as he prayed (John 11:41–42), and proved at last by his voice.

### 🌱 Life Pathway
**The Theft:** In John 11 the thief takes a man whom Jesus loves. Lazarus is 'sick' (John 11:1–3), then 'dead' (John 11:14), then four days in the grave, so far gone that his sister says 'by this time he stinketh' (John 11:39). Death does not only take Lazarus; it takes his sisters' hope, so that both greet Jesus with the same sentence of loss (John 11:21, 32), and it fills the house with mourners who can only weep (John 11:31, 33). And in the council the theft goes deeper still: men who have just heard that a dead man lives decide that the life-giver must die (John 11:53), and later that Lazarus must die too (John 12:10). The thief steals breath, hope, and the heart's ability to receive life when it comes.

**The Source:** Christ, who says, "Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:" (John 11:25) His means in this chapter are his love, which sets the delay and the journey (John 11:5–7); his word to Martha, which moves her from a doctrine to a person (John 11:23–27); his tears and groaning, by which he enters the grief before he ends it (John 11:33–35); his prayer to the Father who always hears him (John 11:41–42); his voice, which calls a dead man by name (John 11:43); and, behind all these, his own death for the people, which gathers the children of God (John 11:50–52).

**The Channel:** Life passes to others in this chapter through people who obey the Lord's commands around the tomb. Martha goes and calls her sister: 'The Master is come, and calleth for thee' (John 11:28). The bystanders are told to take away the stone before the miracle (John 11:39) and to loose the grave clothes after it (John 11:44). They cannot raise anyone; only his voice does that. But they can carry the message that he calls, remove what stands between the dead and his voice, and help the newly living out of what still binds them. We are channels, never the source: "for without me ye can do nothing." (John 15:5)

**The Abundance Guard:** Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, not prosperity, ease, or freedom from suffering. The first line of this chapter's message is 'he whom thou lovest is sick' (John 11:3): love from Jesus did not keep Lazarus from sickness or death. Jesus himself wept at the grave (John 11:35), and Lazarus, raised, would die again, while the rulers planned to kill him (John 12:10). The life Christ gives is not a guarantee against the grave; it is life that the grave cannot keep, the life of the one who is himself 'the resurrection, and the life' (John 11:25).

### 💡 Light Pathway
**The Darkness:** John 11 names its darkness in Jesus' own words: 'if a man walk in the night, he stumbleth, because there is no light in him' (John 11:10). The disciples stumble in misunderstanding, hearing that Lazarus 'sleepeth' and thinking of rest (John 11:11–13). The mourners stumble in half-light, seeing his love and questioning his power (John 11:36–37). And the rulers walk in the deepest night of all: they admit 'this man doeth many miracles' (John 11:47), and their only question is what it will cost them. Caiaphas speaks one of the truest sentences in the Gospel without seeing any of its meaning (John 11:50–51). This is the darkness of people who can look at the glory of God and see only a threat to their place.

**The Source:** Christ, who walks in the day of his Father's appointing (John 11:9) and is 'the light of the world' (John 8:12): "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." (2 Corinthians 4:6)

**The Lamp:** In John 11 the lamp is Christ's plain word and his visible work. He tells the confused disciples 'plainly, Lazarus is dead' (John 11:14). He tells Martha plainly who he is (John 11:25). He prays aloud so that the bystanders will know the Father sent him (John 11:42). And the sign itself is a lamp, 'the glory of God' made visible to those who believe (John 11:40). For us the lamp is the same: Scripture opened, the truth told about death and about Christ, hidden fear and self-protection confessed, the unfruitful works of darkness reproved (Ephesians 5:11), and good works that glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16).

**The Love Guard:** Light without love is glare. Jesus brought the greatest light of his ministry into this chapter, but he came to it by way of tears. He did not rebuke Martha or Mary for saying 'if thou hadst been here' (John 11:21, 32); he answered Martha with a promise and Mary with his presence and his weeping (John 11:25, 33–35). Even the enemies' fears are reported, not mocked. The light of John 11 is the light of one who groans with the grieving before he speaks to the grave; whoever carries it must weep with those who weep before they explain anything (Romans 12:15).

### 🏺 Setting the Scene
John 11 follows John 10 in time and place. Jesus has withdrawn 'beyond Jordan' to the place where John first baptized (John 10:40), out of reach of the leaders who tried to stone him at the feast of the dedication in winter (John 10:31, 39). Bethany, where Lazarus lives with his sisters Martha and Mary, is on the far side of the Mount of Olives from Jerusalem, 'about fifteen furlongs off' (John 11:18), not quite two miles. To go there is to walk back into the reach of men who want him dead, which is why the disciples object (John 11:8). Readers of Luke already know the sisters: Martha busy with serving and Mary sitting at Jesus' feet (Luke 10:38–42). John tells us, even before he narrates it, that this Mary is the one who anointed the Lord (John 11:2; the anointing itself comes in John 12:1–8).

Death and mourning had their customs. Burial usually took place on the day of death, in a family tomb such as a cave cut in rock and closed with a large stone (John 11:38). Mourning went on for days, with friends and neighbours coming to comfort the family, and often with hired mourners and loud lamentation (verify the details for first-century Judea). Later Jewish writings record a belief that the soul lingered near the body for three days and departed when the face began to change (verify the rabbinic sources); whether or not Martha shared it, four days meant that no one could say Lazarus was only unconscious (John 11:17, 39). Most Jews of the time, following the Pharisees and the Scriptures, believed in a resurrection of the dead 'at the last day' (John 11:24; Daniel 12:2), though the Sadducees denied it (Acts 23:8).

The last part of the chapter moves to the council, which John calls the chief priests and the Pharisees (John 11:47). Under Roman rule the high priest and the council kept order in Jerusalem, and they could lose everything, the temple, their authority, and their people's limited freedom, if Rome judged them unable to control the crowds (John 11:48). Caiaphas was high priest in these years; Josephus says he held the office from about AD 18 to 36, and John tells us he was son-in-law to Annas (John 18:13; verify the dates). John closes with the third Passover he names (John 2:13; 6:4; 11:55), and pilgrims going up early to purify themselves, because people who had touched death had to be cleansed before they could keep the feast (Numbers 19:11–12). Listen as you read for three things: what Jesus says about who he is, how he meets grief, and what the raising of Lazarus sets in motion.

#### John 11:1–16 · Sickness, Delay, and the Road Back
**1** Now a certain man was sick, named Lazarus, of Bethany, the town of Mary and her sister Martha.

**2** (It was that Mary which anointed the Lord with ointment, and wiped his feet with her hair, whose brother Lazarus was sick.)

**3** Therefore his sisters sent unto him, saying, Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest is sick.

**4** When Jesus heard that, he said, This sickness is not unto death, but for the glory of God, that the Son of God might be glorified thereby.

**5** Now Jesus loved Martha, and her sister, and Lazarus.

**6** When he had heard therefore that he was sick, he abode two days still in the same place where he was.

**7** Then after that saith he to his disciples, Let us go into Judaea again.

**8** His disciples say unto him, Master, the Jews of late sought to stone thee; and goest thou thither again?

**9** Jesus answered, Are there not twelve hours in the day? If any man walk in the day, he stumbleth not, because he seeth the light of this world.

**10** But if a man walk in the night, he stumbleth, because there is no light in him.

**11** These things said he: and after that he saith unto them, Our friend Lazarus sleepeth; but I go, that I may awake him out of sleep.

**12** Then said his disciples, Lord, if he sleep, he shall do well.

**13** Howbeit Jesus spake of his death: but they thought that he had spoken of taking of rest in sleep.

**14** Then said Jesus unto them plainly, Lazarus is dead.

**15** And I am glad for your sakes that I was not there, to the intent ye may believe; nevertheless let us go unto him.

**16** Then said Thomas, which is called Didymus, unto his fellowdisciples, Let us also go, that we may die with him.

#### John 11:17–27 · Martha Meets Him
**17** Then when Jesus came, he found that he had lain in the grave four days already.

**18** Now Bethany was nigh unto Jerusalem, about fifteen furlongs off:

**19** And many of the Jews came to Martha and Mary, to comfort them concerning their brother.

**20** Then Martha, as soon as she heard that Jesus was coming, went and met him: but Mary sat still in the house.

**21** Then said Martha unto Jesus, Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died.

**22** But I know, that even now, whatsoever thou wilt ask of God, God will give it thee.

**23** Jesus saith unto her, Thy brother shall rise again.

**24** Martha saith unto him, I know that he shall rise again in the resurrection at the last day.

**25** Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:

**26** And whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this?

**27** She saith unto him, Yea, Lord: I believe that thou art the Christ, the Son of God, which should come into the world.

#### John 11:28–37 · Mary, the Mourners, and the Tears
**28** And when she had so said, she went her way, and called Mary her sister secretly, saying, The Master is come, and calleth for thee.

**29** As soon as she heard that, she arose quickly, and came unto him.

**30** Now Jesus was not yet come into the town, but was in that place where Martha met him.

**31** The Jews then which were with her in the house, and comforted her, when they saw Mary, that she rose up hastily and went out, followed her, saying, She goeth unto the grave to weep there.

**32** Then when Mary was come where Jesus was, and saw him, she fell down at his feet, saying unto him, Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died.

**33** When Jesus therefore saw her weeping, and the Jews also weeping which came with her, he groaned in the spirit, and was troubled,

**34** And said, Where have ye laid him? They said unto him, Lord, come and see.

**35** Jesus wept.

**36** Then said the Jews, Behold how he loved him!

**37** And some of them said, Could not this man, which opened the eyes of the blind, have caused that even this man should not have died?

#### John 11:38–44 · At the Tomb
**38** Jesus therefore again groaning in himself cometh to the grave. It was a cave, and a stone lay upon it.

**39** Jesus said, Take ye away the stone. Martha, the sister of him that was dead, saith unto him, Lord, by this time he stinketh: for he hath been dead four days.

**40** Jesus saith unto her, Said I not unto thee, that, if thou wouldest believe, thou shouldest see the glory of God?

**41** Then they took away the stone from the place where the dead was laid. And Jesus lifted up his eyes, and said, Father, I thank thee that thou hast heard me.

**42** And I knew that thou hearest me always: but because of the people which stand by I said it, that they may believe that thou hast sent me.

**43** And when he thus had spoken, he cried with a loud voice, Lazarus, come forth.

**44** And he that was dead came forth, bound hand and foot with graveclothes: and his face was bound about with a napkin. Jesus saith unto them, Loose him, and let him go.

#### John 11:45–53 · The Council and the High Priest
**45** Then many of the Jews which came to Mary, and had seen the things which Jesus did, believed on him.

**46** But some of them went their ways to the Pharisees, and told them what things Jesus had done.

**47** Then gathered the chief priests and the Pharisees a council, and said, What do we? for this man doeth many miracles.

**48** If we let him thus alone, all men will believe on him: and the Romans shall come and take away both our place and nation.

**49** And one of them, named Caiaphas, being the high priest that same year, said unto them, Ye know nothing at all,

**50** Nor consider that it is expedient for us, that one man should die for the people, and that the whole nation perish not.

**51** And this spake he not of himself: but being high priest that year, he prophesied that Jesus should die for that nation;

**52** And not for that nation only, but that also he should gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad.

**53** Then from that day forth they took counsel together for to put him to death.

#### John 11:54–57 · Ephraim, and the Passover Near
**54** Jesus therefore walked no more openly among the Jews; but went thence unto a country near to the wilderness, into a city called Ephraim, and there continued with his disciples.

**55** And the Jews' passover was nigh at hand: and many went out of the country up to Jerusalem before the passover, to purify themselves.

**56** Then sought they for Jesus, and spake among themselves, as they stood in the temple, What think ye, that he will not come to the feast?

**57** Now both the chief priests and the Pharisees had given a commandment, that, if any man knew where he were, he should shew it, that they might take him.

**View A:** When Jesus 'groaned in the spirit, and was troubled' (John 11:33) and came to the grave 'again groaning in himself' (John 11:38), the groaning was a deep, stern indignation. The Greek verb is used elsewhere of a stern warning, "And he straitly charged him, and forthwith sent him away;" (Mark 1:43), and the STEPBible lexicon lists John 11:33 under the sense *to be moved with anger*. On this view Jesus is outraged at death itself, the enemy that entered the world through sin (Romans 5:12), and perhaps at the despairing unbelief around him, and he goes to the tomb as a warrior goes to battle.

**View B:** The groaning is the deep, inward distress of grief and compassion, the same heart that weeps two verses later (John 11:35). He is the one Isaiah called 'a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief' (Isaiah 53:3), and the high priest who can be 'touched with the feeling of our infirmities' (Hebrews 4:15). On this view the words 'groaned' and 'troubled' describe a man shaken to the depths by the sorrow of those he loves, and the onlookers read him rightly: 'Behold how he loved him' (John 11:36).

### 👣 Case Study · Scripture in Action
The apostle Paul learned the lesson of Bethany in Asia. He wrote to the Corinthians about a trouble so heavy that 'we despaired even of life' (2 Corinthians 1:8). He does not tell us exactly what it was, but he tells us what God was doing in it: "But we had the sentence of death in ourselves, that we should not trust in ourselves, but in God which raiseth the dead:" (2 Corinthians 1:9) Like Martha, Paul found himself at a place where no human help was left. Like Lazarus's family, he had to wait through a delay that looked like death. And like them, he learned that the God he trusted is the God who raises the dead, and that the waiting had a purpose: to bring him to the end of himself. "Who delivered us from so great a death, and doth deliver: in whom we trust that he will yet deliver us;" (2 Corinthians 1:10) Years later, in prison and facing execution, Paul wrote that death itself had lost its terror for him, because Christ was his life: "For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain." (Philippians 1:21) That is John 11:25–26 lived out. The believer who dies will live; the believer who lives will never die. Paul did not escape suffering, and in the end he did not escape death. But he learned in the dark that the one who called Lazarus out holds both the delay and the grave in his hand.

### 🏙️ Modern Example
Ruth and her husband Daniel prayed for eighteen months for their son Josh, who was twenty-six and fighting cancer. Their church prayed. Friends sent verses. For a while the treatment seemed to be working, and then it stopped working, and Josh died on a Tuesday morning in March. At the funeral Ruth found she could not sing. Afterward a well-meaning man from their church told her that God must have had a reason, and a relative told her that Josh was in a better place and she should be glad. Ruth went home and said to Daniel what she had been afraid to say to anyone: *If God was there, why didn't he come? We asked him. We asked him every day.* A week later her friend Anita came over with a meal. She did not try to explain anything. She sat on the sofa beside Ruth and cried with her for most of an hour. Then she asked whether she could read something, and she read John 11, slowly, from Martha's 'Lord, if thou hadst been here' to 'Jesus wept'. She said, *Ruth, Martha said exactly what you said, and Jesus didn't scold her. He cried with her. And then he told her who he is.* She read John 11:25–26 twice. Ruth still grieves. But she told her small group months later that the day Anita read that chapter was the day she stopped thinking of God as the one who failed to come, and began to think of Jesus as the one who stood weeping at Josh's grave, and who will one day call him out of it.

### 📜 Voices from Church History
The Westminster Shorter Catechism, written by the Westminster Assembly in the 1640s (verify the date), asks in two short questions what believers receive from Christ at death and at the resurrection. Summarized in plain words (not quoted): Question 37 answers that at death the souls of believers are made perfect in holiness and pass at once into glory, while their bodies, still united to Christ, rest in their graves until the resurrection. Question 38 answers that at the resurrection believers, raised up in glory, will be openly acknowledged and acquitted in the day of judgment and made perfectly blessed in enjoying God for ever (paraphrased, verify the wording). The two answers follow the shape of John 11:25–26. 'Though he were dead, yet shall he live' (John 11:25): the body goes to the grave, but the believer lives with Christ now and will be raised. 'Shall never die' (John 11:26): the life Christ gives is not interrupted by death. And the catechism's phrase about bodies 'still united to Christ' echoes the sleep of John 11:11: the dead in Christ are not lost, only resting until his voice.

### ⛪ Doctrine in My Own Words
- **Theology Proper:** God orders sickness, delay, and death to show his glory and to glorify his Son, and he does it in love, not in spite of it (John 11:4–6, 40).
- **Christology:** Jesus is himself the resurrection and the life: resurrection and eternal life are found in him now, not only at the last day (John 11:25–26).
- **Christology:** At Lazarus's grave Jesus wept as a true man and called the dead to life as the true God, one person with both natures (John 11:35, 43).
- **Anthropology & Sin:** Death is an enemy, not a friend, and the human heart is so dark that it can see God's greatest work and plot against the one who did it (John 11:39, 47–53).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** Jesus died for the people, in their place, so that they would not perish, and his death gathers God's scattered children into one (John 11:50–52).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** The dead can only live when Christ's voice calls them, and his call gives the life it commands (John 11:43–44).
- **Ecclesiology:** Christ's death makes one people of God out of Israel and the nations, and he uses his people to roll away stones and loose those he has raised (John 11:44, 52).
- **Eschatology:** The believer who dies lives with Christ and will rise at the last day; for believers death is a sleep from which Christ will wake them (John 11:11, 24–26).
- **Christian Walk:** Believers bring their grief honestly to Christ, trust his word in the delay, and pray to the Father who always hears the Son (John 11:21–22, 40–42).

### 👑 Christocentric & Humility · Level 4
Every problem in John 11 is answered in Jesus. A man he loves is sick; the sickness is not unto death but for the glory of God, because Jesus is Lord of its outcome (John 11:4). The disciples are afraid of stones in Judaea; he walks in the day his Father has given him, and his hour has not yet come (John 11:9). They misunderstand his words about sleep; he tells them plainly (John 11:14). Martha has a true doctrine but a far-off hope; he gives her himself, the resurrection present in person (John 11:25). Mary and the mourners weep; he weeps with them (John 11:35). The body stinks and the stone is heavy; he commands and prays and calls (John 11:39–43). The raised man is still bound; he says, 'Loose him, and let him go' (John 11:44). And the deepest problem of all, the plot against the life-giver, becomes the answer to death itself: one man dies for the people, so that the children of God are gathered and do not perish (John 11:50–52).

Every truth in the chapter is found in him. God's glory is seen in him (John 11:4, 40). Resurrection and life are in him (John 11:25). True sympathy is in him, the man of sorrows (John 11:35). Access to the Father is in him, the Son whom the Father always hears (John 11:42). Power over death is in his voice (John 11:43). And atonement is in his death, the one man for the people (John 11:50). The chapter humbles us because it shows that we are as helpless before death as Lazarus in his cave: we cannot hear, cannot move, cannot unwrap ourselves, and our best religious leaders can only calculate. It humbles us too because it shows how slowly we believe: disciples, sisters, and mourners all needed to be taught. And it makes Christ glorious because the Lord of life walked toward his own death to give life to his friend, and wept at a grave he was about to empty.

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy
### 📌 Core Concept
Jesus had three friends who lived in a village called Bethany: two sisters, Martha and Mary, and their brother Lazarus. One day Lazarus got very sick, and the sisters sent a message to Jesus: *the friend you love is sick.* Jesus loved them very much. But instead of hurrying, he waited two more days. By the time he arrived, Lazarus had died and had been in his tomb, a cave with a big stone in front of it, for four days. Martha and Mary were heartbroken. Each of them said to Jesus, *If you had been here, our brother would not have died.* Jesus told Martha something amazing: he himself is the resurrection and the life. That means that people who trust him will live again even after they die, and that the life he gives them never ends. Then Jesus went to the tomb, and when he saw everyone crying, he cried too. He really was sad, even though he knew what he was about to do. He told the people to roll the stone away. He prayed to his Father. And then he shouted, *Lazarus, come out!* And Lazarus, who had been dead, walked out of the tomb, still wrapped in his burial cloths. Many people believed in Jesus that day. But some of the religious leaders were so afraid of losing their power that they decided to kill Jesus. They didn't know it, but God was going to use Jesus' death to save his people.

### 📖 Key Verses
"Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:" (John 11:25) Jesus is stronger than death, and everyone who trusts him will live again.

"Jesus wept." (John 11:35) Jesus cried with his friends, so we know he cares when we are sad.

"And when he thus had spoken, he cried with a loud voice, Lazarus, come forth." (John 11:43) Jesus only had to speak, and a dead man came back to life.

"And he that was dead came forth, bound hand and foot with graveclothes: and his face was bound about with a napkin. Jesus saith unto them, Loose him, and let him go." (John 11:44) Jesus gave Lazarus life, and then he asked the people to help him get free of his wrappings.

### 🪞 Analogy
Imagine a little boy whose goldfish has died. He is crying, and his dad comes and sits beside him on the floor. His dad doesn't say, *It's only a fish,* and he doesn't say, *Stop crying, I'll buy you another one.* He puts his arm around his son and he cries a little too, because he loves his son and he is sad that his son is sad. That is a small picture of what Jesus did at Lazarus's grave: he cried with the people he loved. But Jesus could do something no dad can do with a goldfish. After he cried, he called his friend's name, and his friend came back to life. Jesus is the friend who is sad with us, and he is also the Lord who is stronger than death.

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium
### 📜 Walkthrough
#### John 11:1–4 · Word from Bethany
**Says:** "Now a certain man was sick, named Lazarus, of Bethany, the town of Mary and her sister Martha." (John 11:1) "(It was that Mary which anointed the Lord with ointment, and wiped his feet with her hair, whose brother Lazarus was sick.)" (John 11:2) "Therefore his sisters sent unto him, saying, Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest is sick." (John 11:3) "When Jesus heard that, he said, This sickness is not unto death, but for the glory of God, that the Son of God might be glorified thereby." (John 11:4) *Sick* here means weak or ill; *thereby* means by means of it.

**Means:** John introduces a family by name: Lazarus, whose name is a shortened form of the Hebrew Eleazar (the STEPBible lexicon), and his sisters Martha and Mary. Verse 2 is one of John's narrator's asides. He identifies Mary by an event he has not yet told, the anointing of John 12:1–8, which tells us he writes for readers who already know the story (compare Mark 14:3–9, where the woman is not named). The sisters' message is a model of prayer. They do not tell Jesus what to do; they tell him who is in need and appeal to his love: 'he whom thou lovest is sick'. Jesus' answer has two layers. 'This sickness is not unto death' does not mean Lazarus will not die; he does (John 11:14). It means that death will not be the end of the story. The sickness is 'for the glory of God', and in the same breath Jesus says that 'the Son of God' is glorified by it, placing his own glory beside the Father's. In John, the glory of the Son comes to its height in his death and resurrection (John 12:23; 17:1), and this sign will lead straight to both: the raising of Lazarus is the event that moves the council to plan Jesus' death (John 11:53). The glory, then, is double. God is glorified when the dead man lives, and the Son is glorified by the path to the cross that this sign opens.

**Asks:** When you pray for someone who is sick or in trouble, do you, like the sisters, bring the need to Jesus and rest it on his love, or do you tell him what he must do and when? Can you believe that a sickness may be 'for the glory of God' without believing that God does not care?

#### John 11:5–10 · Love That Waits, and Twelve Hours in the Day
**Says:** "Now Jesus loved Martha, and her sister, and Lazarus." (John 11:5) "When he had heard therefore that he was sick, he abode two days still in the same place where he was." (John 11:6) "Then after that saith he to his disciples, Let us go into Judaea again." (John 11:7) "His disciples say unto him, Master, the Jews of late sought to stone thee; and goest thou thither again?" (John 11:8) "Jesus answered, Are there not twelve hours in the day? If any man walk in the day, he stumbleth not, because he seeth the light of this world." (John 11:9) "But if a man walk in the night, he stumbleth, because there is no light in him." (John 11:10) *Abode* means stayed; *of late* means recently; *thither* means there.

**Means:** Verses 5 and 6 are among the most surprising sentences in the Gospel. John says Jesus loved all three, naming Martha first, and then says that *therefore*, when he heard, he stayed where he was for two more days. The delay is not a failure of love; John presents it as an expression of it. Jesus waits so that the sign will be greater, the faith of the disciples stronger (John 11:15), and the glory of God clearer (John 11:4, 40). Many interpreters reckon the timing this way: a day for the messenger to reach Jesus, the two days of waiting, and a day's journey to Bethany make up the four days of John 11:17, so that Lazarus may already have died by the time the messenger arrived (verify; John does not give the travel times). If so, Jesus' waiting did not cause the death; it meant that when he came, no one could doubt it was a death. Then Jesus says, 'Let us go into Judaea again', and the disciples remember the stones of John 8:59 and John 10:31. Jesus answers with a proverb. A working day has 'twelve hours' of daylight, and a man walking in daylight does not trip. His own 'day' is the time his Father has appointed for his work, and until his hour comes, no one can cut it short (John 7:30; 9:4). But the saying has a second edge, turned toward the listeners: the one who walks 'in the night' stumbles 'because there is no light in him'. Jesus has just said he is the light of the world (John 8:12; 9:5). To walk with him is to walk in the day; to walk without him is to stumble in the dark, whatever the clock says.

**Asks:** What delay in your life have you read as a sign that Jesus does not love you? How does John 11:5–6 change the way you read it? And where are you trying to walk without the light, trusting your own sense of danger or safety instead of Christ's word?

#### John 11:11–16 · Our Friend Sleepeth
**Says:** "These things said he: and after that he saith unto them, Our friend Lazarus sleepeth; but I go, that I may awake him out of sleep." (John 11:11) "Then said his disciples, Lord, if he sleep, he shall do well." (John 11:12) "Howbeit Jesus spake of his death: but they thought that he had spoken of taking of rest in sleep." (John 11:13) "Then said Jesus unto them plainly, Lazarus is dead." (John 11:14) "And I am glad for your sakes that I was not there, to the intent ye may believe; nevertheless let us go unto him." (John 11:15) "Then said Thomas, which is called Didymus, unto his fellowdisciples, Let us also go, that we may die with him." (John 11:16) *Howbeit* means however; *to the intent* means so that; *Didymus* means twin.

**Means:** Jesus calls Lazarus 'Our friend', including the disciples in his love. He speaks of death as sleep, and he speaks of himself as the one who will wake the sleeper, which is exactly what he will do. The picture of death as sleep was already in the Old Testament (Daniel 12:2) and becomes the ordinary Christian way of speaking of believers who have died (Acts 7:60; 1 Corinthians 15:20; 1 Thessalonians 4:13–14). It does not mean that the dead are unconscious (see the Debate Corner); it means that for those who belong to Christ, death is as temporary as sleep, and he will wake them. The disciples misunderstand, as characters in John so often do (John 2:20; 3:4; 4:11; 6:52): they hear 'sleep' and think Lazarus is getting the rest that heals. There is a small irony in their words 'he shall do well', because the Greek verb behind them is the ordinary verb *to be saved* (see the Deep Dive): they mean *he will recover*, but the reader knows how much more Lazarus will be saved. So Jesus speaks 'plainly', and then says something startling: he is 'glad', for their sakes, that he was not there, because what they are about to see will make them believe. Faith in John is not a single moment but a growing trust; the disciples already believed (John 2:11), and they need to believe more deeply. Thomas speaks next; the STEPBible lexicon traces his name to a Hebrew word for *twin*, and his Greek surname, Didymus, means the same. He expects that going back to Judaea means dying with Jesus, and he says so: 'Let us also go, that we may die with him'. It is a brave, loyal, gloomy sentence, the words of a man who can see the danger and not yet the resurrection. The same Thomas will later doubt and then confess, 'My Lord and my God' (John 20:28).

**Asks:** Do you think of the death of believers as Jesus did, as a sleep from which he will wake them? And where are you like Thomas, willing to follow Jesus into danger but unable yet to see anything beyond it?

#### John 11:17–22 · Four Days, and Martha's Even Now
**Says:** "Then when Jesus came, he found that he had lain in the grave four days already." (John 11:17) "Now Bethany was nigh unto Jerusalem, about fifteen furlongs off:" (John 11:18) "And many of the Jews came to Martha and Mary, to comfort them concerning their brother." (John 11:19) "Then Martha, as soon as she heard that Jesus was coming, went and met him: but Mary sat still in the house." (John 11:20) "Then said Martha unto Jesus, Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died." (John 11:21) "But I know, that even now, whatsoever thou wilt ask of God, God will give it thee." (John 11:22) *Nigh* means near; a *furlong* is about an eighth of a mile, so fifteen furlongs is a little under two miles.

**Means:** 'Four days' settles any doubt: Lazarus is not in a coma. The detail about the distance from Jerusalem explains why 'many of the Jews' are there: they have come out from the city to comfort the family, and so there will be many witnesses of what happens next, including some who will report it to the Pharisees (John 11:45–46). As in Luke 10:38–42, the sisters act in character: Martha goes out to meet Jesus, and Mary sits in the house. Martha's first words are a lament, not an accusation, but they carry pain: 'if thou hadst been here'. She believes in his power; what she cannot understand is his absence. Then she adds, 'even now', a sentence of stubborn faith. She does not dare say *raise him*, but she believes that God will give Jesus whatever he asks. Her faith is real, but it still pictures Jesus as a man whose prayers God answers, not yet as the one who has life in himself (John 5:26).

**Asks:** What do you say to Jesus when he seems to have come too late? Can you, like Martha, hold both 'if thou hadst been here' and 'even now' in the same breath, bringing your grief and your faith to him together?

#### John 11:23–27 · I Am the Resurrection, and the Life
**Says:** "Jesus saith unto her, Thy brother shall rise again." (John 11:23) "Martha saith unto him, I know that he shall rise again in the resurrection at the last day." (John 11:24) "Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:" (John 11:25) "And whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this?" (John 11:26) "She saith unto him, Yea, Lord: I believe that thou art the Christ, the Son of God, which should come into the world." (John 11:27) *Whosoever* means whoever; *which should come* means who was to come.

**Means:** Jesus' first sentence, 'Thy brother shall rise again', could be heard as ordinary comfort at a funeral, and Martha hears it that way. She agrees with the orthodox hope of her people: the dead will rise 'at the last day' (Daniel 12:2; John 5:28–29; 6:40). But Jesus is not offering a doctrine; he is offering himself. 'I am the resurrection, and the life' is the fifth of the 'I am' sayings with an image in John, and the emphatic 'I' stands first in the Greek (see the Deep Dive). The resurrection Martha expects at the end of history is standing in front of her in person, because the one who will raise the dead at the last day has the power of resurrection now. He then unfolds the claim in two parallel promises. The first is for the believer who dies: 'though he were dead, yet shall he live'. Physical death is real, but it is not the end; the believer will live, both now with Christ and in the resurrection. The second is for the believer who is living: 'whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die'. The life Christ gives now is eternal life, which bodily death cannot break (John 5:24; 8:51). Then Jesus asks, 'Believest thou this', and Martha answers with one of the great confessions of the Gospel: 'thou art the Christ, the Son of God, which should come into the world'. These are almost exactly the titles of John's purpose statement (John 20:31). Martha may not yet understand all that Jesus has said, but she confesses who he is, and that is the point: faith rests on the person before it grasps every promise.

**Asks:** Is your hope in the resurrection a belief about the future, or trust in a person who is present with you now? If Jesus asked you today, 'Believest thou this', what would you say, and what would you need to confess to say it honestly?

#### John 11:28–32 · The Master Is Come, and Calleth for Thee
**Says:** "And when she had so said, she went her way, and called Mary her sister secretly, saying, The Master is come, and calleth for thee." (John 11:28) "As soon as she heard that, she arose quickly, and came unto him." (John 11:29) "Now Jesus was not yet come into the town, but was in that place where Martha met him." (John 11:30) "The Jews then which were with her in the house, and comforted her, when they saw Mary, that she rose up hastily and went out, followed her, saying, She goeth unto the grave to weep there." (John 11:31) "Then when Mary was come where Jesus was, and saw him, she fell down at his feet, saying unto him, Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died." (John 11:32) *Master* means teacher; *hastily* means quickly.

**Means:** Martha becomes a messenger. She carries a private word to her sister: 'The Master is come, and calleth for thee'. John has not recorded Jesus asking for Mary, but Martha says he did, and Mary rises at once. Jesus stays outside the village, perhaps to meet the sisters away from the crowd, and perhaps because he is going toward the tomb. The mourners follow Mary, assuming she is going to the grave to weep, so the crowd of witnesses is gathered to the place where the sign will happen. Mary does what she always does in the Gospels: she is at Jesus' feet (Luke 10:39; John 12:3). And she says exactly what Martha said, word for word in the KJV. The sisters had probably said it to each other many times in those four days. But Mary adds nothing: no 'even now', no confession. She only falls down and weeps. Grief has left her with one sentence, and she brings it to him.

**Asks:** Who has carried Jesus' call to you, as Martha carried it to Mary? Whom could you tell today, 'The Master is come, and calleth for thee'? And when all you have left is one sentence of grief, will you bring it to his feet?

#### John 11:33–37 · Jesus Wept
**Says:** "When Jesus therefore saw her weeping, and the Jews also weeping which came with her, he groaned in the spirit, and was troubled," (John 11:33) "And said, Where have ye laid him? They said unto him, Lord, come and see." (John 11:34) "Jesus wept." (John 11:35) "Then said the Jews, Behold how he loved him!" (John 11:36) "And some of them said, Could not this man, which opened the eyes of the blind, have caused that even this man should not have died?" (John 11:37) *Groaned* here means was deeply stirred; *troubled* means shaken, disturbed.

**Means:** Jesus answers Mary not with words but with his own heart. John piles up the language of emotion: he 'groaned in the spirit', he 'was troubled', he 'wept'. The word behind 'groaned' is a strong one; outside this chapter it is used of a stern charge or of indignation (Matthew 9:30; Mark 1:43; 14:5), and many interpreters hear anger in it here, anger at death, at the sin that brought it, and at the power of the enemy over God's creatures (see View A / View B and the Council's second question). 'Was troubled' is literally *troubled himself* (see the Deep Dive), which suggests that his distress was not something that simply happened to him but something he took on. Then he asks, 'Where have ye laid him', and they answer, 'Lord, come and see', the same words Jesus used when he invited his first disciples (John 1:39). And 'Jesus wept', a verse often called the shortest in the English Bible. The verb for his weeping is different from the loud wailing of Mary and the mourners in verse 33; it describes quiet tears (see the Deep Dive). The onlookers draw two conclusions, and John records both. Some see love: 'Behold how he loved him'. Others see a contradiction: if he could heal the blind man of John 9, why did he not prevent this? They have seen enough to raise the right question and not enough to wait for the answer.

**Asks:** What does it mean to you that the Son of God wept at a grave he was about to empty? When you face grief, your own or someone else's, which is harder for you: to weep, or to wait? And when you see others suffering, are you quicker to say 'Behold how he loved him' or 'Could not this man' have prevented it?

#### John 11:38–42 · Take Ye Away the Stone
**Says:** "Jesus therefore again groaning in himself cometh to the grave. It was a cave, and a stone lay upon it." (John 11:38) "Jesus said, Take ye away the stone. Martha, the sister of him that was dead, saith unto him, Lord, by this time he stinketh: for he hath been dead four days." (John 11:39) "Jesus saith unto her, Said I not unto thee, that, if thou wouldest believe, thou shouldest see the glory of God?" (John 11:40) "Then they took away the stone from the place where the dead was laid. And Jesus lifted up his eyes, and said, Father, I thank thee that thou hast heard me." (John 11:41) "And I knew that thou hearest me always: but because of the people which stand by I said it, that they may believe that thou hast sent me." (John 11:42) *Stinketh* means gives off the smell of decay.

**Means:** Jesus comes to the tomb 'again groaning', the same strong word as before, as he faces death itself. The tomb is a cave closed with a stone, like the one in which he himself will be laid (John 20:1). His first command is to the bystanders: 'Take ye away the stone'. He could have moved it by a word, but he gives the people a part in the work. Martha, practical to the end, objects: after four days, the body will smell. Her faith had said 'even now' (John 11:22), but at the edge of the tomb it falters before the plain facts of decay. Jesus does not rebuke her harshly; he recalls her to his word. John has not recorded the exact sentence 'if thou wouldest believe, thou shouldest see the glory of God', but it gathers up what Jesus said to the messengers (John 11:4) and to Martha (John 11:25–26). The stone is moved, and Jesus prays. He does not ask; he gives thanks, in the past tense: 'thou hast heard me'. His prayer has already been answered, because he is the Son who can say to the Father, 'thou hearest me always'. He says the thanksgiving aloud for the crowd, so that the sign will not be mistaken for magic or for a power of his own apart from the Father, but will be seen as the Father's witness 'that thou hast sent me'. The sign is a revelation of the relationship between the Father and the Son (John 5:19–21, 36).

**Asks:** What 'stone' has Jesus asked you to move, in your own life or someone else's, before you see what he will do? When the facts seem to argue against his promise, what word of his do you need him to recall to you, as he recalled Martha to 'Said I not unto thee'?

#### John 11:43–44 · Lazarus, Come Forth
**Says:** "And when he thus had spoken, he cried with a loud voice, Lazarus, come forth." (John 11:43) "And he that was dead came forth, bound hand and foot with graveclothes: and his face was bound about with a napkin. Jesus saith unto them, Loose him, and let him go." (John 11:44) *Graveclothes* are the strips of cloth wrapped around a body for burial; a *napkin* here is a cloth over the face; *loose* means untie.

**Means:** Jesus 'cried with a loud voice'. He does not touch the body, as he touched the bier at Nain (Luke 7:14), or take the hand, as with Jairus's daughter (Luke 8:54). He speaks, and he speaks one name. Many preachers have observed that if he had not named Lazarus, every grave would have opened (verify the source of the saying; it is often attributed to Augustine or to later preachers). The point stands without the saying: his voice has that power, and one day it will be used for all (John 5:28–29). This is what Jesus promised in John 5:25: the dead 'shall hear the voice of the Son of God: and they that hear shall live'. The verb for his cry is the same one the crowd will use when they shout for Barabbas and cry out for Jesus' crucifixion (John 18:40; 19:6, 15; see the Deep Dive): the voice that calls the dead to life will be answered by the voices that call for his death. 'And he that was dead came forth', still wrapped, his face covered, a detail that sets Lazarus's rising apart from Jesus' own: Lazarus comes out bound, needing others to free him, and will die again; Jesus will leave his grave clothes lying in order, 'the napkin, that was about his head' folded by itself (John 20:7), and will never die again (Romans 6:9). Jesus' last command is to the bystanders: 'Loose him, and let him go'. Only Christ gives life, but he calls his people to help the newly living out of what still binds them.

**Asks:** Have you heard Christ's voice call you out of spiritual death (Ephesians 2:1, 5)? And who around you has been given new life but is still bound by old wrappings, habits, shame, fear, or lies, that you could help to loose in his name?

#### John 11:45–48 · Many Believed, and Some Reported
**Says:** "Then many of the Jews which came to Mary, and had seen the things which Jesus did, believed on him." (John 11:45) "But some of them went their ways to the Pharisees, and told them what things Jesus had done." (John 11:46) "Then gathered the chief priests and the Pharisees a council, and said, What do we? for this man doeth many miracles." (John 11:47) "If we let him thus alone, all men will believe on him: and the Romans shall come and take away both our place and nation." (John 11:48) *What do we* means what are we doing, or what shall we do; *our place* most likely means the temple, or their position.

**Means:** As so often in John, the sign divides its witnesses (John 7:43; 9:16; 10:19). 'Many' believe. 'Some' go to the Pharisees, whether as worried informants or as enemies, John does not say. The chief priests, mostly Sadducees who denied the resurrection (Acts 23:8), and the Pharisees, who affirmed it, meet together in council. Their words are an astonishing admission: 'this man doeth many miracles'. They do not deny the signs; they fear their effect. If he goes on, 'all men will believe on him', and the Romans will take away 'our place and nation'. 'Our place' probably means the temple (the same word is used of it in Acts 6:13–14; verify), and perhaps their whole position of authority with it. Their fear is political and religious at once: a popular Messiah could provoke a revolt, and Rome would crush it. The tragic irony is that they will get rid of Jesus to save the temple and the nation, and within a generation, in AD 70, the Romans will destroy both anyway (verify the historical details in a standard history).

**Asks:** When you have seen the evidence of what Jesus can do, what has been your first question: how to believe, or what it will cost you? What 'place' do you guard so carefully that you would rather manage Jesus than follow him?

#### John 11:49–53 · One Man for the People
**Says:** "And one of them, named Caiaphas, being the high priest that same year, said unto them, Ye know nothing at all," (John 11:49) "Nor consider that it is expedient for us, that one man should die for the people, and that the whole nation perish not." (John 11:50) "And this spake he not of himself: but being high priest that year, he prophesied that Jesus should die for that nation;" (John 11:51) "And not for that nation only, but that also he should gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad." (John 11:52) "Then from that day forth they took counsel together for to put him to death." (John 11:53) *Expedient* means to our advantage; *took counsel* means made plans.

**Means:** Caiaphas cuts through the council's anxious talk with contempt, 'Ye know nothing at all', and a cold calculation: better for one man to die than for the whole nation to perish. He is proposing to sacrifice an innocent man for the sake of political safety. 'That same year' does not mean the high priesthood was an annual office; John uses the phrase for that fateful year, the year of Jesus' death (John 18:13). Then John, the narrator, steps in with one of the most remarkable comments in the Gospel. Caiaphas did not say this 'of himself'. As high priest, the man whose office was to stand between God and the people, he 'prophesied', speaking words that meant far more than he intended. God spoke through him, as he once spoke through the unwilling Balaam (Numbers 23–24). Caiaphas meant *substitution for our safety*; God meant substitution for their salvation. Jesus would die 'for that nation', on its behalf and in its place, so that it would not perish (compare John 3:16), 'And not for that nation only'. His death would 'gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad'. John is thinking of the 'other sheep' of John 10:16, God's people among the Gentiles as well as the Jews dispersed abroad, who are 'children of God' before they are gathered, because the Father has given them to the Son (John 1:12; 6:37; 10:16, 29). The gathering is by the death of one man. And from that day they plot to kill him: the raising of Lazarus is the direct cause of the cross.

**Asks:** Caiaphas used a truth to justify a crime; God used a crime to accomplish salvation. Where do you see God's purpose overruling human evil (Genesis 50:20; Acts 4:27–28)? And what does it mean for you personally that Jesus died 'for the people', in their place, and that you, if you belong to him, are one of the scattered children he gathers?

#### John 11:54–57 · Ephraim, and the Passover Near
**Says:** "Jesus therefore walked no more openly among the Jews; but went thence unto a country near to the wilderness, into a city called Ephraim, and there continued with his disciples." (John 11:54) "And the Jews' passover was nigh at hand: and many went out of the country up to Jerusalem before the passover, to purify themselves." (John 11:55) "Then sought they for Jesus, and spake among themselves, as they stood in the temple, What think ye, that he will not come to the feast?" (John 11:56) "Now both the chief priests and the Pharisees had given a commandment, that, if any man knew where he were, he should shew it, that they might take him." (John 11:57) *Thence* means from there; *continued* means stayed; *shew* means make known, report.

**Means:** Jesus withdraws, not out of fear but because his hour has not yet come; he will go to Jerusalem at the Passover, at the time the Father has set (John 12:1, 23). Ephraim is usually identified with a town north-east of Jerusalem on the edge of the Judean wilderness (verify the identification). John marks the season: this is the third Passover he names (John 2:13; 6:4), and the last. Pilgrims come early 'to purify themselves', because anyone made unclean, for example by touching a dead body, had to be cleansed before eating the Passover (Numbers 9:6–10; 19:11–12; 2 Chronicles 30:17–18). The irony is deep: the crowds are purifying themselves for the feast while their rulers plot to kill the true Passover lamb (John 1:29; 19:36). In the temple people wonder aloud whether Jesus will dare to come, and the rulers have issued an order for anyone who knows where he is to report him. The man who gave life to Lazarus is now a wanted man. The stage is set for John 12, and for the hour.

**Asks:** The crowds carefully purified their bodies for the feast while missing the one to whom the feast pointed. Where might you be keeping the outward forms of faith while missing Christ himself? And are you watching for him, as they were, with hostility, curiosity, or longing?

### 🧨 Cultural Landmines and Misconceptions
**Grief as a lack of faith.** Some believers think that if they truly trust God, they should not cry at funerals, or that showing grief dishonours the hope of the resurrection. John 11 answers that directly: Jesus, who knew exactly what he was about to do, wept (John 11:35). Paul does not tell the Thessalonians not to grieve, but not to grieve 'even as others which have no hope' (1 Thessalonians 4:13). Christian grief is real grief, carried in hope.

**'He's in a better place, so don't be sad.'** Many modern funerals rush from death to comfort, as if death were natural and nothing to mourn. Jesus did not treat death as natural. He groaned and was troubled (John 11:33, 38), and Scripture calls death 'The last enemy' (1 Corinthians 15:26). The hope of heaven is true, but it does not make death good; it makes death defeated.

**The 'celebration of life' without resurrection.** In many cultures today, funerals celebrate a person's achievements and memories with little or no mention of what happens after death. John 11 points the other way: the greatest comfort is not what the dead did but who Christ is, 'the resurrection, and the life' (John 11:25). Remembering a life well lived is good; it is not the Christian hope.

**Lazarus as a symbol only.** Some modern readers treat the raising of Lazarus as a symbolic story about spiritual renewal, not a historical event. John tells it with names, places, distances, days, and reactions, and he makes it the event that leads directly to the plot against Jesus (John 11:18, 47–53; 12:9–11). It has spiritual meaning because it happened.

**'The Jews' in John 11.** In this chapter 'the Jews' who comfort the sisters, weep with them, and believe on Jesus (John 11:19, 31, 36, 45) are Jewish neighbours and friends, and 'the Jews' who sought to stone him (John 11:8) are the Jerusalem leaders and crowd of an earlier moment. Martha, Mary, Lazarus, and Jesus himself were Jews. The chapter must never be taught as a verdict on the Jewish people; it shows both belief and opposition within Israel, as in every people.

**Two different Lazaruses, two different Marys.** Readers sometimes confuse Lazarus of Bethany with the beggar Lazarus in Jesus' parable (Luke 16:19–31), or Mary of Bethany with Mary Magdalene. They are different people. It is striking, though, that the parable ends with Abraham's words, 'neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead' (Luke 16:31), and that in John 11 a man named Lazarus does rise, and the rulers respond by plotting to kill him (John 12:10).

### 🏗️ Theological Framework
John 11 is the climax of the Book of Signs and the hinge of the Gospel. The last and greatest sign of the public ministry is also the event that sends Jesus to the cross (John 11:53; 12:9–11, 17–19). Read through the Reformed conviction that Scripture interprets Scripture, the chapter gathers around seven heads.

*The resurrection as a person: Christology and eschatology joined.* Martha holds the orthodox Jewish hope of a general resurrection 'at the last day' (John 11:24; Daniel 12:2). Jesus does not deny it; he affirms it elsewhere in the strongest terms (John 5:28–29; 6:39–40, 44, 54). But he relocates it: 'I am the resurrection, and the life' (John 11:25). The resurrection is not first an event but a person, and whoever is joined to him by faith already possesses the life of the age to come. This is the Johannine form of what theologians call inaugurated eschatology, the *already* and the *not yet*. 'The hour is coming, and now is' (John 5:25): the dead in sin hear the Son's voice now and live, and the dead in the graves will hear it at the last day and come forth (John 5:28–29). John 11:25 speaks to the believer who dies ('yet shall he live'), and John 11:26 to the believer who lives ('shall never die'); together they teach that union with Christ is a life that bodily death cannot interrupt and that the resurrection of the body will complete.

*The two natures in one person.* No chapter in the Gospels sets Christ's humanity and deity closer together. He loves particular friends (John 11:5), is moved with deep emotion and troubled (John 11:33, 38), asks where the body lies (John 11:34), and weeps (John 11:35). He also knows that Lazarus has died without being told (John 11:14), speaks of himself as the resurrection and the life (John 11:25), prays as the Son whom the Father always hears (John 11:41–42), and calls the dead out of the tomb by his word (John 11:43). The church's confession at Chalcedon in 451, that Christ is one person in two natures, truly God and truly man, without confusion, change, division, or separation (summarized, not quoted), is the church's account of this chapter. Pope Leo I's *Tome* to Flavian (449), read at Chalcedon, is often summarized as saying that it belongs to the humanity to weep for a dead friend and to the deity to raise him, while both are the acts of one and the same Christ (paraphrased, verify the wording and the reference to Lazarus). The Westminster Confession teaches that Christ in his work acts according to both natures, each doing what is proper to it, while what is proper to one nature is sometimes ascribed to the person named by the other (chapter 8, sections 2 and 7, paraphrased, verify).

*Providence and the glory of God in suffering.* 'This sickness is not unto death, but for the glory of God' (John 11:4). The Reformed doctrine of providence holds that God upholds, directs, disposes, and governs all creatures, actions, and things, from the greatest to the least, for the praise of the glory of his wisdom, power, justice, goodness, and mercy (Westminster Confession, chapter 5, section 1, paraphrased, verify). John 11 shows providence with a human face: the Son who loves the family waits (John 11:5–6), and the waiting serves the glory of God, the faith of the disciples, and the good of the sisters (John 11:4, 15, 40). The chapter does not say that God causes every sickness for a sign; it says that this one was given a purpose, and it shows that God's purpose and his love are not rivals (compare Romans 8:28).

*Effectual calling and the voice that raises the dead.* The raising of Lazarus is the clearest picture in the Gospels of what Jesus said in John 5:21 and 5:25: the Son quickens whom he will, and the dead hear his voice and live. Reformed theology has long read it as an acted parable of regeneration: the sinner, 'dead in trespasses and sins' (Ephesians 2:1), can no more hear, move, or free himself than Lazarus in his cave, until the voice of Christ calls him effectually by name. The Canons of Dort compare the regeneration God works to creation and to the raising of the dead, as a work not of moral persuasion but of his almighty power (third and fourth heads, article 12, paraphrased, verify), and the Westminster Confession teaches that in effectual calling God enlightens the mind, renews the will, and draws the sinner effectually to Christ, yet so that he comes most freely (chapter 10, sections 1–2, paraphrased, verify). The picture must not be pressed beyond its point: Lazarus was a believer's brother and Jesus' friend before he died, and the sign is first a sign of the bodily resurrection. But Jesus himself joins the two (John 5:24–29), and so may we.

*Substitution and the gathering of God's children.* Caiaphas's sentence is a thesis in atonement theology spoken by a man who did not believe it: one man should die *for* the people, *huper* (G5228, John 11:50), on their behalf and in their place, 'that the whole nation perish not'. John takes it up and widens it: Jesus would die 'for that nation', and to 'gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad' (John 11:51–52). Reformed theology finds here the substitutionary character of Christ's death (compare Isaiah 53:5–6; 2 Corinthians 5:21) and its intended effect: to gather a particular people, 'the children of God', from Israel and the nations into one (John 10:16; 17:20–21). The doctrine of definite or particular redemption holds that Christ died with the intention of saving those the Father had given him, and that his death secures their gathering (Canons of Dort, second head, article 8, paraphrased, verify). Others read the text differently: Arminian and Wesleyan theology holds that 'the children of God' names those whom God foresaw would believe, and that Christ's death made salvation possible for all while effective for believers (compare John 3:16; 1 John 2:2). Both readings agree that the gathering reaches beyond Israel and that it happens through Christ's death; the Reformed reading argues that the verse speaks of a death that actually gathers, not one that only makes gathering possible.

*God's sovereignty over human sin.* Caiaphas 'prophesied' while plotting murder (John 11:51, 53). The Reformed doctrine of providence holds that God's decree extends even to sinful acts, not by bare permission only, but by ordering and governing them to his own holy ends, while the sinfulness proceeds only from the creature and God neither is nor can be the author of sin (Westminster Confession, chapter 3, section 1, and chapter 5, section 4, paraphrased, verify). John 11 is a narrative of that doctrine, as are Genesis 50:20 and Acts 2:23 and 4:27–28: Caiaphas is fully responsible for his counsel (John 18:14; 19:11), and God fully sovereign over its outcome.

*The state of the dead and the last day.* 'Our friend Lazarus sleepeth' (John 11:11) and the double promise of John 11:25–26 bear on what happens between death and the resurrection. The Westminster Confession teaches that after death the bodies of men return to dust, but their souls, which neither die nor sleep, return at once to God; the souls of the righteous, made perfect in holiness, are received into heaven to await the full redemption of their bodies (chapter 32, section 1, paraphrased, verify). The Debate Corner below states the alternatives.

### 📚 Scholarly Insights
- **Augustine**, *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (Tractate 49, verify): reads the raising of Lazarus both as a real miracle and as a sign of the raising of the soul dead in sin; compares the stone to the weight of the law or of habitual sin, and the loosing of the grave clothes to the ministry of the church in releasing the penitent (verify each); these allegorical applications are Augustine's, not John's.
- **John Chrysostom**, *Homilies on the Gospel of John* (Homilies 62–64, verify): stresses the reality of Christ's human emotion and his restraint of it, explains that Jesus prayed aloud not because he needed to but for the sake of the weak faith of the bystanders, and warns his hearers against excessive mourning for Christian dead (verify each).
- **Leo the Great**, *Tome to Flavian* (449, verify): uses the contrast between Christ weeping for Lazarus and raising him as an example of the two natures acting in one person (verify the wording).
- **John Calvin**, *Commentary on the Gospel According to John*: on John 11:33 argues that Christ took on true human affections, though without sin, and that his groaning shows him preparing for a contest with death as a champion goes into battle; on John 11:51 explains that God made Caiaphas's tongue an instrument of prophecy without making him a prophet (verify each).
- **B. B. Warfield**, 'On the Emotional Life of Our Lord' (1912, in *The Person and Work of Christ*, verify): argues that the verb behind 'groaned' denotes indignant anger, and pictures Jesus advancing to the tomb in a rage against death and him who has the power of death (verify the wording and location).
- **J. C. Ryle**, *Expository Thoughts on the Gospels* (John): draws from John 11 lessons on sickness among those Christ loves, the reality of his sympathy, and the certainty of the resurrection (verify).
- **B. F. Westcott**, *The Gospel According to St. John* (1880): reads 'was troubled' at John 11:33 as 'troubled himself', a voluntary taking on of emotion; the STEPBible lexicon cites Westcott for this reading.
- **C. H. Dodd**, *The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel* (1953, verify): treats the raising of Lazarus as the culmination of the signs, the one that most fully displays Jesus as the giver of life and that leads directly to his death.
- **Leon Morris**, *The Gospel According to John* (New International Commentary, 1971): defends the historicity of the account and reads the groaning as anger at death and at the unbelief around him (verify).
- **Raymond E. Brown**, *The Gospel According to John* (Anchor Bible, 1966): discusses the relation of the Lazarus story to the Synoptic tradition and argues that in John it replaces the temple cleansing as the immediate cause of Jesus' death (verify).
- **D. A. Carson**, *The Gospel According to John* (Pillar New Testament Commentary, 1991): works out the timing of the four days, reads the groaning as outrage at death and at the unbelief of the mourners, and treats Caiaphas's words as an example of John's irony (verify each).
- **Rudolf Bultmann**, *The Gospel of John* (1941; English 1971, verify): reads John 11:25–26 as the evangelist's correction of a futurist hope, so that the resurrection becomes a present existential reality of faith; many scholars have answered that John holds present and future together (John 5:25, 28–29; 6:40).

### ⚔️ Debate Corner
**The question:** What do 'Our friend Lazarus sleepeth' (John 11:11) and the promise of John 11:25–26 teach about the state of believers between death and the resurrection? "And whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this?" (John 11:26)

**Position 1 · The dead sleep: the whole person ceases or rests unconscious until the resurrection.** This is the teaching of Seventh-day Adventists and Jehovah's Witnesses, and, in various forms, of some earlier Christian groups and of some modern theologians who hold conditional immortality (verify particular writers). Holders argue that Jesus himself calls death sleep (John 11:11; compare Daniel 12:2; 1 Thessalonians 4:13); that Lazarus, raised after four days, reports nothing of any heaven he had left; that Scripture says 'the dead know not any thing' (Ecclesiastes 9:5); and that the Christian hope is always the resurrection of the body, not the survival of a soul. They want to protect the biblical unity of the human person and the centrality of the resurrection.

**Position 2 · The resurrection is present: John 11:25–26 is fulfilled in the life of faith now, and the future bodily resurrection is secondary or symbolic.** This is the reading of Rudolf Bultmann and some existentialist and liberal interpreters (verify particular writers). Holders argue that Jesus corrects Martha's future hope ('at the last day', John 11:24) with a present claim ('I am'), so that eternal life is a new quality of existence given in the encounter with Christ; they regard the futurist sayings of John 5:28–29 and 6:39–40 as later additions to the Gospel (verify). They want to protect the present reality of eternal life and to make the gospel intelligible to modern people.

**Position 3 · Already and not yet: the believer who dies lives consciously with Christ, and will be raised bodily at the last day.** This is the historic Christian and Reformed reading, taught by the Westminster Confession (chapter 32) and Shorter Catechism (Questions 37–38), and in its essentials by Augustine, Calvin, and the great majority of the church (verify each). Holders argue that Jesus affirms Martha's last-day hope and adds to it, rather than replacing it; that 'though he were dead, yet shall he live' and 'shall never die' describe a life with Christ that death does not interrupt; and that 'sleep' describes how death looks and how temporary it is for believers, not the condition of the soul.

**Most consistent with Reformed hermeneutics.** Scripture interpreting Scripture favours Position 3, and it does so by keeping what is true in the other two. (1) *Jesus affirms the last day.* He does not say to Martha *you are wrong*; he says 'I am the resurrection'. The same Gospel records him promising four times to raise his own 'at the last day' (John 6:39, 40, 44, 54) and describing the hour when all in the graves will come forth (John 5:28–29). Position 2 can only avoid this by removing texts from the Gospel. The sign itself is bodily: a man who stank comes out of a tomb (John 11:39, 44). (2) *The promise reaches into death.* 'Though he were dead, yet shall he live' (John 11:25) is not only a promise of future resurrection, or Jesus would be repeating Martha's creed. 'Shall never die' (John 11:26) uses the strongest negation in Greek with 'unto the age' (see the Deep Dive): the life Christ gives cannot be broken by death, although the body dies. That matches what Jesus told the dying thief, "And Jesus said unto him, Verily I say unto thee, To day shalt thou be with me in paradise." (Luke 23:43), what he said of the patriarchs, that God 'is not a God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him' (Luke 20:38), and what Paul hoped for, 'to depart, and to be with Christ; which is far better' (Philippians 1:23; compare 2 Corinthians 5:8). (3) *Sleep is a picture, and Jesus explains it.* In John 11:11–14 Jesus uses 'sleepeth', the disciples misunderstand, and he says 'plainly, Lazarus is dead'. The metaphor fits the body, which lies down and will rise, and it fits the believer's death, which is as temporary and as safe as sleep; it is never used to teach that the soul is unconscious. Ecclesiastes 9:5 describes life 'under the sun' from the perspective of this world, not the soul's state before God. (4) *Lazarus's silence proves nothing.* John does not record anything he said; silence is not testimony. Position 1 is right that the Christian hope is bodily resurrection, not the escape of a soul from a body, and right to resist a sentimental heaven that has no need of the last day. Position 2 is right that eternal life begins now, in the one who says 'I am'. Position 3 holds all of it: life now, life with Christ in death, and life in the body at the last day.

**Pastoral Warning:** This debate is never academic, because it is asked at deathbeds and gravesides. Held carelessly, each position harms real people. Position 1 can leave the grieving with a long silence where Scripture offers the presence of Christ, and can make death seem less an enemy than a pause. Position 2 can empty the funeral of its hope, offering a spiritual quality of life to people who need to know whether they will see their loved one's face again. And Position 3 itself can be misused: turned into a sentimental 'they're in a better place' that skips over the grief Jesus felt, or used to tell mourners they should not weep. Teach John 11:25–26 as Jesus spoke it: to a grieving sister, after she had poured out her sorrow, with tears to follow, and with a question at the end, 'Believest thou this' (John 11:26). Give the grieving both the tears of John 11:35 and the voice of John 11:43. And never promise the hope of John 11:25–26 to anyone apart from faith in Christ, because the promise is to 'he that believeth in me'.

### 🔤 Original Language Deep Dive
Every Greek definition below comes from one source: the brief lexicon published by STEPBible.org (Tyndale House, Cambridge), whose Greek entries are drawn largely from G. Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament*. It is called 'the STEPBible lexicon' below.

**Sick, and sickness.** *Ēsthenei* (G770, John 11:2, V-IAI-3S), 'was sick', and *asthenei* (G770, John 11:3, V-PAI-3S), 'is sick': the verb means to be weak. *Astheneia* (G769, John 11:4, N-NSF), 'sickness', is the matching noun, weakness. The word carries no hint of blame; it names the frailty of the body.

**Two words for love.** The sisters say 'he whom thou lovest' with *phileis* (G5368, John 11:3, V-PAI-2S). John the narrator says 'Now Jesus loved Martha' with *ēgapa* (G25, John 11:5, V-IAI-3S). The mourners say 'Behold how he loved him' with *ephilei* (G5368, John 11:36, V-IAI-3S). The STEPBible lexicon describes *phileō* as the love of emotion and friendship and refers to its entry for *agapaō* for the distinction between them. In John 11 both verbs describe the same love, Jesus' love for this family, one on the lips of the sisters and the crowd, the other in the narrator's voice. That is worth remembering when we come to John 21:15–17, where the change between the two verbs is debated (plan.md D4): John 11 shows that John can use both of Jesus' own love without any drop in warmth. It does not settle the question in John 21, but it cautions against building much on the difference alone. *Phileō* occurs 13 times in the Greek of John; *agapaō* once in this chapter.

**He abode.** *Emeinen* (G3306, John 11:6, V-AAI-3S), 'he abode', from the verb John uses throughout for abiding and remaining (John 1:32; 15:4). Here it describes a deliberate staying.

**Stumbling and light.** *Proskoptei* (G4350, John 11:9, V-PAI-3S), 'stumbleth'. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 11:9–10 under the intransitive sense 'to stumble', and gives the later metaphorical sense *to take offence at, stumble at* for texts like Romans 9:32 and 1 Peter 2:8, where people stumble at Christ the stone. *Phōs* (G5457, John 11:9, N-ASN), 'light', is John's word for Christ as the light of the world (John 1:4–9; 8:12; 9:5; 12:35–36, 46).

**Friend, sleep, and awake.** *Philos* (G5384, John 11:11, A-NSM), 'friend', from the same root as *phileō*; Jesus will call his disciples friends (John 15:13–15). *Kekoimētai* (G2837, John 11:11, V-RMI-3S), 'sleepeth', a perfect middle: *has fallen asleep*, and remains so. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 11:12 under the ordinary sense, to fall asleep, and John 11:11 under the metaphorical sense 'of death', with Acts 7:60, 1 Corinthians 15:6, 18, 20, 51, and 1 Thessalonians 4:13–15. The verb occurs 17 times in the Greek New Testament. *Exupnisō* (G1852, John 11:11, V-AAS-1S), 'that I may awake him'. John 11:13 then distinguishes *koimēseōs* (G2838, John 11:13, N-GSF), 'rest', from *hupnou* (G5258, John 11:13, N-GSM), 'sleep', literally 'the rest of sleep'. The disciples' reply uses *sōthēsetai* (G4982, John 11:12, V-FPI-3S), 'he shall do well'. The STEPBible lexicon's entry for this verb gives the sense *to save from peril, injury or suffering*, lists healing and restoring to health among its uses, and notes that in the New Testament it is used especially of salvation from spiritual disease and death. The disciples mean *he will get well*; John's readers can hear the larger word.

**Plainly.** *Parrēsia* (G3954, John 11:14, N-DSF), 'plainly', openness or boldness of speech. The same word returns in John 11:54, where Jesus no longer walks 'openly', *parrēsia* (G3954, John 11:54, N-DSF), among the Jews. He speaks plainly of Lazarus's death, and then withdraws from plain sight before his own.

**Twin.** *Didumos* (G1324, John 11:16, N-NSM-P), 'Didymus'. The STEPBible lexicon explains it as the surname of the apostle Thomas, from the Greek word for *twin*, and traces 'Thomas' to a Hebrew word with the same meaning.

**Tomb, cave, and four days.** *Mnēmeiō* (G3419, John 11:17, N-DSN), 'grave'. The STEPBible lexicon gives its first sense as 'a memorial, record' and its second as a monument or a sepulchre, a tomb; it is the word used of Jesus' own tomb in John 19–20. *Spēlaion* (G4693, John 11:38, N-NSN), 'cave'. *Tetartaios* (G5066, John 11:39, A-NSM), 'four days': the lexicon defines the adjective as *of or on the fourth day*, and gives the idiom, used here only in the New Testament, *to be four days dead*. *Ozei* (G3605, John 11:39, V-PAI-3S), 'he stinketh'.

**Furlongs.** *Stadiōn* (G4712, John 11:18, N-GPM), 'furlongs'. The Greek *stadion* was a measure of about 185 metres or 600 feet (verify), so fifteen came to a little under three kilometres, or about one and three-quarter miles.

**Comfort.** *Paramuthēsōntai* (G3888, John 11:19, V-ADS-3P), 'to comfort'. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 11:19 and 11:31 under the sense 'to comfort, console'. The neighbours came to do what friends should do; they could comfort, but they could not raise.

**Rise again, resurrection.** *Anastēsetai* (G450, John 11:23, V-FMI-3S), 'shall rise again', from the verb 'to arise'. *Anastasei* (G386, John 11:24, N-DSF) and *anastasis* (G386, John 11:25, N-NSF), 'resurrection'. The STEPBible lexicon gives the first sense *a raising up, awakening, rising* and the second *a rising from the dead*, divided into the resurrection of Christ, the raising of individuals in Old Testament history, and *the general resurrection*. The noun occurs 4 times in the Greek of John, in John 5:29 and here. Martha speaks of the general resurrection; Jesus says he is it.

**I am.** John 11:25 begins with the pronoun *egō* (G1473, John 11:25, P-1NS), which Greek does not need, since the verb *eimi* (G1510, John 11:25, V-PAI-1S) already means 'I am'. The added pronoun gives the emphasis: *I myself am* the resurrection. This is the form of the 'I am' sayings with an image throughout John (John 6:35; 8:12; 10:9, 11; 14:6; 15:1). *Zōē* (G2222, John 11:25, N-NSF), 'life', is John's word for the life of God given in the Son (John 1:4; 3:16; 20:31); the STEPBible lexicon distinguishes it from natural life and lists John's uses under the life of the kingdom of God, *the present life of grace and the life of glory which is to follow* (it names John 6:51 and 6:53 there).

**Though he die, he shall live; he shall never die.** The first promise joins *pisteuōn* (G4100, John 11:25, V-PAP-NSM), 'he that believeth', a present participle, with *apothanē* (G599, John 11:25, V-2AAS-3S), 'though he were dead', an aorist subjunctive after 'even if', and *zēsetai* (G2198, John 11:25, V-FDI-3S), 'yet shall he live', a future. The second promise joins *zōn* (G2198, John 11:26, V-PAP-NSM), 'whosoever liveth', and *pisteuōn* (G4100, John 11:26, V-PAP-NSM), 'and believeth', to the double negative *ou* (G3756, John 11:26) and *mē* (G3361, John 11:26), the aorist subjunctive *apothanē* (G599, John 11:26, V-2AAS-3S), and the phrase 'unto the age', *aiōna* (G165, John 11:26, N-ASM). Grammarians describe the double negative with the aorist subjunctive as the most emphatic way Greek has of denying a future possibility (verify with a standard grammar, such as Blass, Debrunner, and Funk). The KJV's 'shall never die' gathers both the double negative and 'unto the age' into one word. The same construction appears in John 8:51–52 and 10:28. Jesus does not deny that believers will die bodily (the first promise says they will); he denies that the life he gives them will ever end.

**I have believed.** Martha answers with *pepisteuka* (G4100, John 11:27, V-RAI-1S), a perfect tense: *I have believed*, and still do. The KJV's 'I believe' catches the sense. Her faith is settled, not a new decision at that moment. Her titles, 'the Christ, the Son of God', are those of John 20:31.

**The Master calls.** *Didaskalos* (G1320, John 11:28, N-NSM-T), 'Master', teacher, the Greek equivalent of Rabbi (John 1:38; 20:16). *Phōnei* (G5455, John 11:28, V-PAI-3S), 'calleth', the verb of calling by voice, from the same root as the noun 'voice'; compare the shepherd who calls his own sheep by name (John 10:3).

**Weeping: two words.** Mary and the mourners are *klaiousan* (G2799, John 11:33, V-PAP-ASF) and *klaiontas* (G2799, John 11:33, V-PAP-APM), 'weeping'. The STEPBible lexicon defines *klaiō* as *any loud expression of pain or sorrow, especially for the dead, to weep, lament*, and in its note on synonyms under *dakruō* calls it *audible weeping, to cry*. The verb occurs 8 times in the Greek of John, here, in John 16:20, and at the empty tomb, where Mary Magdalene weeps (John 20:11–15). Jesus, by contrast, *edakrusen* (G1145, John 11:35, V-AAI-3S), 'wept'. The lexicon defines *dakruō* simply as 'to weep, shed tears', and lists only this verse: it occurs once in the Greek New Testament. The contrast should not be overdrawn, since the lexicon does not say that Jesus' weeping was silent. But John chose a different word for his tears than for the loud lament around him, and the natural sense is quiet tears rather than wailing.

**Groaning and troubled.** *Enebrimēsato* (G1690, John 11:33, V-ADI-3S), 'he groaned', and *embrimōmenos* (G1690, John 11:38, V-PNP-NSM), 'groaning'. The STEPBible lexicon explains the verb from a root meaning *to snort with anger*, used of horses, and hence *to speak or act with deep feeling*. It gives two senses: first, *to be moved with anger*, under which it lists Mark 14:5 and John 11:33, and, with 'in himself', John 11:38; second, *to admonish sternly*, under which it lists Matthew 9:30 and Mark 1:43. The verb occurs 5 times in the Greek New Testament. The lexicon therefore supports View A's claim that anger is in the word, though it cannot tell us the object of the anger, and it does not exclude grief joined with it. *Pneumati* (G4151, John 11:33, N-DSN), 'in the spirit', is Jesus' own human spirit, as in John 13:21, not the Holy Spirit. *Etaraxen* (G5015, John 11:33, V-AAI-3S), 'was troubled', is active in form with 'himself' as its object. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 11:33 under the metaphorical sense *to trouble, disquiet*, with the note *troubled himself*, citing Westcott. The same verb, passive, describes Jesus' soul in John 12:27 and his spirit in John 13:21, and his disciples' hearts, which he tells not to be troubled (John 14:1, 27).

**Thanks, and always.** *Eucharistō* (G2168, John 11:41, V-PAI-1S), 'I thank thee', and *pantote* (G3842, John 11:42, ADV), 'always'. The thanksgiving comes before the miracle, and it rests on a relationship that has no gaps.

**A loud cry.** *Ekraugasen* (G2905, John 11:43, V-AAI-3S), 'he cried', with *megalē* (G3173, John 11:43, A-DSF), 'loud', literally 'with a great voice'. The STEPBible lexicon defines *kraugazō*, when used of men, as *to cry out, shout*, and lists John 11:43 with the phrase *with a great voice*. The verb occurs 4 times in the Greek of John: here, and three times of the crowd crying out for Barabbas and for crucifixion (John 18:40; 19:6, 15). The same kind of cry that calls Lazarus out of the tomb will call for Jesus to be put into one.

**Graveclothes and napkin.** *Keiriais* (G2750, John 11:44, N-DPF), 'graveclothes'. The STEPBible lexicon says the word first meant a bed-cord, and in the plural 'swathings', and lists only this verse. *Soudariō* (G4676, John 11:44, N-DSN), 'napkin', a word the lexicon marks as borrowed from Latin, meaning a handkerchief or kerchief, used *as a head covering for the dead* in John 11:44 and John 20:7. *Lusate* (G3089, John 11:44, V-AAM-2P), 'loose', and *aphete* (G863, John 11:44, V-2AAM-2P), 'let him go': the second verb is the ordinary verb for letting go, releasing, and also forgiving; the council uses it a few lines later, *aphōmen* (G863, John 11:48, V-2AAS-1P), 'if we let him thus alone'. Jesus says of Lazarus, *let him go*; the council says of Jesus, *we cannot let him alone*.

**Council, signs, place, and nation.** *Sunedrion* (G4892, John 11:47, N-ASN), 'a council'. The STEPBible lexicon's second sense is *the supreme ecclesiastical court of the Jews, the Sanhedrin*; here the word may describe a formal session or an informal meeting of its leaders (verify). *Sēmeia* (G4592, John 11:47, N-APN), 'miracles', John's word 'signs'; the noun occurs 17 times in the Greek of John. *Topon* (G5117, John 11:48, N-ASM), 'place'. *Ethnos* (G1484, John 11:48, N-ASN), 'nation'. The STEPBible lexicon notes that the singular is used of the Jewish people in this chapter (John 11:48, 50–53) and elsewhere, while the plural usually means the Gentiles; the word occurs 5 times in the Greek of John.

**Expedient, for, and perish.** *Sumpherei* (G4851, John 11:50, V-PAI-3S), 'it is expedient'. The STEPBible lexicon gives *it is expedient, profitable or an advantage*, and lists John 11:50 with John 16:7, where Jesus says 'It is expedient for you that I go away', and John 18:14, where John recalls Caiaphas's counsel. *Huper* (G5228, John 11:50, PREP), 'for', on behalf of, and in contexts of death, in the place of (compare John 10:11; 2 Corinthians 5:14–15, 21). *Laou* (G2992, John 11:50, N-GSM), 'the people', the word the Greek Old Testament uses for Israel as God's people. *Apolētai* (G622, John 11:50, V-2AMS-3S), 'perish', the verb of John 3:16 and 10:28.

**Prophesied.** *Eprophēteusen* (G4395, John 11:51, V-AAI-3S), 'he prophesied'. The STEPBible lexicon defines the verb first as *telling forth the Divine counsels*, with foretelling future events as *an idea merely incidental, not essential*, and lists John 11:51 with the construction 'that'. Caiaphas both spoke God's counsel and, unknowingly, foretold what would happen.

**Children, scattered, gathered, one.** *Tekna* (G5043, John 11:52, N-APN), 'children', the word of John 1:12, where those who receive Christ are given power 'to become the sons of God' (the same noun). *Dieskorpismena* (G1287, John 11:52, V-RPP-APN), 'that were scattered abroad', a perfect passive participle: they are in a state of scatteredness. The STEPBible lexicon lists the verb of scattered sheep (Matthew 26:31) and of persons, and says that in John 11:52 it is used as the 'opposite to' *sunagō*. *Sunagagē* (G4863, John 11:52, V-2AAS-3S), 'he should gather together', the same verb used of the council gathering to plot, *sunēgagon* (G4863, John 11:47, V-2AAI-3P). The council gathers to plan his death; his death gathers the children of God. *Hen* (G1520, John 11:52, A-ASN), 'one', the neuter numeral of John 10:30 and 17:21–23: the unity the Father and the Son share is the pattern of the unity into which the scattered children are gathered.

**Plotted, and purify.** *Sunebouleusanto* (G4823, John 11:53, V-AMI-3P), 'they took counsel together', the Textus Receptus reading. *Hagnisōsin* (G48, John 11:55, V-AAS-3P), 'to purify themselves'. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 11:55 under the ceremonial sense of purifying and notes that in the Greek Old Testament the verb is always used ceremonially.

**Textual notes.** These should be explained calmly, without scorn for either side; none of them changes any doctrine. (1) *John 11:25.* A few early witnesses, including the papyrus P45 and some Old Latin manuscripts, lack 'and the life' (verify the witnesses); almost all manuscripts, and all major editions, include it. (2) *John 11:31.* The Textus Receptus reads 'saying, She goeth unto the grave'; many early manuscripts read a participle meaning *supposing that she goeth*, which most modern versions follow (verify). The sense is the same. (3) *John 11:41.* The Textus Receptus reads 'from the place where the dead was laid'; many early manuscripts have only *they took away the stone* (verify). The fuller reading makes explicit what the shorter assumes. (4) *John 11:50.* The Textus Receptus reads 'expedient for us'; many early manuscripts read *expedient for you*, which sharpens Caiaphas's rebuke of the council (verify). Either way, he speaks of their advantage, and John of God's purpose.

### 🚷 Preaching Mistakes to Avoid
- **Information without life:** a sermon on Jewish burial customs, the chronology of the four days, the geography of Bethany, and the Greek of 'groaned' that never brings the hearers to the one who says 'I am the resurrection, and the life', or asks them Jesus' question, 'Believest thou this' (John 11:25–26).
- **Heat without light:** a sermon that thunders against the council's cynicism, or against grieving people's doubts, without first weeping with the grieving as Jesus did (John 11:35), and without asking where the preacher, too, has guarded his own 'place' (John 11:48).
- **The delay as a formula:** preaching that God always comes at the last minute, or that every sickness will end in healing if we believe, when the chapter's promise is the resurrection, and many faithful believers die before Christ's return (Hebrews 11:13, 39).
- **Tears without the voice, or the voice without tears:** preaching only 'Jesus wept', so that Jesus becomes a sympathetic friend who cannot help, or only 'Lazarus, come forth', so that he becomes a wonder-worker who does not feel. The chapter holds both (John 11:35, 43).
- **Allegory run wild:** assigning a hidden meaning to every detail (the two days, the four days, the stone, the napkin) beyond what John himself explains. The sign has a meaning John gives: Jesus is the resurrection and the life, and the voice that raised Lazarus is the voice that will raise the dead (John 5:25, 28–29; 11:25).
- **Caiaphas as a cartoon, and 'the Jews' as villains:** preaching Caiaphas as a monster rather than a religious leader protecting his institution, which makes it too easy for hearers to miss their own likeness to him, and preaching 'the Jews' of this chapter as a people rather than as Jewish friends who wept, many who believed, and a few leaders who plotted (John 11:19, 45–46).

## 🟣 Tier 4 — Christocentric, Humility, Life & Light
### 👑 Every Answer in Jesus
John 11 demolishes every ground of self-glory. Our love for our friends is no ground: the sisters loved their brother and could only send a message (John 11:3). Our faith is no ground: Martha's great confession did not stop her from objecting at the stone (John 11:27, 39), and the disciples had to be taught again what they thought they knew (John 11:12–15). Our courage is no ground: Thomas was ready to die with Jesus and could see nothing past the grave (John 11:16). Our religious position is no ground: the council had the temple, the law, and the high priesthood, and used them to plot against the Lord of life (John 11:47–53). And our own life is no ground, because every one of us is heading for the place where Lazarus lay, with nothing to offer but decay (John 11:39).

And every good thing the chapter holds is found in Christ. God's glory is seen in him (John 11:4, 40). Love that waits for our good is his (John 11:5–6). Light for the road is his, for he walks in the day and is the light of the world (John 11:9; 8:12). Resurrection and life are his, not as gifts he hands us from a distance but as himself (John 11:25). Sympathy that weeps is his, for he is truly man (John 11:35). Power over death is his, for he is truly God (John 11:43). Access to the Father is his, for the Father always hears him (John 11:42). And atonement is his, for he is the one man who died for the people, to gather the scattered children of God into one (John 11:50–52). This is what Paul means when he says of Christ, "In whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." (Colossians 2:3) The council thought wisdom meant calculating how to keep their place; the true wisdom of God was the one they plotted to kill, laying down his life to give life. The chapter humbles us by showing that we are Lazarus in the tomb, the disciples who misunderstand, the sisters who cannot see past the grave, and sometimes the council that protects its place. And it makes Christ glorious by showing the Lord of life walking toward his own death to empty another man's tomb, weeping on the way.

## 📚 Doctrine
### Theology Proper · God's Glory Through Sickness, Delay, and Death
**Proposition:** God rules over sickness, delay, and death, and orders them for the display of his own glory and the glorifying of his Son. His sovereign purpose and his love are not rivals: the one who loves his people may permit their suffering and delay his help, precisely because he loves them and intends a greater good than their immediate relief.

**Textual Anchor:** "When Jesus heard that, he said, This sickness is not unto death, but for the glory of God, that the Son of God might be glorified thereby." (John 11:4) "Now Jesus loved Martha, and her sister, and Lazarus." (John 11:5) 'he abode two days still in the same place where he was' (John 11:6); and 'if thou wouldest believe, thou shouldest see the glory of God' (John 11:40), with *doxan* (G1391, John 11:40, N-ASF).

**Cross-References:** "I kill, and I make alive; I wound, and I heal: neither is there any that can deliver out of my hand." (Deuteronomy 32:39); "And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose." (Romans 8:28); "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD." (Isaiah 55:8)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *God is not in control of suffering; sickness and death happen outside his rule, and he can only respond to them.* Open theists and many popular writers hold this, wanting to protect God from blame for evil. But Jesus declares the purpose of the sickness before it runs its course (John 11:4), knows of the death without being told (John 11:14), and times his coming (John 11:6, 15); Scripture says the LORD kills and makes alive (Deuteronomy 32:39). (2) *If God allows suffering, he cannot really love the sufferer.* Many hurting people and many sceptics hold this, wanting a love they can see. John joins the love and the delay with the word 'therefore' (John 11:5–6), and shows the Son weeping at the grave he has chosen not to prevent (John 11:35). (3) *God sends every sickness in order to heal it, if only we have faith.* Some healing movements hold this, wanting to honour God's power. But the purpose John names is God's glory, which in this chapter includes the cross (John 11:4, 53), and faithful believers still sicken and die (Philippians 2:27; 2 Timothy 4:20).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Trust in God's timing when his help seems late. Freedom to grieve without concluding that God has stopped loving us. And worship that seeks God's glory in suffering, not only relief from it.

### Christology · The Resurrection and the Life
**Proposition:** Jesus Christ is himself the resurrection and the life. The power that will raise the dead at the last day and the eternal life of the age to come are present in his person, so that whoever believes in him, though he dies, will live, and whoever lives and believes in him will never die. He is the Christ, the Son of God, who came into the world to give this life.

**Textual Anchor:** "Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:" (John 11:25), with *egō* (G1473, John 11:25, P-1NS) *eimi* (G1510, John 11:25, V-PAI-1S) and *anastasis* (G386, John 11:25, N-NSF); "And whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this?" (John 11:26); and Martha's confession, 'thou art the Christ, the Son of God, which should come into the world' (John 11:27).

**Cross-References:** "For as the Father hath life in himself; so hath he given to the Son to have life in himself;" (John 5:26); "I am he that liveth, and was dead; and, behold, I am alive for evermore, Amen; and have the keys of hell and of death." (Revelation 1:18); "But is now made manifest by the appearing of our Saviour Jesus Christ, who hath abolished death, and hath brought life and immortality to light through the gospel:" (2 Timothy 1:10)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus was a wise teacher or prophet who pointed people to the resurrection but did not claim to be its source.* Many modern people and some Muslim and liberal writers hold this, wanting to honour Jesus without his claims. But Jesus does not say *I teach* or *I bring* the resurrection; he says 'I am the resurrection' (John 11:25), and he proves it by raising a man four days dead. (2) *The resurrection is only a future event, and Christ's life is only for the world to come.* Some believers live this way, wanting to keep the future hope clear. But Jesus moves Martha from 'at the last day' to 'I am', so that eternal life is possessed now (John 11:24–26; 5:24). (3) *Eternal life is only a present quality of existence, with no future bodily resurrection.* Existentialist readers, following Bultmann, hold this, wanting to make the gospel present and intelligible. But Jesus affirms the last day elsewhere (John 6:39–40) and raises a body here (John 11:44). See the Debate Corner.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Faith that rests on a person, not only on a doctrine. Hope at every graveside, because the resurrection is not a distant idea but a living Lord. And urgency in evangelism, because the promise is to 'he that believeth in me'.

### Christology · True God and True Man at the Grave
**Proposition:** Jesus Christ is one person in two natures, truly God and truly man. At Lazarus's grave he loved, was deeply moved and troubled, asked, and wept, as a true man with a true human spirit; and he knew what he had not been told, prayed as the Son whom the Father always hears, and raised the dead by his word, as the true God. Both his tears and his command are the acts of one and the same Lord.

**Textual Anchor:** 'he groaned in the spirit, and was troubled' (John 11:33), with *enebrimēsato* (G1690, John 11:33, V-ADI-3S) and *etaraxen* (G5015, John 11:33, V-AAI-3S); "Jesus wept." (John 11:35) "And I knew that thou hearest me always: but because of the people which stand by I said it, that they may believe that thou hast sent me." (John 11:42) "And when he thus had spoken, he cried with a loud voice, Lazarus, come forth." (John 11:43)

**Cross-References:** "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth." (John 1:14); "For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities; but was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin." (Hebrews 4:15)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus only seemed to have human emotions; as God he could not really grieve.* Docetists in the early church, and some believers today without knowing it, hold this, wanting to protect Christ's deity from weakness. But John records the groaning, the trouble, and the tears with care (John 11:33–35), and the Son of God truly became flesh (John 1:14). (2) *Jesus was a man with an unusually close relationship to God, not God himself.* Adoptionists in the early church and many liberal writers today hold this, wanting a Jesus who is one of us. But he calls the dead out of the grave by his own word, as only the LORD can (John 11:43; 1 Samuel 2:6), and claims to be the resurrection itself (John 11:25). (3) *Jesus' human nature was absorbed into his divine nature, so that his emotions were really divine emotions.* Eutychians in the fifth century held a form of this, wanting to stress his unity. But Scripture shows a real human spirit that is troubled (John 11:33; 12:27) and a real human prayer (John 11:41–42); the natures are united in one person, not mixed.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Comfort in grief, because the Lord who rules death has felt its sorrow. Confidence in his power, because the one who weeps with us is the one who will call our dead by name. And worship of the one Christ, whose humanity we may trust and whose deity we must adore.

### Anthropology & Sin · Death the Enemy, and the Heart That Plots Against Life
**Proposition:** Death is not natural or friendly; it is an enemy that entered the world through sin and reduces even those Jesus loves to decay. Human beings cannot deliver themselves or others from it. And sin so darkens the human heart that people can see the plainest evidence of God's power and respond by protecting their own interests and plotting against the Lord of life.

**Textual Anchor:** 'Lord, by this time he stinketh: for he hath been dead four days' (John 11:39), with *ozei* (G3605, John 11:39, V-PAI-3S); 'if a man walk in the night, he stumbleth, because there is no light in him' (John 11:10); "Then gathered the chief priests and the Pharisees a council, and said, What do we? for this man doeth many miracles." (John 11:47) "If we let him thus alone, all men will believe on him: and the Romans shall come and take away both our place and nation." (John 11:48) "Then from that day forth they took counsel together for to put him to death." (John 11:53)

**Cross-References:** "Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned:" (Romans 5:12); "The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death." (1 Corinthians 15:26); "And he said unto him, If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead." (Luke 16:31)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Death is a natural part of the circle of life, to be accepted rather than mourned.* Many modern people, and some forms of Eastern and naturalistic thought, hold this, wanting to make peace with mortality. But Jesus groaned and was troubled at the grave (John 11:33, 38), and Scripture calls death an enemy that came by sin (Romans 5:12; 1 Corinthians 15:26). (2) *People reject Christ only because they lack evidence; show them a miracle and they will believe.* Many seekers and some apologists assume this, wanting faith to be reasonable. The council admitted 'this man doeth many miracles' (John 11:47) and plotted to kill him; some who saw Lazarus raised went to report him (John 11:46). The problem is the heart, not the evidence (Luke 16:31; John 3:19). (3) *Religious leaders and institutions are safe from this darkness.* Some assume that religious office protects against it, wanting to trust their leaders. The plot against Jesus was formed by the chief priests and the Pharisees in council, led by the high priest (John 11:47–50).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Honest grief that calls death what it is. Humility about our own hearts, which can calculate like the council. And prayer for those who have seen and still refuse, since only the voice of Christ can raise the dead.

### Soteriology & Grace · One Man for the People
**Proposition:** Jesus Christ died for the people, on their behalf and in their place, so that they would not perish. His death is the means by which God gathers into one the children of God who were scattered abroad, from Israel and from the nations. God accomplished this saving death through the sinful counsel of men, who remain responsible for their sin, while he overruled it for salvation.

**Textual Anchor:** "Nor consider that it is expedient for us, that one man should die for the people, and that the whole nation perish not." (John 11:50), with *huper* (G5228, John 11:50, PREP) and *apolētai* (G622, John 11:50, V-2AMS-3S); "And this spake he not of himself: but being high priest that year, he prophesied that Jesus should die for that nation;" (John 11:51) "And not for that nation only, but that also he should gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad." (John 11:52), with *sunagagē* (G4863, John 11:52, V-2AAS-3S).

**Cross-References:** "But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed." (Isaiah 53:5); "For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him." (2 Corinthians 5:21); "And other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring, and they shall hear my voice; and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd." (John 10:16)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus' death was only a political execution, the fate of a man who threatened the authorities.* Many historians hold this, wanting a Jesus who fits ordinary history. John agrees that it was a political calculation (John 11:48–50), and then says God meant it as a death 'for that nation' and for the scattered children of God (John 11:51–52). (2) *Christ's death was an example of self-sacrifice, not a death in the place of others.* Holders of the moral-influence view, from Peter Abelard (verify) to many modern writers, want to stress the transforming power of love. But the logic of the verse is substitution: one dies so that the many do not perish (John 11:50; Isaiah 53:5–6). (3) *Christ died to make salvation possible for everyone, without securing it for anyone in particular.* Many Arminian and Wesleyan believers hold this, wanting to protect the universal offer. Reformed theology answers that John speaks of a death that actually gathers 'the children of God' (John 11:52; 10:15–16), while gladly affirming that the offer is to 'whosoever' (John 11:26; 3:16).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Wonder that the innocent one died in our place. Assurance that his death was not a tragic accident but God's plan, and that it will accomplish what God intended. And a heart for the nations, because the scattered children of God are still being gathered.

### Soteriology & Grace · The Voice That Raises the Dead
**Proposition:** The spiritually dead can no more raise themselves than Lazarus could leave his tomb. Life comes only when Christ calls effectually by his word, and his call gives the life it commands, so that the dead hear and come forth. The Father hears the Son always, and the Son gives life to whom he will.

**Textual Anchor:** "And when he thus had spoken, he cried with a loud voice, Lazarus, come forth." (John 11:43), with *ekraugasen* (G2905, John 11:43, V-AAI-3S); "And he that was dead came forth, bound hand and foot with graveclothes: and his face was bound about with a napkin. Jesus saith unto them, Loose him, and let him go." (John 11:44)

**Cross-References:** "Verily, verily, I say unto you, The hour is coming, and now is, when the dead shall hear the voice of the Son of God: and they that hear shall live." (John 5:25); "Even when we were dead in sins, hath quickened us together with Christ, (by grace ye are saved;)" (Ephesians 2:5); "For as the Father raiseth up the dead, and quickeneth them; even so the Son quickeneth whom he will." (John 5:21)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Sinners are only sick or weak, and need encouragement and good examples to choose God.* Pelagians and semi-Pelagians have held this, wanting to affirm human freedom and responsibility. Scripture pictures sinners as dead (Ephesians 2:1, 5), and the Lazarus sign shows what that means: a dead man contributes nothing to his raising. (2) *God's call can always be finally resisted, so that its success depends on the sinner.* Arminian theology holds that the call to salvation is sincere and gracious but resistible, wanting to protect the reality of human choice. The Reformed answer is that when Christ calls effectually, as he called Lazarus, the dead hear and live (John 5:25; 6:37, 44), and that the one raised comes 'most freely', not by force. (3) *The Lazarus story is only a symbol of spiritual renewal, not a bodily miracle.* Some modern readers hold this. The sign is bodily first, and its spiritual meaning rests on its reality (John 11:39, 44; 12:9–11).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Humility, because no one raised himself. Confidence in evangelism, because the voice that raised Lazarus still speaks through the gospel. And patient love for those who are still bound, because the living sometimes need others to help loose their grave clothes.

### Ecclesiology · Gathered Together in One
**Proposition:** The death of Christ creates one people of God, gathered from Israel and from the nations scattered abroad, and united in him as the Father and the Son are one. Christ alone gives life, but he calls his people to serve that life: to carry his call, to remove what stands between the dead and his voice, and to help free those he has raised from what still binds them.

**Textual Anchor:** 'that also he should gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad' (John 11:52), with *hen* (G1520, John 11:52, A-ASN) and *dieskorpismena* (G1287, John 11:52, V-RPP-APN); 'The Master is come, and calleth for thee' (John 11:28); 'Take ye away the stone' (John 11:39); and 'Loose him, and let him go' (John 11:44).

**Cross-References:** "That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us: that the world may believe that thou hast sent me." (John 17:21); "For he is our peace, who hath made both one, and hath broken down the middle wall of partition between us;" (Ephesians 2:14); "Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ." (Galatians 6:2)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The church is a voluntary association of like-minded individuals.* Many modern evangelicals assume this, wanting to stress personal faith. John says the church is gathered by Christ's death (John 11:52), not assembled by human preference. (2) *God has two separate peoples, Israel and the church, with separate destinies.* Some forms of dispensational theology hold this, wanting to honour God's promises to Israel. John says Jesus died for 'that nation' and for the scattered children of God, to gather them 'in one' (John 11:51–52; compare John 10:16; Ephesians 2:14); the Reformed reading sees one people of God in Christ, while many who hold this view today affirm that too in their own terms. (3) *Christians can give spiritual life to others by their own efforts, programs, or persuasiveness.* Churches sometimes act as if this were so, wanting to be fruitful. In John 11 the bystanders move the stone and loose the grave clothes, but only Christ's voice raises (John 11:39, 43–44).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Love across every ethnic and social line, because the gathered children were scattered everywhere. Practical service to the grieving and the newly converted, taking away stones and loosing grave clothes. And humility in ministry, because we never give life, only serve it.

### Eschatology · Death, Sleep, and the Resurrection at the Last Day
**Proposition:** For believers death is a sleep: real, but temporary and safe, because Christ will wake them. The believer who dies lives with Christ, and at the last day all the dead will be raised, believers to life. The raising of Lazarus is a sign and a foretaste of that day, and a pledge that the voice that called him will call all who are in the graves.

**Textual Anchor:** "These things said he: and after that he saith unto them, Our friend Lazarus sleepeth; but I go, that I may awake him out of sleep." (John 11:11), with *kekoimētai* (G2837, John 11:11, V-RMI-3S); "Martha saith unto him, I know that he shall rise again in the resurrection at the last day." (John 11:24) and the double promise of John 11:25–26.

**Cross-References:** "For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so them also which sleep in Jesus will God bring with him." (1 Thessalonians 4:14); "Marvel not at this: for the hour is coming, in the which all that are in the graves shall hear his voice," (John 5:28); "We are confident, I say, and willing rather to be absent from the body, and to be present with the Lord." (2 Corinthians 5:8)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *At death the whole person ceases to exist or sleeps unconscious until the resurrection.* Seventh-day Adventists and Jehovah's Witnesses hold this, wanting to protect the unity of the person and the hope of resurrection. But Jesus explains his metaphor ('Lazarus is dead', John 11:14) and promises that the believer 'shall never die' (John 11:26); the believer who departs is 'present with the Lord' (2 Corinthians 5:8). (2) *There is no future bodily resurrection; eternal life is only spiritual.* Some liberal and existentialist readers hold this. Jesus affirms the last day and raises a body (John 11:24, 44; 5:28–29). (3) *All will be saved in the end, whatever they believe.* Universalists hold this, wanting to magnify God's love. But the promise of John 11:25–26 is to 'he that believeth in me', and John 5:29 speaks of two resurrections.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Peace at the deathbed of believers. A way to speak of Christian dead that is honest and hopeful: they sleep in Jesus. And a longing for the last day, when his voice will call our own names.

### Christian Walk · Faith, Prayer, and Grief in the Delay
**Proposition:** Believers may bring their grief and disappointment honestly to Christ, as Martha and Mary did, and he receives it without rebuke. They are called to trust his word when the facts seem to contradict it, to grieve with hope rather than without it, and to pray to the Father in the name of the Son whom the Father always hears.

**Textual Anchor:** "Then said Martha unto Jesus, Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died." (John 11:21) "But I know, that even now, whatsoever thou wilt ask of God, God will give it thee." (John 11:22) "Jesus saith unto her, Said I not unto thee, that, if thou wouldest believe, thou shouldest see the glory of God?" (John 11:40) and 'Father, I thank thee that thou hast heard me' (John 11:41), with *eucharistō* (G2168, John 11:41, V-PAI-1S).

**Cross-References:** "But I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are asleep, that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope." (1 Thessalonians 4:13); "Rejoice with them that do rejoice, and weep with them that weep." (Romans 12:15); "For his anger endureth but a moment; in his favour is life: weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning." (Psalm 30:5)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Strong faith does not grieve; tears show a lack of trust.* Some believers hold this, wanting to honour the hope of heaven. Jesus wept, and Paul forbids hopeless grief, not grief (John 11:35; 1 Thessalonians 4:13). (2) *Honest complaint to God is sinful.* Some believers hold this, wanting to be reverent. Both sisters said 'if thou hadst been here', and Jesus answered with a promise and with tears, not with rebuke (John 11:21, 25, 32–35; compare the Psalms of lament). (3) *Prayer is a technique for getting what we want if we have enough faith.* Some forms of the Word of Faith movement hold this, wanting to honour God's promises. Jesus' prayer at the tomb is thanksgiving grounded in his relationship with the Father and aimed at the Father's glory and others' faith (John 11:41–42), not a formula.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Honest prayers of lament that end in trust. A habit of returning to Christ's promises when circumstances contradict them. And a community that weeps with those who weep, instead of hurrying them.

### Assumed Here
- **Pneumatology:** John 11 does not teach about the Holy Spirit directly; 'in the spirit' (John 11:33) refers to Jesus' own human spirit. The chapter assumes the Spirit's work in giving life, which Scripture teaches plainly in John 3:5–8 (born of the Spirit), John 6:63 (the Spirit that quickeneth), and Romans 8:11 (the Spirit who will quicken our mortal bodies).

## ⚖️ The Council's Hard Questions
### ❓ Question 1 · Why Did He Wait
**Moderator:** John says, 'Now Jesus loved Martha, and her sister, and Lazarus', and then, 'When he had heard therefore that he was sick, he abode two days still' (John 11:5–6). Did Jesus let Lazarus die? And how can a delay that causes so much grief be called love?

**Historian:** Start with the timing, because it matters. When Jesus arrives, Lazarus has been in the tomb four days (John 11:17). Jesus was beyond Jordan (John 10:40), probably a day's journey or more from Bethany (verify the location). If the messenger took a day to reach him, Jesus waited two days, and the journey took a day, then Lazarus may well have died about the time the messenger arrived. Many commentators, Carson among them, reckon it this way (verify). If so, the two days did not cause the death. But we should be honest: John does not give us the travel times, and he does not use the timing to excuse Jesus. He says the delay was deliberate.

**Literary Scholar:** And he joins it to love with a 'therefore'. That is the scandal of the verse, and John does not soften it. But look at what the delay produces in the narrative. It makes the death certain beyond doubt: four days, the stink, the mourners (John 11:17, 19, 39). It gathers the witnesses from Jerusalem (John 11:19, 45). It moves Martha from 'at the last day' to 'I am' (John 11:24–27). And Jesus himself gives the purpose twice: 'for the glory of God' (John 11:4), and 'for your sakes', 'to the intent ye may believe' (John 11:15). The delay is not absence; it is authorship.

**Theologian:** The doctrine behind it is providence joined to love. God does not merely permit and then react; he orders sickness and death to his ends, and his ends are good, above all the glory of his Son and the good of those who love him (Romans 8:28). But I want to guard against two mistakes. One is to say that every delay will end the way this one did, with the dead raised in this life. That is not promised; Hebrews 11 tells of believers who 'died in faith, not having received the promises' (Hebrews 11:13). The other mistake is to think that because God has a purpose, the grief is unreal or unimportant. Jesus' own tears forbid that (John 11:35).

**Pastor:** In the group, someone will be living inside verse 6. They prayed and he did not come, and someone died. I would not rush to verse 4 with them. I would start with verse 5: 'Jesus loved'. And then I would let them see that Jesus did not rebuke Martha or Mary for their 'if thou hadst been here', and that he wept. The purpose of the delay is true, but it is not a sentence to throw at a grieving person; it is a promise to hold them in.

**Resolution:** Jesus deliberately delayed, and John presents the delay as an expression of his love, not a failure of it: it served the glory of God, the faith of the disciples, and the good of the family, in ways an earlier arrival would not have. The chapter does not promise that every delay ends in rescue in this life, and it does not make grief unreal; it teaches that the one who waits is the one who loves and who rules the outcome. "Now Jesus loved Martha, and her sister, and Lazarus." (John 11:5)

### ❓ Question 2 · Why Did He Groan and Weep
**Moderator:** Jesus 'groaned in the spirit, and was troubled' (John 11:33), 'wept' (John 11:35), and came to the grave 'again groaning in himself' (John 11:38). He knew he would raise Lazarus in minutes. Why the tears? And was the groaning anger or grief?

**Philologist:** The words pull in two directions, and we should let them. The verb for 'groaned' is a hard word. The STEPBible lexicon traces it to a root used of horses snorting, lists John 11:33 and 11:38 under *to be moved with anger*, and elsewhere the same verb is a stern warning (Mark 1:43). So anger is in the word. But 'was troubled' is literally *troubled himself*, the word John uses for Jesus' soul facing the cross (John 12:27), and 'wept' is a different verb from the loud wailing of the mourners, a word for shedding tears. John gives us indignation, inner agitation, and tears together.

**Church Historian:** The church has read it both ways, and often both at once. Chrysostom emphasised the reality of Christ's human emotion and his restraint of it (verify). Calvin said Christ came to the grave like a champion preparing for combat, and that he took on true human affections without sin (verify). B. B. Warfield argued strongly that the word means anger, and pictured Jesus advancing on the tomb in indignation against death and the one who has the power of death (verify). Leo the Great used this very scene to show the two natures: to weep for a friend belongs to the humanity, to raise him to the deity, and both belong to one person (verify).

**Theologian:** I think the anger and the grief have the same object: death, and the sin and the devil that stand behind it. Jesus is not angry at Mary for weeping. He is angry at what has made her weep. And he grieves because he loves. That is why he can weep over a grave he is about to empty: knowing the outcome does not make the present evil less evil, or the present sorrow less sorrowful. We see the same thing in Hebrews: 'he also himself likewise took part of the same; that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death' (Hebrews 2:14). The Son who became flesh felt what flesh feels before death, and hated what death is.

**Literary Scholar:** Some interpreters add that the anger is at the unbelief of the mourners, whose loud wailing showed they had no hope (verify). That is possible, since the first groaning comes when he sees 'the Jews also weeping' (John 11:33). But the onlookers read his tears as love, 'Behold how he loved him' (John 11:36), and John does not correct them. I would keep the main object where the scene puts it: at the tomb.

**Resolution:** The text supports both indignation and grief, and they need not be set against each other: Jesus was roused against death and its causes, and he was moved to tears by the sorrow of those he loved, as a true man with a true human spirit, even while as the Son of God he knew what he would do. His tears show that knowing the end does not make present sorrow unreal; his anger shows that death is an enemy, not a friend. "Jesus wept." (John 11:35)

### ❓ Question 3 · Caiaphas's Prophecy: How Can an Enemy Prophesy, and for Whom Did Jesus Die
**Moderator:** Caiaphas said it was 'expedient for us, that one man should die for the people' (John 11:50), and John says 'he prophesied' (John 11:51). How can a man plotting murder prophesy? And whom does John mean by 'the children of God that were scattered abroad' (John 11:52)?

**Historian:** Caiaphas was a skilled political survivor. Josephus says he held the high priesthood for many years, longer than most in that century, which suggests he knew how to work with Rome (verify). His argument is pure politics: a messianic movement could lead to a revolt, and Rome would crush the temple and the nation. Sacrifice one man and save the institution. There is also a grim irony in history: they did remove Jesus, and in AD 70 Rome destroyed the temple anyway (verify the details in a standard history).

**Theologian:** John's explanation is precise: 'this spake he not of himself: but being high priest that year, he prophesied' (John 11:51). God spoke through the office, not through the man's piety. Scripture has precedents: Balaam blessed Israel when he wanted to curse it (Numbers 23–24). And it fits the doctrine that God governs even sinful acts to his holy ends without being the author of sin. Joseph said of his brothers, 'ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good' (Genesis 50:20), and the church prayed that Herod, Pilate, and the people had done 'whatsoever thy hand and thy counsel determined before to be done' (Acts 4:28). Caiaphas is guilty; God is sovereign; the cross is both.

**Philologist:** The word John uses is the ordinary word for prophesying, and the STEPBible lexicon notes that its primary sense is telling forth God's counsels, with foretelling only incidental. Caiaphas told forth God's counsel without knowing it. And notice the preposition: 'die for the people', *huper*, the preposition of benefit and, in contexts of death, of substitution (see the Deep Dive). Caiaphas meant substitution for political safety. God meant substitution for salvation.

**Teacher:** On 'the children of God', I would ask the group to read John 10:16 alongside it. Jesus has 'other sheep' not of this fold, whom he 'must bring'. John 11:52 says his death will 'gather together in one' the scattered children of God. In John's own setting, that includes Jews scattered abroad and, especially, Gentiles, like the Greeks who come to see Jesus in the next chapter (John 12:20–21).

**Theologian:** And the Reformed reading adds that they are called 'children of God' before they are gathered, because the Father has given them to the Son (John 6:37; 10:29); the death gathers a people God already knows. Arminian readers take the phrase to mean those God foresaw would believe. Both agree that the gathering is worldwide and happens through the cross, and that everyone who believes is welcome (John 11:26; 3:16).

**Resolution:** Caiaphas spoke a cynical political calculation, and God made his words a prophecy of the gospel: Jesus would die for the nation, in its place, and to gather God's scattered children from every nation into one. God overruled human sin for salvation without excusing the sinner. "And not for that nation only, but that also he should gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad." (John 11:52)

### 🔦 The Life & Light Audit
**Pastor:** If a hearer obeyed this lesson exactly, then tonight they would read John 11:25–26 and answer Jesus' question honestly, naming a fear of death. Within a day they would contact one grieving person and sit with them without explaining, move one practical 'stone' for someone sick or bereaved, call a person whose loss everyone else has forgotten, and ask one gentle question of someone who thinks death is the end. What would come alive in them is hope with tears: a grief that is no longer afraid, because the one who wept is the resurrection. And in others, comfort, as someone who has been left alone with 'if thou hadst been here' finds a friend at their side and hears Christ's promise. The dark place lit would be the hearer's own avoidance of death and grief first, and their own habit of guarding their 'place' like the council.

**Theologian:** The practices keep source discipline. No practice asks the believer to raise anyone or to explain God's purposes; the believer weeps with those who weep, moves stones, carries the Master's call, and reads Christ's words. Life is always located in Christ (John 11:25, 43), and the Channel is described with the bystanders' humble tasks, not the Lord's work. The Abundance Guard is kept by starting from 'he whom thou lovest is sick' and never promising healing. The Light practices turn first on the hearer's own fears and self-protection (Q25, Q26, the Inward practices), and the Love Guard is built into the Outward ones: tears and presence before words.

**Teacher:** A Level 1 participant can do the Inward Life practice tonight: read two verses and say one sentence to Jesus. The Outward practices name the kind of person to reach (someone who lost a loved one in the past year; someone sick or grieving who needs a practical help; a housebound or hospitalised member; someone whose loss is old and forgotten; someone who speaks of death as the end; someone avoided because their grief is uncomfortable) and leave a blank for the name. The Doing objective is one contact with one grieving person in one day.

**Fix:** The Audit found three risks, and the unit addresses them. First, a lesson on the resurrection can be used to hurry grieving people past their grief ('he's in a better place'); so the Modern Example, the Cultural Landmines, the Debate Corner's Pastoral Warning, the Line to Say, and the Leader's Map all put Jesus' tears before his promise, and the Shepherding Notes name the recently bereaved. Second, the delay of John 11:6 can be heard as a promise that every prayer for healing will be answered with rescue if we wait; so the Common Errors table, the Preaching Mistakes, and the Council's first question say plainly that the chapter promises resurrection, not escape from death. Third, the council's cynicism invites hearers to condemn others' hypocrisy; so the Light questions and practices turn the 'place' of John 11:48 on the participant and the leader first, and the Preaching Mistakes warn against treating Caiaphas or 'the Jews' as villains apart from ourselves. The naming round reads only the second row of the Light Planner, never the private first row.

### ✅ Answer Key
**Q1.** They sent word, 'Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest is sick' (John 11:3), describing Lazarus by Jesus' love for him. Jesus said the sickness was not unto death, but for the glory of God, that the Son of God might be glorified by it (John 11:4).

**Q2.** He stayed two more days where he was (John 11:6). The word 'therefore' at the start of John 11:6 ties the delay to the love of John 11:5: because he loved them, he waited.

**Q3.** The Jews had recently tried to stone him there (John 11:8; compare John 10:31). Jesus said a man who walks in the day does not stumble, because he sees the light of this world, but a man who walks in the night stumbles, because there is no light in him (John 11:9–10). Thomas said, 'Let us also go, that we may die with him' (John 11:16).

**Q4.** Jesus said Lazarus was sleeping and he would go to wake him; the disciples thought he meant ordinary sleep and that Lazarus would recover; then Jesus said plainly, 'Lazarus is dead' (John 11:11–14). He was glad for their sakes, so that they would believe (John 11:15).

**Q5.** Four days (John 11:17). Martha said that if Jesus had been there her brother would not have died; she said that even now God would give Jesus whatever he asked; and she believed Lazarus would rise again in the resurrection at the last day (John 11:21–24).

**Q6.** He said he is the resurrection and the life (John 11:25). The one who believes in him, though he die, shall live; whoever lives and believes in him shall never die (John 11:25–26). Martha confessed that he is the Christ, the Son of God, who was to come into the world (John 11:27).

**Q7.** Mary said, 'Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died' (John 11:32). Jesus groaned in the spirit and was troubled, asked where they had laid him, wept, and came to the grave groaning again (John 11:33–35, 38). Some said, 'Behold how he loved him', and others asked why he, who opened the eyes of the blind, could not have kept Lazarus from dying (John 11:36–37).

**Q8.** He commanded them to take away the stone; Martha objected that the body would stink after four days; he answered that he had told her that if she would believe, she would see the glory of God (John 11:39–40). He thanked the Father for hearing him, said he spoke aloud so the people would believe the Father sent him, cried 'Lazarus, come forth', and when Lazarus came out bound, told them to loose him and let him go (John 11:41–44).

**Q9.** Together they teach that death is an enemy God will swallow up (Isaiah 25:8; 1 Corinthians 15:26); that God himself will open the graves of his people and raise them (Ezekiel 37:12–13; Daniel 12:2); that the Son of God's voice will raise the dead (John 5:25, 28–29); that Christ's resurrection is the firstfruits of his people's (1 Corinthians 15:20–23); and that believers grieve with hope (1 Thessalonians 4:13–14). In John 11 Jesus claims to be that power in person, and shows it by his voice (John 11:25, 43).

**Q10.** Love can delay when it intends a greater good than immediate relief (John 11:5–6). The delay made Lazarus's death certain, gathered many witnesses, deepened the disciples' faith, led Martha to confess Jesus as the Christ, and displayed the glory of God and of the Son in a way a healing would not have (John 11:4, 15, 27, 40, 42, 45).

**Q11.** He adds that the resurrection is not only a future event but a person present with her, and that the life of the age to come belongs now to those who believe in him (John 11:25–26). Believing a doctrine keeps hope at a distance; trusting the person brings it near. He asks, 'Believest thou this' because the promise is received by faith in him (John 11:26–27; 20:31).

**Q12.** God meant that Jesus would die in the place of the people, so that they would not perish, and that his death would gather the scattered children of God, Jews and Gentiles, into one people (John 11:50–52; 10:16). It shows that God rules even over those who oppose him, turning their evil plans to accomplish his saving purpose (Acts 4:27–28; Genesis 50:20).

**Q13.** Both views have support: the lexicon's sense *to be moved with anger* and Mark 1:43 support indignation, while the tears of John 11:35 and the onlookers' 'Behold how he loved him' support grief (John 11:36). Many interpreters hold both: Jesus was angry at death and its causes and grieved with those he loved (John 11:33–38; Hebrews 2:14–15). It means he is roused against what hurts us and moved by our sorrow at the same time (Hebrews 4:15).

**Q14.** Like the family at Bethany, Paul was brought to the end of human hope so that he would trust only the God who raises the dead (2 Corinthians 1:9); Jesus waited so that the disciples and the sisters would believe more deeply and see God's glory (John 11:6, 15, 40). Answers to the personal question will vary.

**Q15.** Anita came, stayed, and wept with Ruth before saying anything, as Jesus wept before he raised Lazarus (John 11:33–35; Romans 12:15). She read Jesus' words rather than offering her own explanations, and she let Ruth see that Martha's lament was received, not rebuked (John 11:21, 25–26). The order mattered because comfort came before explanation. Members may name someone in grief.

**Q16.** Answers will vary. A believer's grave is a resting place, not an end, because the body is still Christ's and will be raised (John 11:11; 1 Thessalonians 4:14). 'Though he were dead, yet shall he live' supports both the life of the soul with Christ now and the resurrection of the body; 'shall never die' shows that the life Christ gives is not broken by death (John 11:25–26; Philippians 1:23).

**Q17.** Because Jesus himself is the resurrection, death cannot separate believers from the life they have in him, and believers who have died are safe in him (John 11:25–26; Romans 8:38–39). He ends with a question because the promise is received by faith, and he invites a personal answer (John 11:26–27).

**Q18.** Jesus grieves with us, and hates death, even when he knows the end (John 11:33–35). He does not only fix our sorrows later; he enters them now (Hebrews 4:15; Isaiah 53:3). Answers to the personal question will vary.

**Q19.** Answers will vary. Jesus did not rebuke either sister; he answered Martha with a promise and Mary with tears (John 11:21–27, 32–35). That invites honest prayer, while humbling ourselves to trust that his timing is love, not neglect (John 11:5–6; 1 Peter 5:6–7).

**Q20.** Answers will vary: a meal, a visit, a ride to the doctor, practical help with a funeral, listening to someone freed from an addiction who still struggles. The bystanders moved the stone and loosed the grave clothes at Jesus' word, but only his voice gave life (John 11:39, 43–44). We point to him by speaking his words and praying in his name (John 15:5).

**Q21.** Model answer, not the only answer: we try to save ourselves or others by worry, control, activity, or good advice, as if life depended on us (John 11:21, 37, 47). It makes Christ more glorious that the one who shared our sorrow is the one who has power over the grave: his tears and his voice belong to the same Lord (John 11:35, 43; Revelation 1:18).

**Q22.** Model answer, not the only answer: a widow or widower in the first year of loss; a friend with a terminal diagnosis; a parent who has lost a child; someone so depressed or ashamed that they live as if already entombed (John 11:17, 21, 44). Encourage members to write a specific name.

**Q23.** Model answer, not the only answer: a loved one, hope, sleep, faith that Jesus cares, the will to go on, relationships, and sometimes even the wish to live (John 11:21, 32, 37).

**Q24.** Model answer, not the only answer: Christ gives himself, the resurrection and the life, with his presence in grief and his promise beyond death (John 11:25–26, 35), and a voice that calls the dead out and a people who help loose them (John 11:43–44). We withhold his comfort when we stay away from grieving people because we do not know what to say (Romans 12:15).

**Q25.** Model answer, not the only answer: refusing to think about my own death; an old grief I have never mourned; a fear I hide behind busyness; protecting my reputation, position, or comfort rather than obeying Christ (John 11:10, 48). The honest answer belongs in the private first row of the Light Planner.

**Q26.** Model answer, not the only answer: seeing someone come to faith and worrying about how it will change my church or my plans; seeing God answer a prayer and moving on without thanks; resisting a call because of what it would cost (John 11:47–48; Luke 16:31).

**Q27.** Model answer, not the only answer: a friend who says death is the end; a relative who assumes everyone goes to heaven; a coworker terrified of illness (Hebrews 2:15; John 11:25–26). Carry light by sharing your own hope in Christ, listening first, and speaking gently and briefly, never mocking their fear (1 Peter 3:15; Ephesians 4:15).

### 🗺️ Life & Light Leader's Map
**Worked Life Planner Row:** *Who:* 'Mrs. Ellis, who lost her husband of forty years last spring and has stopped coming on Sundays since the funeral'. *What the thief has stolen:* her companion, her routine, her sense that anyone remembers, and some of her hope that God cares (John 11:21, 32). *What Christ gives instead:* the one who wept at a grave (John 11:35), and who is the resurrection and the life for her husband and for her (John 11:25–26). *What I will do:* on Wednesday afternoon, call her, tell her I have been thinking of her and of her husband by name, and ask if I can bring a meal on Saturday; sit with her and let her talk; read John 11:25–26 only if she would like it. *Done?* Tick it when done, and write one line about how it went.

**Worked Light Planner Row:** *Where is it dark (second row):* 'my coworker Sam, who laughs off any talk of death but has not been the same since his father's heart attack'. *What is hidden there:* a fear of death he will not admit, and no hope beyond it (Hebrews 2:15). *What light Christ shines:* the one who walks in the day and gives light to those who follow him (John 11:9–10), and who promised Martha that those who believe in him will live (John 11:25). *How I carry it there:* on Friday at lunch, ask Sam how his father is doing, listen, and, if the door opens, tell him in two sentences why I am not afraid of death; do not argue or push; pray for him afterwards.

**When Someone Has No One to Name:** Walk through their circles with them: the neighbour whose spouse died; the colleague with a sick parent; the church member in hospital or care home; the family that lost a child or a pregnancy; the friend whose loss was years ago and whom everyone has forgotten. Remind them that Jesus loved Lazarus by name and went to Bethany when it cost him (John 11:5, 8, 43). If they still cannot name anyone, ask them to pray tonight, 'Lord, show me one person who is grieving', and to bring the name next week.

**When Someone Points the Light at Others:** When a member names another church's leaders, a politician, or a family member as a 'Caiaphas' on their Light Planner, bring them gently back to the first row and to John 11:48. Say something like: 'The council were protecting their place. Where am I protecting mine? Let's start there.' Remind them that the Love Guard forbids using the light to shame, and that Jesus himself answered grief with tears and his enemies with a death for them (John 11:35, 50–52). If someone is genuinely being harmed by a leader, the right step is to care for them and speak privately to the right people (Matthew 18:15), not to broadcast accusations. If the member persists, speak with them after the session.

### 🐑 Shepherding Notes
- **The recently bereaved.** Some in the group will be in the first months or years of grief, and John 11 will open the wound. Let them weep; do not hurry them to verse 25. Jesus himself wept (John 11:35). Tell them beforehand, if you can, what the lesson covers, and give them permission to listen without speaking. Follow up personally the next day.
- **Those whose prayers for healing were not answered with healing.** Someone may have prayed as the sisters did and lost the person anyway, and may hear the raising of Lazarus as a cruel contrast. Acknowledge that Lazarus, too, died again, and that the chapter's great promise is not escape from death but resurrection (John 11:25–26). Do not suggest their faith was too small.
- **The terminally ill and their families.** A member facing their own death, or a loved one's, may find this lesson overwhelming or deeply comforting. Ask privately how they would like to participate, and pray with them afterward. Read John 11:25–26 and 14:1–3 to them if they wish.
- **Those who fear death.** Some carry a hidden terror of dying, of judgment, or of losing those they love (Hebrews 2:15). Do not shame the fear; point them to the one who has the keys of death (Revelation 1:18) and to his question, 'Believest thou this'.
- **Members from other faith backgrounds, or with Jewish family.** Explain that 'the Jews' in this chapter includes the sisters' Jewish friends who comforted them and believed, as well as the leaders who plotted (John 11:19, 45–53), and that Martha, Mary, Lazarus, and Jesus were Jews. Members from traditions that teach soul sleep or no conscious afterlife may hear the Debate Corner differently; answer questions gently, and offer to talk after the session.
- **Members who are angry at God.** Some will identify with the mourners who asked why Jesus did not prevent the death (John 11:37). Do not argue them out of it in the group. Point to Jesus' own groaning and tears, which show he is not indifferent, and invite them to bring their anger to him honestly.
- **The Light Planner's first row.** It is private. Never ask anyone to read it aloud or to share it. If someone chooses to speak about their own heart, receive it with gentleness and pray for them; do not probe for details.

### 🎯 Teaching Angles
**For a Tier 1 Group:** Bring a length of white cloth or gauze and a sheet of paper with 'I am the resurrection, and the life' written large. Wrap a volunteer's hands loosely in the cloth and ask how easy it would be to walk, eat, or hug someone like that. Then read John 11:43–44 and ask two volunteers to unwrap them while the leader reads 'Loose him, and let him go'. Ask: who gave Lazarus life, and who helped him get free? Then read John 11:35 and ask: why did Jesus cry if he knew Lazarus would live? End by holding up the paper, reading John 11:25 together, and asking: 'Who do you know who is sad because someone died, and how could you be kind to them this week?'

**For a Tier 2 Group:** Trace three lines through the chapter. The line of belief: 'believe' and its forms occur 9 times in the KJV of John 11; list who is invited to believe, who believes, and who does not, and ask why the council's words 'all men will believe on him' are so ironic (John 11:15, 25–27, 40, 42, 45, 48). The line of glory: 'glory' and 'glorified' occur 3 times in the KJV of John 11 (John 11:4, 40); ask how the glory of God and the glory of the Son are joined, and how the sign leads to the cross (John 11:4, 53; 12:23). The line of sleep: 'sleep' and its forms occur 4 times in the KJV of John 11 (John 11:11–13); ask why Jesus uses the picture and why he then speaks plainly, and read 1 Thessalonians 4:13–14. Then ask the group to set John 11:25–26 beside John 5:24–29 and explain how Jesus is the resurrection both now and at the last day.

**Tough Question 1:** *If Jesus loved Lazarus, why did he let him die? And why doesn't he raise my loved one?* John says Jesus loved the family and 'therefore' waited, and that the delay served God's glory and their faith (John 11:4–6, 15). But the chapter also shows that Jesus wept with them; he did not treat their grief lightly (John 11:35). Lazarus was raised to die again; the greater promise, for Lazarus and for every believer, is the resurrection at the last day and life with Christ now (John 11:24–26; 1 Thessalonians 4:14). Your loved one who died in Christ will hear the same voice. See the Council's first question.

**Tough Question 2:** *Why would Jesus weep if he knew he was about to raise Lazarus?* Because knowing the end does not make present sorrow unreal. As a true man he grieved with those he loved, and he was roused against death, the enemy that sin brought into the world (John 11:33–35; Romans 5:12; 1 Corinthians 15:26). His tears show us how he feels about our grief, and his command shows us what he will do about it (John 11:43). See the Council's second question.

**Tough Question 3:** *When Jesus says believers 'shall never die', does he mean they don't really die, or that the dead are just asleep until the resurrection?* Believers do die in the body; Jesus says so in the same breath ('though he were dead', John 11:25). 'Shall never die' means that the life he gives is never broken: the believer who dies is with the Lord (Philippians 1:23; 2 Corinthians 5:8), and the body sleeps until he raises it at the last day (John 11:11, 24; 1 Thessalonians 4:14). Jesus himself explained his picture of sleep by saying 'plainly, Lazarus is dead' (John 11:14). See the Debate Corner.
