# John 8 · The Light of the World (KJV)
> Unit john-08 · John 8:1–59 · loop john-kjv-study v2.0 · DRAFT for human doctrine review

## 🧭 At a Glance
### 🧵 Golden Thread
Jesus, the I AM, is the light of the world: he forgives the guilty, frees the slaves of sin, and exposes those who claim Abraham but reject Abraham's Lord.

### 🌗 Life & Light Line
John 8 answers a death that wears religious clothes: a woman dragged out to be stoned, and a crowd proud that they 'were never in bondage to any man' (John 8:33) while sin holds them as slaves and they are on the way to 'die in your sins' (John 8:24). Christ gives instead the freedom of the Son who abides in the house for ever, 'free indeed' (John 8:36), and a word that, kept, means a man 'shall never see death' (John 8:51). It exposes the darkness of accusers who hide their own sin behind another's, and of hearts where his word 'hath no place' (John 8:37); the light he gives instead is himself, 'the light of the world' (John 8:12), who sends the accusers away convicted and sends the guilty woman away uncondemned and called to holiness (John 8:9–11).

### 🎯 Objectives
- Explain the claim of John 8:58, 'Before Abraham was, I am', alongside Exodus 3:14 and the 'I am he' sayings of John 8:24 and 8:28, and say why the crowd took up stones (John 8:59).
- Trace how Jesus deals with sin in two scenes (the woman in John 8:1–11, and the crowd that claims Abraham in John 8:31–47) and show from John 8:32–36 how the Son makes sinners free.
- **Doing:** Within the next day, name one sin that holds you as a slave and bring it to Christ in confession (John 8:34, 36), and speak one word of truth and mercy, not accusation, to someone who is ashamed of their past (John 8:11).

## 🏛️ Core Foundations
### ✝️ Christological Root
**Gospel Foundation:** John 8 rests on three sayings of Jesus, spoken in the temple at the close of the feast of tabernacles to a crowd that would end the day with stones in its hands. First, what he is to a dark world: "Then spake Jesus again unto them, saying, I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life." (John 8:12) Second, what he does for slaves: "If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed." (John 8:36) Third, who he is: "Jesus said unto them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Before Abraham was, I am." (John 8:58) And before any of these, John sets a scene in which he does what the sayings promise, to one guilty woman: "She said, No man, Lord. And Jesus said unto her, Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more." (John 8:11)

**The Bridge:** At a feast that remembered the pillar of fire that lit Israel's way through the wilderness (Exodus 13:21), and in the court where, by later tradition, great lamps were lit (verify), Jesus says that he himself is the light, and that whoever follows him will not walk in darkness. At the end of the day he takes the name God gave Moses at the burning bush, 'I AM' (Exodus 3:14), and the crowd understands him well enough to reach for stones. The doctrines grounded here are the eternal deity of the Son, who is 'from above' and existed before Abraham (John 8:23, 58); the necessity of faith in him, without which men die in their sins (John 8:24); the bondage of the will to sin and to the devil (John 8:34, 44); the freedom the Son gives by the truth (John 8:32, 36); and forgiveness that does not cancel holiness but creates it: 'go, and sin no more' (John 8:11).

**Canonical Echoes:** Jesus (John 8:58) → Promise (Exodus 3:14; Isaiah 9:2; Isaiah 43:10) → Doctrine (Romans 6:17–18; Colossians 1:13) → Life (Revelation 21:23)

**What Gets Lost:** Taught without Christ at the centre, John 8 falls into pieces that can each be preached wrongly. The woman's story becomes a lesson in tolerance ('who are we to judge?'), and the call to 'sin no more' is dropped. 'The truth shall make you free' becomes a motto for education, carved over university doors, with no Son in it. 'Ye are of your father the devil' becomes a weapon against other people. And 'Before Abraham was, I am' becomes a puzzle about grammar. With Christ at the centre, every piece holds together: the one who forgives the woman is the I AM who alone has the right to condemn; the truth that frees is the Son himself; and the light that exposes the accusers is the same light that gives life to all who follow him.

### ⚔️ Spiritual Warfare
**The Lie:** You are free already. Your family, your religion, your upbringing, and your good opinion of yourself guarantee it: 'We be Abraham's seed, and were never in bondage to any man' (John 8:33). The lie has a twin that it uses on others: their sin is the real problem, and yours can stay hidden while you point at theirs (John 8:3–5). And behind both stands the old lie of the garden, that sin brings freedom and death will not follow: "And the serpent said unto the woman, Ye shall not surely die:" (Genesis 3:4)

**The Truth:** Jesus names the slavery and the only way out of it: "Jesus answered them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin." (John 8:34) "If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed." (John 8:36)

**The Battleground:** The conscience and the family tree. The conscience, which convicted the accusers one by one, eldest first (John 8:9), and which the enemy wants either numbed or crushed. And the family tree, the question of who our father is (John 8:39, 41, 44): where we draw our identity, our security, and our sense of being right with God. The tongue is here too, in the accuser's voice and the liar's (John 8:4, 44).

**The Weapon:** The word of Christ, continued in: "Then said Jesus to those Jews which believed on him, If ye continue in my word, then are ye my disciples indeed;" (John 8:31) The devil is a liar and 'abode not in the truth' (John 8:44); the believer overcomes him by abiding in the truth that Christ speaks and is, and by the Son's own work, who was manifested to 'destroy the works of the devil' (1 John 3:8).

### 🌱 Life Pathway
**The Theft:** In John 8 the thief steals by accusation, by slavery, by lies, and by death. He steals a woman's dignity and very nearly her life, setting her 'in the midst' as a specimen and a pawn in someone else's argument (John 8:3–6). He steals freedom while leaving people sure that they are free (John 8:33–34). He steals the family name, so that children of Abraham do the works of a murderer (John 8:40–41, 44). He steals hearing, so that Jesus' words find 'no place' (John 8:37) and men 'cannot hear my word' (John 8:43). And at the end he steals life itself: 'ye shall die in your sins' (John 8:21, 24). He is 'a murderer from the beginning' (John 8:44) because he comes 'for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy' (John 10:10).

**The Source:** Christ, the Son who 'abideth ever' (John 8:35) and who alone can set a slave free: "If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed." (John 8:36) He came "that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly" (John 10:10). His means in this chapter are his word of no condemnation (John 8:11), his word continued in (John 8:31–32), his saying kept, by which a man 'shall never see death' (John 8:51), and his being 'lifted up' on the cross (John 8:28).

**The Channel:** The woman went away with a word that was not hers: 'Neither do I condemn thee' (John 8:11). That is how life passes through a believer: not by our own verdicts on others, but by carrying Christ's word to people the world has already sentenced. The disciple who continues in his word (John 8:31) becomes someone who can say to another slave of sin, *the Son sets slaves free; I know, because he freed me*. The believer never becomes the Son; we are freed slaves pointing to the one who abides in the house for ever. "For without me ye can do nothing." (John 15:5)

**The Abundance Guard:** Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, not prosperity, ease, or freedom from suffering. The freedom of John 8:36 is not the freedom to do as we please, which Jesus calls slavery (John 8:34); it is freedom from sin to serve righteousness (Romans 6:18). The Jesus who gives it was slandered as a Samaritan and a madman, and had stones thrown at him before the day was out (John 8:48, 59). The promise that his people 'shall never see death' (John 8:51) does not keep them from dying; it keeps them from death's power and its sting.

### 💡 Light Pathway
**The Darkness:** The darkness of John 8 is the darkness of the hidden self. The accusers drag one sin into the light to hide their own, and bring the woman but not the man (John 8:3–4; Leviticus 20:10). The crowd judges 'after the flesh' (John 8:15), by appearances and pedigree. They do not know where Jesus comes from or where he goes (John 8:14), and do not know the Father (John 8:19). They 'understood not' (John 8:27). They cannot hear his word, because they love another father's lies (John 8:43–44). And the deepest darkness is refusing the light because it tells the truth: 'because I tell you the truth, ye believe me not' (John 8:45).

**The Source:** Christ, who says, "I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life." (John 8:12) "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." (2 Corinthians 4:6)

**The Lamp:** In John 8 the lamp is a single sentence that turns each accuser's eyes on his own heart: 'He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her' (John 8:7). It is the word of Christ, continued in (John 8:31), and the truth he tells (John 8:40, 45). For us the lamp is the same: the Scriptures opened, our own hidden sin confessed before anyone else's is mentioned, the unfruitful works of darkness reproved (Ephesians 5:11) with the gentleness Jesus showed, and good works that glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16), including the work of restoring the fallen rather than exhibiting them (Galatians 6:1).

**The Love Guard:** Light without love is glare. Jesus exposed the accusers without naming a single one of their sins aloud; he let their own consciences do it, and let them leave one by one. To the woman, whose sin was not in doubt, he spoke no word of contempt. He called her 'Woman', the same courteous address he used to his own mother (John 2:4), refused to condemn her, and then told her the truth about her life: 'go, and sin no more' (John 8:11). The Pharisees' light was all glare, a woman set 'in the midst' (John 8:3). Christ's light is grace and truth together (John 1:14).

### 🏺 Setting the Scene
John 8 continues the visit to Jerusalem that began at the feast of tabernacles in John 7. The feast has just ended, or is ending, and Jesus is still teaching in the temple courts. Later Jewish tradition, written down in the Mishnah about AD 200 (tractate *Sukkah*, chapter 5, verify), describes great golden lampstands lit in the temple's Court of the Women during the feast, so bright that every courtyard in Jerusalem was lit by them. John tells us that Jesus spoke some of this chapter's words 'in the treasury' (John 8:20). The treasury was probably in or beside that same court, where the chests for offerings stood (compare Mark 12:41–43, verify the location). If so, Jesus said 'I am the light of the world' (John 8:12) in the place where the feast's lamps had blazed. The feast also remembered the pillar of fire that lit Israel's way through the wilderness (Exodus 13:21). John does not describe the lamps, and we cannot be sure every detail of the Mishnah's account was practised in Jesus' day; but the setting helps us hear the claim.

The chapter opens with the account of the woman taken in adultery (John 7:53–8:11). The King James Version prints it in full, and this study teaches it as printed. Many modern Bibles set it in brackets or in a footnote, because it is missing from the oldest Greek manuscripts of John that survive; the leader's notes explain this calmly, and no doctrine of the faith depends on it. The law of Moses commanded death for adultery, for both the man and the woman (Leviticus 20:10; Deuteronomy 22:22), and required at least two witnesses, whose hands were to be first against the guilty (Deuteronomy 17:6–7). But under Roman rule the Jewish authorities could not lawfully carry out a death sentence on their own; later, before Pilate, they say, 'It is not lawful for us to put any man to death' (John 18:31). That is why the question they put to Jesus was a trap.

The rest of the chapter is one long, rising controversy. 'The Jews' here are the people in the temple who argued with Jesus, among them the Pharisees (John 8:13) and even some 'which believed on him' (John 8:31), not the Jewish people as a whole: Jesus and his disciples were Jews too. Listen for three questions the chapter keeps asking. Who is Jesus ('Who art thou?', John 8:25)? Who is our father (John 8:39, 41, 44)? And who is free (John 8:33, 36)? The chapter ends with the answer to the first, and with stones.

#### John 8:1–11 · Neither Do I Condemn Thee
**1** Jesus went unto the mount of Olives.

**2** And early in the morning he came again into the temple, and all the people came unto him; and he sat down, and taught them.

**3** And the scribes and Pharisees brought unto him a woman taken in adultery; and when they had set her in the midst,

**4** They say unto him, Master, this woman was taken in adultery, in the very act.

**5** Now Moses in the law commanded us, that such should be stoned: but what sayest thou?

**6** This they said, tempting him, that they might have to accuse him. But Jesus stooped down, and with his finger wrote on the ground, as though he heard them not.

**7** So when they continued asking him, he lifted up himself, and said unto them, He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her.

**8** And again he stooped down, and wrote on the ground.

**9** And they which heard it, being convicted by their own conscience, went out one by one, beginning at the eldest, even unto the last: and Jesus was left alone, and the woman standing in the midst.

**10** When Jesus had lifted up himself, and saw none but the woman, he said unto her, Woman, where are those thine accusers? hath no man condemned thee?

**11** She said, No man, Lord. And Jesus said unto her, Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more.

#### John 8:12–20 · I Am the Light of the World
**12** Then spake Jesus again unto them, saying, I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life.

**13** The Pharisees therefore said unto him, Thou bearest record of thyself; thy record is not true.

**14** Jesus answered and said unto them, Though I bear record of myself, yet my record is true: for I know whence I came, and whither I go; but ye cannot tell whence I come, and whither I go.

**15** Ye judge after the flesh; I judge no man.

**16** And yet if I judge, my judgment is true: for I am not alone, but I and the Father that sent me.

**17** It is also written in your law, that the testimony of two men is true.

**18** I am one that bear witness of myself, and the Father that sent me beareth witness of me.

**19** Then said they unto him, Where is thy Father? Jesus answered, Ye neither know me, nor my Father: if ye had known me, ye should have known my Father also.

**20** These words spake Jesus in the treasury, as he taught in the temple: and no man laid hands on him; for his hour was not yet come.

#### John 8:21–30 · Ye Shall Die in Your Sins
**21** Then said Jesus again unto them, I go my way, and ye shall seek me, and shall die in your sins: whither I go, ye cannot come.

**22** Then said the Jews, Will he kill himself? because he saith, Whither I go, ye cannot come.

**23** And he said unto them, Ye are from beneath; I am from above: ye are of this world; I am not of this world.

**24** I said therefore unto you, that ye shall die in your sins: for if ye believe not that I am he, ye shall die in your sins.

**25** Then said they unto him, Who art thou? And Jesus saith unto them, Even the same that I said unto you from the beginning.

**26** I have many things to say and to judge of you: but he that sent me is true; and I speak to the world those things which I have heard of him.

**27** They understood not that he spake to them of the Father.

**28** Then said Jesus unto them, When ye have lifted up the Son of man, then shall ye know that I am he, and that I do nothing of myself; but as my Father hath taught me, I speak these things.

**29** And he that sent me is with me: the Father hath not left me alone; for I do always those things that please him.

**30** As he spake these words, many believed on him.

#### John 8:31–36 · The Truth Shall Make You Free
**31** Then said Jesus to those Jews which believed on him, If ye continue in my word, then are ye my disciples indeed;

**32** And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free.

**33** They answered him, We be Abraham's seed, and were never in bondage to any man: how sayest thou, Ye shall be made free?

**34** Jesus answered them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin.

**35** And the servant abideth not in the house for ever: but the Son abideth ever.

**36** If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed.

#### John 8:37–47 · Your Father the Devil
**37** I know that ye are Abraham's seed; but ye seek to kill me, because my word hath no place in you.

**38** I speak that which I have seen with my Father: and ye do that which ye have seen with your father.

**39** They answered and said unto him, Abraham is our father. Jesus saith unto them, If ye were Abraham's children, ye would do the works of Abraham.

**40** But now ye seek to kill me, a man that hath told you the truth, which I have heard of God: this did not Abraham.

**41** Ye do the deeds of your father. Then said they to him, We be not born of fornication; we have one Father, even God.

**42** Jesus said unto them, If God were your Father, ye would love me: for I proceeded forth and came from God; neither came I of myself, but he sent me.

**43** Why do ye not understand my speech? even because ye cannot hear my word.

**44** Ye are of your father the devil, and the lusts of your father ye will do. He was a murderer from the beginning, and abode not in the truth, because there is no truth in him. When he speaketh a lie, he speaketh of his own: for he is a liar, and the father of it.

**45** And because I tell you the truth, ye believe me not.

**46** Which of you convinceth me of sin? And if I say the truth, why do ye not believe me?

**47** He that is of God heareth God's words: ye therefore hear them not, because ye are not of God.

#### John 8:48–59 · Before Abraham Was, I Am
**48** Then answered the Jews, and said unto him, Say we not well that thou art a Samaritan, and hast a devil?

**49** Jesus answered, I have not a devil; but I honour my Father, and ye do dishonour me.

**50** And I seek not mine own glory: there is one that seeketh and judgeth.

**51** Verily, verily, I say unto you, If a man keep my saying, he shall never see death.

**52** Then said the Jews unto him, Now we know that thou hast a devil. Abraham is dead, and the prophets; and thou sayest, If a man keep my saying, he shall never taste of death.

**53** Art thou greater than our father Abraham, which is dead? and the prophets are dead: whom makest thou thyself?

**54** Jesus answered, If I honour myself, my honour is nothing: it is my Father that honoureth me; of whom ye say, that he is your God:

**55** Yet ye have not known him; but I know him: and if I should say, I know him not, I shall be a liar like unto you: but I know him, and keep his saying.

**56** Your father Abraham rejoiced to see my day: and he saw it, and was glad.

**57** Then said the Jews unto him, Thou art not yet fifty years old, and hast thou seen Abraham?

**58** Jesus said unto them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Before Abraham was, I am.

**59** Then took they up stones to cast at him: but Jesus hid himself, and went out of the temple, going through the midst of them, and so passed by.

**View A:** In 'Neither do I condemn thee' (John 8:11), Jesus forgives the woman. The Son of man 'hath power on earth to forgive sins' (Mark 2:10), and Jesus says the same to another sinful woman: "And he said unto her, Thy sins are forgiven." (Luke 7:48) She calls him 'Lord' (John 8:11), and he answers her with a word of pardon and then a word of new life, in that order. Many preachers and commentators through the centuries, Augustine among them (verify), have read it so.

**View B:** In 'Neither do I condemn thee' (John 8:11), Jesus declines to pass a legal sentence, without yet declaring her forgiven. The witnesses have gone (John 8:9), and the law required witnesses before anyone could be condemned (Deuteronomy 17:6); and Jesus had not come as a judge to condemn: "For God sent not his Son into the world to condemn the world; but that the world through him might be saved." (John 3:17) His words leave her time to repent, and 'go, and sin no more' is the call to do so. Several careful commentators hold this reading, while agreeing that Jesus did not excuse her sin (verify).

**Q13.** Which view does the text best support, and why? Before you answer, notice that both views agree on two things: Jesus did not call her sin nothing, and he did not send her back to it. What does each view make of the order of his two sentences, 'Neither do I condemn thee' and then 'go, and sin no more' (John 8:11)?

### 👣 Case Study · Scripture in Action
King David had committed adultery with Bathsheba and arranged the death of her husband, Uriah (2 Samuel 11). For months he kept it hidden. Then the prophet Nathan came and told him a story about a rich man who stole a poor man's only lamb. David was furious, and passed sentence at once: "And David's anger was greatly kindled against the man; and he said to Nathan, As the LORD liveth, the man that hath done this thing shall surely die:" (2 Samuel 12:5) He was quick to condemn another man's sin, and blind to his own, like the accusers in John 8. Then Nathan turned the light: "And Nathan said to David, Thou art the man. Thus saith the LORD God of Israel, I anointed thee king over Israel, and I delivered thee out of the hand of Saul;" (2 Samuel 12:7) Unlike the accusers, who went out one by one without a word, David stayed and confessed: "And David said unto Nathan, I have sinned against the LORD. And Nathan said unto David, The LORD also hath put away thy sin; thou shalt not die." (2 Samuel 12:13) The man who deserved the stones under the law heard, through the prophet, a word like the one the woman heard from Jesus. He still suffered the consequences of his sin (2 Samuel 12:14), and Psalm 51, whose title ties it to this moment, shows a man who was not excused but forgiven, and who asked for a clean heart.

### 👑 Christocentric & Humility · Level 4
Every problem in John 8 is answered in Jesus. A guilty woman is dragged out to die, and the law's sentence hangs over her; he is the only one present with the right to throw the first stone, and he does not. Her accusers use Moses to trap him; he upholds the law's holiness ('sin no more') and exposes their own sin without a single accusation. The Pharisees say his witness is not true; he answers that the Father bears witness with him. The crowd cannot tell who he is ('Who art thou?'); he tells them he is 'from above', and that they will know it when they have lifted him up. Slaves insist they are free; he is the Son who abides in the house for ever and can set them free. They claim Abraham and God as their father while planning murder; he tells them the truth about their hearts and their real father. They call him a Samaritan and possessed; he answers by honouring his Father. They ask whether he is greater than Abraham; he tells them that Abraham rejoiced to see his day, and that before Abraham came to be, 'I am'. And when they take up stones, the hour is not yet come, and he passes through the midst of them.

Every truth in the chapter is found in him. Light is in him (John 8:12). The true witness is his (John 8:14). The knowledge of the Father is in him (John 8:19). Freedom is his to give (John 8:36). Truth is what he tells (John 8:40, 45), and he is without sin (John 8:46). Life beyond death is in his saying (John 8:51). Abraham's hope was his day (John 8:56). And eternal being is his own: 'I am' (John 8:58). The chapter humbles every hearer. It humbles the accuser, because no one could throw the first stone. It humbles the religious, because descent from Abraham did not make them free. It humbles the proud, because the devil's first marks are murder and lies, and the crowd showed both. And it makes Christ glorious, because the eternal I AM stood in a courtyard with a disgraced woman and gave her what no court on earth could, and then went on toward the hour when he would be 'lifted up' (John 8:28) for sinners like her and like us.

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy
### 📌 Core Concept
Early one morning Jesus was teaching people in the temple, God's house in Jerusalem. Some religious leaders pushed through the crowd dragging a woman who had done something very wrong: she had broken her promise to her husband. They said God's law said she should be killed with stones, and they asked Jesus what he thought. It was a trick. Jesus bent down and wrote on the ground with his finger. Then he stood up and said, in words a child can hold, *whoever here has never done anything wrong can throw the first stone*. One by one, starting with the oldest, the men walked away, because each of them knew he had done wrong things too. Only Jesus was left with the woman. Jesus had never done anything wrong, so he was the only one who could have thrown a stone. Instead he said, *I don't condemn you either; go, and don't do this any more*. Then Jesus told everyone, 'I am the light of the world'. He said that people who do wrong things are like slaves, stuck and unable to get free, but that he, God's Son, can make them really free. The people argued with him and said they were already free because they came from Abraham's family. At the end Jesus said something only God can say: 'Before Abraham was, I am'. He meant that he has always been alive, because he is God. Some people were so angry that they picked up stones to throw at him, but Jesus slipped away, because it was not yet God's time.

### 📖 Key Verses
"She said, No man, Lord. And Jesus said unto her, Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more." (John 8:11) Jesus forgives people who have done wrong, and then helps them to live a new way.

"Then spake Jesus again unto them, saying, I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life." (John 8:12) If we follow Jesus, we are never lost in the dark, because he shows us the way to God.

"And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free." (John 8:32) When we listen to Jesus and keep listening, the truth he tells sets us free.

"If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed." (John 8:36) Only Jesus, God's Son, can set us free from the wrong things that trap us.

"Jesus said unto them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Before Abraham was, I am." (John 8:58) Jesus was alive before anyone was born, because he is God.

### 🪞 Analogy
Imagine your bedroom at night with the lights off. You trip over things, bump your toes, and you can't find the door. Someone switches on the light. Now you can see the mess on the floor, the shoes and toys you were tripping over. The light doesn't make the mess; it shows you what was there all along. But it also shows you the way to the door. Jesus is like that light. When he shines into our hearts, we see the wrong things we have done, and that can feel embarrassing. But he doesn't switch the light on to laugh at us, like the men who laughed at the woman. He switches it on so that we can see our way to him, and then he helps us clean up. And he doesn't just show us the door. He opens it, like a son of the house who has the key, so that we can walk out free.

- Do I think I'm fine with God because my family goes to church?
- Do I believe Jesus would forgive me, even for the worst thing I've done?
- Do I believe that Jesus is God, who has always been alive?

Tomorrow, if you hear someone being laughed at or picked on for something they did, don't join in. Be kind to them instead, the way Jesus was kind to the woman.

### 🌗 Life and Light, Simply
Jesus gives life because he forgives people who have done wrong, and sets them free from the wrong things that trap them, so they can live a new way. Jesus gives light because he is like a bright light in a dark room: he shows us the truth about ourselves and about God, and he shows us the way home.

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium
### 📜 Walkthrough
#### John 8:1–2 · The Mount of Olives and the Temple at Dawn
**Says:** "Jesus went unto the mount of Olives." (John 8:1) "And early in the morning he came again into the temple, and all the people came unto him; and he sat down, and taught them." (John 8:2)

**Means:** John 7 ended with the council breaking up and 'every man went unto his own house' (John 7:53). Jesus goes instead to the mount of Olives, the ridge east of Jerusalem across the Kidron valley. Luke tells us this was his habit in his last week, teaching in the temple by day and lodging on the mount by night (Luke 21:37–38). At dawn he is back in the temple courts, and 'all the people' gather. 'He sat down, and taught them': a Jewish teacher usually sat to teach (compare Matthew 5:1; Luke 4:20), and the posture tells us this is a public, settled teaching session, not a chance meeting. The account that follows, John 7:53–8:11, is the passage about which the manuscripts differ; this study teaches it as the KJV prints it, and the question is explained calmly in the Original Language Deep Dive and the Council's first question.

**Asks:** Jesus had a place of retreat and a place of public work, and he went from one to the other. The day's hard encounter began after a night on the mount and a morning among the people. Where do you meet with God before the day's conflicts meet you?

#### John 8:3–6 · The Trap
**Says:** "And the scribes and Pharisees brought unto him a woman taken in adultery; and when they had set her in the midst," (John 8:3) "They say unto him, Master, this woman was taken in adultery, in the very act." (John 8:4) "Now Moses in the law commanded us, that such should be stoned: but what sayest thou?" (John 8:5) "This they said, tempting him, that they might have to accuse him. But Jesus stooped down, and with his finger wrote on the ground, as though he heard them not." (John 8:6) *Master* here means teacher; *tempting* means testing, trying to trap.

**Means:** The scribes, experts in the law, and the Pharisees interrupt the teaching and set the woman 'in the midst', in front of everyone, as an exhibit. The charge is *moicheia* (G3430, John 8:3), adultery. 'Taken' is *kateilēmmenēn* (G2638, John 8:3, V-RPP-ASF), from *katalambanō*, which the STEPBible lexicon lists at John 8:3–4 under the sense 'to surprise, discover'; it is the same verb John used for the darkness that did not overtake the light (John 1:5). 'In the very act' is *autophōrō* (G1888, John 8:4), which the lexicon explains as a word used properly of theft and then generally of being caught 'in the very act'. The evidence against her is not in doubt.

But a question hangs over the scene: where is the man? Adultery is not committed alone, and the law they cite commanded death for both: "And the man that committeth adultery with another man's wife, even he that committeth adultery with his neighbour's wife, the adulterer and the adulteress shall surely be put to death." (Leviticus 20:10) The law named stoning for a betrothed woman and the man with her (Deuteronomy 22:23–24); whether 'such' in John 8:5 points to that case or to adultery generally is debated (verify). Either way, the accusers have brought half the guilty parties, and they have not come to uphold the law. John tells us why they came: 'tempting him, that they might have to accuse him'. *Peirazontes* (G3985, John 8:6) is testing with hostile intent; *katēgorein* (G2723, John 8:6) is to bring a formal charge. The trap has two jaws. If Jesus says 'Stone her', he contradicts the mercy for which sinners loved him (Luke 15:1–2) and claims a power of execution that Rome kept for itself (John 18:31). If he says 'Let her go', he sets himself against Moses.

Jesus does not answer. He 'stooped down', *kupsas* (G2955, John 8:6), 'and with his finger wrote on the ground'. The verb is an imperfect, *egraphen* (G1125, John 8:6, V-IAI-3S): he was writing, or kept writing. John does not tell us what he wrote, and every answer is a guess. Some early writers suggested he wrote the accusers' own sins (verify); some readers hear an echo of Jeremiah, "they that depart from me shall be written in the earth" (Jeremiah 17:13); others remember that the law itself was 'written with the finger of God' (Exodus 31:18). What John does record is the effect: a silence that refuses to be hurried, and that throws the question back on those who asked it.

**Asks:** It is possible to use someone else's sin as a weapon for our own ends, and to call it zeal for God's law. Before we speak about another person's fall, we should ask what we want: their restoration, or our own point scored? And when we are pressed to answer on the spot, silence can be the beginning of wisdom.

#### John 8:7–9 · Without Sin Among You
**Says:** "So when they continued asking him, he lifted up himself, and said unto them, He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her." (John 8:7) "And again he stooped down, and wrote on the ground." (John 8:8) "And they which heard it, being convicted by their own conscience, went out one by one, beginning at the eldest, even unto the last: and Jesus was left alone, and the woman standing in the midst." (John 8:9)

**Means:** They 'continued asking', *epemenon* (G1961, John 8:7): they kept pressing. So Jesus stands and gives one sentence. 'He that is without sin', *anamartētos* (G361, John 8:7), a word the STEPBible lexicon defines in its moral sense as 'without sin' and lists only here in the New Testament. 'Let him first cast a stone', *baletō* (G906, John 8:7, V-2AAM-3S). The word 'first' matters. The law required the witnesses to throw first: "The hands of the witnesses shall be first upon him to put him to death, and afterward the hands of all the people. So thou shalt put the evil away from among you." (Deuteronomy 17:7) Jesus does not abolish the law. He applies it, and turns its demand on the witnesses themselves. He is not saying that no judge may ever sentence a criminal unless the judge is sinless; the law itself appointed judges who were sinners. He is saying to these men, in this case, who brought half the guilty to spring a trap: look at yourselves before you throw.

He stoops and writes again, and leaves them alone with his sentence. 'Being convicted by their own conscience': *suneidēseōs* (G4893, John 8:9), which the STEPBible lexicon defines in its ethical sense as 'innate discernment, self-judging consciousness, conscience'; and *elegchomenoi* (G1651, John 8:9, V-PPP-NPM), a present passive participle, 'being convicted', 'being shown their guilt'. The clause about conscience stands in the Textus Receptus behind the KJV, though some manuscripts that contain the passage lack it (verify); either way, their leaving says the same. They go out 'one by one, beginning at the eldest'. Perhaps the elders had the longest memories of their own sins; perhaps the younger simply followed their seniors (verify neither). The trap has closed on the men who set it. 'And Jesus was left alone', *kateleiphthē* (G2641, John 8:9) *monos* (G3441, John 8:9), 'and the woman standing in the midst'. Augustine remarks, in substance, that when the accusers had gone, only two were left: wretchedness and mercy (*Tractates on John* 33, verify).

**Asks:** The only one in the courtyard who was without sin was the one who had the right to throw the first stone, and he did not. The first place to turn the light is always our own heart. Where have you been ready to throw a stone that you would not want thrown at you?

#### John 8:10–11 · Neither Do I Condemn Thee
**Says:** "When Jesus had lifted up himself, and saw none but the woman, he said unto her, Woman, where are those thine accusers? hath no man condemned thee?" (John 8:10) "She said, No man, Lord. And Jesus said unto her, Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more." (John 8:11)

**Means:** 'Woman' is not cold; it is the courteous address Jesus used to his own mother (John 2:4; 19:26) and to the woman at the well (John 4:21). He asks two questions, and lets her say aloud what has happened: no one has condemned her. She answers, 'No man, Lord'. Her word is *kurie* (G2962, John 8:11), which can mean 'Sir' (as in John 4:11) or 'Lord'; the KJV renders it 'Lord', and on this occasion both are true.

Then Jesus speaks two sentences, and the order matters. First: 'Neither do I condemn thee', *katakrinō* (G2632, John 8:11, V-PAI-1S), a present: 'I do not condemn you'. Then: 'go', *poreuou* (G4198, John 8:11, V-PNM-2S), 'and sin no more', *mēketi* (G3371, John 8:11), 'no longer', with *hamartane* (G264, John 8:11, V-PAM-2S), a present imperative: stop living in sin. He does not call her sin nothing; he calls it sin. He does not send her back to it; he sends her away from it. And he gives the mercy first, so that the holiness grows out of it, not the other way round. He said nearly the same to the man at Bethesda: "Afterward Jesus findeth him in the temple, and said unto him, Behold, thou art made whole: sin no more, lest a worse thing come unto thee." (John 5:14)

Readers differ over whether 'Neither do I condemn thee' is a declaration of forgiveness or a refusal to pass a legal sentence (see the View A / View B panel). Either way, the one who alone had the right to condemn did not. The whole of John's Gospel tells us how he could do this without denying the law: "For God sent not his Son into the world to condemn the world; but that the world through him might be saved." (John 3:17) Before long the sinless one would be 'lifted up' (John 8:28), and the church has always read the cross as the place where the sentence she deserved was borne by another (2 Corinthians 5:21).

**Asks:** Christ's mercy does not leave us where it finds us. If you have heard 'Neither do I condemn thee', you have also heard 'go, and sin no more'. Hold both. And if you are tempted to think your sin too public or too shameful for his mercy, look at where this woman was standing when she heard it.

#### John 8:12–14 · I Am the Light of the World
**Says:** "Then spake Jesus again unto them, saying, I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life." (John 8:12) "The Pharisees therefore said unto him, Thou bearest record of thyself; thy record is not true." (John 8:13) "Jesus answered and said unto them, Though I bear record of myself, yet my record is true: for I know whence I came, and whither I go; but ye cannot tell whence I come, and whither I go." (John 8:14) *Bearest record* means testify; *whence* means from where; *whither* means to where.

**Means:** This is the second of Jesus' 'I am' sayings with an image, after the bread of life (John 6:35): *egō* (G1473, John 8:12) *eimi* (G1510, John 8:12), 'I am', with the emphatic pronoun, 'the light', *phōs* (G5457, John 8:12), 'of the world'. The STEPBible lexicon defines *phōs* as light, the opposite of darkness, and lists metaphorical uses of God and of Christ. The claim gathers up the Bible's whole story of light: the light of the first day (Genesis 1:3); the pillar of fire that led Israel by night (Exodus 13:21); the LORD who is 'my light and my salvation' (Psalm 27:1); the promise that the people in darkness would see 'a great light' (Isaiah 9:2); and the Servant given 'for a light to the Gentiles' (Isaiah 49:6), which is why he is the light 'of the world', not only of Israel. At the feast of tabernacles, where by later tradition great lamps lit the temple courts (verify), Jesus says that he is what the lamps and the pillar pointed to. John prepared us for this on his first page: 'the life was the light of men' (John 1:4).

The promise has two sides. 'He that followeth me', *akolouthōn* (G190, John 8:12, V-PAP-NSM), a present participle, is one who keeps following, as Israel followed the pillar through the desert. Such a person 'shall not walk in darkness', where the Greek puts two negatives together (*ou mē*), the strongest way the language has of saying 'never' (a standard observation of Greek grammar). And he 'shall have the light of life': the light that gives life, and that is life.

The Pharisees object on a point of law: 'Thou bearest record of thyself', *martureis* (G3140, John 8:13, V-PAI-2S), 'thy record', *marturia* (G3141, John 8:13), 'is not true', *alēthēs* (G227, John 8:13). A man's word about himself was not enough to establish a matter (Deuteronomy 19:15), and Jesus himself had made the same point in John 5:31. His answer here is that his case is unique: he alone knows 'whence I came, and whither I go'. He came from the Father and returns to him (John 13:3; 16:28). No other witness is in a position to testify to that origin; only one who has been there can say.

**Asks:** It is not enough to admire the light or to discuss it; the promise is to the one who follows. Is there any area of your life where you have stopped following and started walking by your own lamp?

#### John 8:15–18 · Two Witnesses
**Says:** "Ye judge after the flesh; I judge no man." (John 8:15) "And yet if I judge, my judgment is true: for I am not alone, but I and the Father that sent me." (John 8:16) "It is also written in your law, that the testimony of two men is true." (John 8:17) "I am one that bear witness of myself, and the Father that sent me beareth witness of me." (John 8:18)

**Means:** 'Ye judge after the flesh', *sarka* (G4561, John 8:15): by human standards, by what can be seen, as Jesus warned in John 7:24. That is exactly what the accusers had done that morning, and what the Pharisees are doing now. 'I judge no man' does not deny that Jesus is the judge; the Father 'hath committed all judgment unto the Son' (John 5:22). It means that he has not come now to condemn (John 3:17). 'And yet if I judge, my judgment is true', because he does not judge alone: 'I and the Father that sent me'.

Then he meets their legal objection on its own ground. The law required two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 17:6; 19:15). He has two: himself, and the Father who sent him. The Father's witness has already been named: the works the Father gave him to do, and the Scriptures (John 5:36–39). The argument is not a trick of procedure. It is the only possible witness to the Son's identity: only God can testify about God.

**Asks:** We judge by appearances all the time, and we are often wrong. Jesus' judgment is never 'after the flesh', and never alone; it is always one with the Father's. When you are tempted to settle who someone is by what you can see, remember whose judgment is true.

#### John 8:19–20 · Where Is Thy Father?
**Says:** "Then said they unto him, Where is thy Father? Jesus answered, Ye neither know me, nor my Father: if ye had known me, ye should have known my Father also." (John 8:19) "These words spake Jesus in the treasury, as he taught in the temple: and no man laid hands on him; for his hour was not yet come." (John 8:20)

**Means:** 'Where is thy Father?' They want the second witness produced. The question may be simply dismissive, or it may carry a sneer about his birth, which seems to surface again in John 8:41 (verify). Jesus answers that the Father cannot be produced like a witness on a bench, because he is known only in the Son: 'if ye had known me, ye should have known my Father also'. Later he will say it plainly to Philip: "Jesus saith unto him, Have I been so long time with you, and yet hast thou not known me, Philip? he that hath seen me hath seen the Father; and how sayest thou then, Shew us the Father?" (John 14:9)

John adds the setting: 'in the treasury', *gazophulakiō* (G1049, John 8:20), which the STEPBible lexicon describes as the temple treasury, including, apparently, the trumpet-shaped chests into which the people's offerings were thrown (Mark 12:41–43). It was among the busiest places in the temple, and probably near the court where the feast's lamps stood (verify). Jesus made his claims in the most public place, and still 'no man laid hands on him; for his hour was not yet come'. The hour, *hōra* (G5610, John 8:20), and the pluperfect *elēluthei* (G2064, John 8:20, V-2LAI-3S), 'had not yet come', repeat the narrator's comment of John 7:30. Nothing happens to Jesus before the Father's appointed time.

**Asks:** Many people want God on their own terms, produced on demand. Jesus says that the Father is known through the Son, or not at all. If you want to know what God is like, look at Jesus.

#### John 8:21–24 · From Beneath and From Above
**Says:** "Then said Jesus again unto them, I go my way, and ye shall seek me, and shall die in your sins: whither I go, ye cannot come." (John 8:21) "Then said the Jews, Will he kill himself? because he saith, Whither I go, ye cannot come." (John 8:22) "And he said unto them, Ye are from beneath; I am from above: ye are of this world; I am not of this world." (John 8:23) "I said therefore unto you, that ye shall die in your sins: for if ye believe not that I am he, ye shall die in your sins." (John 8:24)

**Means:** Jesus repeats and sharpens the warning of John 7:34. He is going away, to the Father, by way of the cross; they will seek him and not find him; and they will 'die in your sins'. In John 8:21 the Textus Receptus has the singular, *hamartia* (G266, John 8:21), 'in your sin', which the KJV renders with the plural; many read the singular as the root sin of unbelief, and the plural of John 8:24 as the sins that flow from it.

Their answer is another of John's misunderstandings, and a bitter piece of irony: 'Will he kill himself?' He will indeed lay down his life of himself (John 10:18), but it is they who will kill him. Jesus explains the gulf between them: 'Ye are from beneath', *katō* (G2736, John 8:23), 'I am from above', *anō* (G507, John 8:23). He belongs to another realm, as the Baptist had said: 'He that cometh from above is above all' (John 3:31).

Then comes the chapter's first great 'I am' without an image. 'If ye believe not', *pisteusēte* (G4100, John 8:24, V-AAS-2P), 'that I am he': the Greek has simply *egō* (G1473, John 8:24) *eimi* (G1510, John 8:24), 'I am', and the KJV supplies 'he' to make English sense. The phrase echoes the LORD's words in Isaiah: "Ye are my witnesses, saith the LORD, and my servant whom I have chosen: that ye may know and believe me, and understand that I am he: before me there was no God formed, neither shall there be after me." (Isaiah 43:10) Faith in Jesus as the I AM is not an optional extra for the spiritually advanced. Without it, a person dies in his sins, unforgiven, with no way to the place where he has gone.

**Asks:** The most serious warning Jesus gives in this chapter is not about adultery or any single sin; it is about unbelief in him. Do you believe not only that Jesus was a good teacher, but that he is 'he', the one who is from above?

#### John 8:25–27 · Who Art Thou?
**Says:** "Then said they unto him, Who art thou? And Jesus saith unto them, Even the same that I said unto you from the beginning." (John 8:25) "I have many things to say and to judge of you: but he that sent me is true; and I speak to the world those things which I have heard of him." (John 8:26) "They understood not that he spake to them of the Father." (John 8:27)

**Means:** 'Who art thou?' *Tis* (G5101, John 8:25), 'who?', is the question of the whole Gospel, and it is asked here in exasperation. Jesus' reply is one of the hardest sentences in John to translate. The phrase built on *archēn* (G746, John 8:25), 'the beginning', can be read as the KJV reads it, 'Even the same that I said unto you from the beginning': I am what I have told you all along. Others read it as an exclamation of weariness ('Why do I speak to you at all?'), or as 'Primarily, I am what I tell you'. Modern translations differ, and the grammar can bear more than one reading (verify). The KJV's sense fits the chapter well: Jesus has never hidden who he is; they have not heard.

He has 'many things to say and to judge' about them, but he restrains himself to one task: to speak to the world what he has heard from the Father, who 'is true'. And John adds his own comment, as he often does: 'They understood not that he spake to them of the Father'. Their darkness is not lack of information; it is lack of hearing (John 8:43).

**Asks:** Sometimes the question 'Who are you, Jesus?' is asked in honest seeking, and sometimes in weary defiance. His answer is the same either way: what he has said from the beginning. Have you heard it?

#### John 8:28–30 · When Ye Have Lifted Up the Son of Man
**Says:** "Then said Jesus unto them, When ye have lifted up the Son of man, then shall ye know that I am he, and that I do nothing of myself; but as my Father hath taught me, I speak these things." (John 8:28) "And he that sent me is with me: the Father hath not left me alone; for I do always those things that please him." (John 8:29) "As he spake these words, many believed on him." (John 8:30)

**Means:** 'When ye have lifted up', *hupsōsēte* (G5312, John 8:28, V-AAS-2P), 'the Son of man'. In John, 'lifted up' means the cross and, through it, exaltation: 'as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up' (John 3:14), and 'This he said, signifying what death he should die' (John 12:33). Jesus tells his hearers that they will do the lifting, and that the cross will reveal who he is: 'then shall ye know that I am he', *egō eimi* once more. The cross, which looked like the refutation of his claims, would be their proof. Some of those who heard him came to know it only weeks later, when Peter preached in Jerusalem that God had made 'that same Jesus, whom ye have crucified, both Lord and Christ' (Acts 2:36), and they were 'pricked in their heart' (Acts 2:37).

He speaks only what the Father taught him; the Father who sent him is with him and 'hath not left me alone'; and 'I do always those things that please him'. That is a claim no other man could make, and it matches the question he will ask in John 8:46. As he spoke, 'many believed on him', *episteusan* (G4100, John 8:30). The next verses will test what kind of believing it was.

**Asks:** The cross is where we learn who Jesus is. If you want to understand him, do not start with a list of his teachings; start at the place where he was lifted up.

#### John 8:31–32 · Continue in My Word
**Says:** "Then said Jesus to those Jews which believed on him, If ye continue in my word, then are ye my disciples indeed;" (John 8:31) "And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free." (John 8:32)

**Means:** Jesus turns to 'those Jews which believed on him', *pepisteukotas* (G4100, John 8:31, V-RAP-APM), a perfect participle: they had come to believe, in some sense. He sets before them the mark of real discipleship: 'If ye continue', *meinēte* (G3306, John 8:31, V-AAS-2P), from *menō*, John's great word 'abide' (John 15:4), 'in my word, then are ye my disciples', *mathētai* (G3101, John 8:31), 'indeed', *alēthōs* (G230, John 8:31), 'truly'. A moment of agreement is not yet discipleship. Discipleship is staying in his word: hearing it, trusting it, obeying it, going on with it. John has shown us already that some disciples 'went back, and walked no more with him' (John 6:66), and that others stayed because he has 'the words of eternal life' (John 6:68).

The promise follows: 'ye shall know', *gnōsesthe* (G1097, John 8:32, V-FDI-2P), 'the truth', *alētheian* (G225, John 8:32), 'and the truth shall make you free', *eleutherōsei* (G1659, John 8:32, V-FAI-3S). The STEPBible lexicon defines the verb in the New Testament as 'to make free from sin', listing John 8:32 and 36 with Romans 6:18 and 22, Romans 8:2, and Galatians 5:1. The truth here is not truth in general, still less education, but the truth that Jesus speaks (John 8:40, 45) and that he is: "Jesus saith unto him, I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me." (John 14:6) In John 8:36 the same promise is made with the Son in place of the truth. The truth that frees and the Son who frees are one.

**Asks:** Many people have a moment of believing. Jesus asks for a life of continuing. Are you continuing in his word, reading it, trusting it, and doing it, or did you stop at a moment?

#### John 8:33–36 · The Servant and the Son
**Says:** "They answered him, We be Abraham's seed, and were never in bondage to any man: how sayest thou, Ye shall be made free?" (John 8:33) "Jesus answered them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin." (John 8:34) "And the servant abideth not in the house for ever: but the Son abideth ever." (John 8:35) "If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed." (John 8:36) *We be* means we are; *servant* here means slave.

**Means:** The offer of freedom offends them. 'We be Abraham's seed', *sperma* (G4690, John 8:33), 'and were never in bondage', *dedouleukamen* (G1398, John 8:33, V-RAI-1P), 'to any man'. As history, the claim is astonishing: Israel had been slaves in Egypt, exiles in Babylon, and at that moment lived under Rome. They probably mean that as Abraham's children they had never been inwardly slaves, whoever ruled their land (verify the rabbinic parallels sometimes cited). Their confidence rests entirely on their descent.

Jesus answers with a solemn 'Verily, verily', the double *amēn* (G281, John 8:34), which occurs 3 times in the KJV of this chapter (John 8:34, 51, 58), each time before a word that cuts to the root. 'Whosoever committeth sin', *poiōn* (G4160, John 8:34, V-PAP-NSM), a present participle: the one who goes on doing sin, whose life runs in that channel (compare 1 John 3:8), 'is the servant', *doulos* (G1401, John 8:34), 'of sin'. The STEPBible lexicon defines *doulos* as 'a slave, bond-man', the opposite of one who is free. Not a hired servant who can give notice, but property. Sin is not only something we do; it is a master that owns the one who does it.

Then a picture from household law. 'The servant abideth not in the house for ever: but the Son abideth ever'. The verb both times is *menei* (G3306, John 8:35, V-PAI-3S). A slave has no permanent place in the family; he can be sold or sent away, as the bondwoman's son was sent from Abraham's own tent (Genesis 21:10; Paul draws on the same story in Galatians 4:30). The son belongs to the house for ever, and he has the authority to free the slaves in it. 'If the Son therefore shall make you free', *eleutherōsē* (G1659, John 8:36, V-AAS-3S), 'ye shall be free', *eleutheroi* (G1658, John 8:36), 'indeed', *ontōs* (G3689, John 8:36), which the lexicon glosses 'really, actually, truly'. Their freedom is nominal; his is real.

**Asks:** The deepest slavery is the one we do not admit. Our family, our church, our upbringing, and our good opinion of ourselves cannot free us. Only the Son can. What sin do you keep returning to, and have you asked the Son to make you free from it?

#### John 8:37–41 · Abraham's Seed, Abraham's Children
**Says:** "I know that ye are Abraham's seed; but ye seek to kill me, because my word hath no place in you." (John 8:37) "I speak that which I have seen with my Father: and ye do that which ye have seen with your father." (John 8:38) "They answered and said unto him, Abraham is our father. Jesus saith unto them, If ye were Abraham's children, ye would do the works of Abraham." (John 8:39) "But now ye seek to kill me, a man that hath told you the truth, which I have heard of God: this did not Abraham." (John 8:40) "Ye do the deeds of your father. Then said they to him, We be not born of fornication; we have one Father, even God." (John 8:41)

**Means:** Jesus grants the physical fact: 'ye are Abraham's seed'. But he distinguishes seed from children, *tekna* (G5043, John 8:39). Children share their father's likeness and do his works. And their works are not Abraham's: 'ye seek to kill me, because my word hath no place in you', no room, no welcome. Abraham 'believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness' (Genesis 15:6); he welcomed the messengers God sent him (Genesis 18:1–8); and when God spoke, he obeyed. 'This did not Abraham'. John the Baptist had warned the same crowds: "And think not to say within yourselves, We have Abraham to our father: for I say unto you, that God is able of these stones to raise up children unto Abraham." (Matthew 3:9)

The contrast of fathers is set up in John 8:38: Jesus speaks what he has seen with his Father; they do what they have seen with theirs. Stung, they answer, 'We be not born of fornication; we have one Father, even God'. The prophets had spoken of Israel's idolatry as spiritual harlotry (Hosea 1:2), and they insist they are God's legitimate children. Some commentators hear in their emphatic 'We' a sneer at the circumstances of Jesus' own birth, a slander that appears in later anti-Christian writing (Origen answers such a charge in *Against Celsus*, book 1, verify); the text does not say so, and the suggestion should be offered as possible, not certain.

**Asks:** Spiritual privilege is real, but it is not the same as spiritual life. A Christian home, a baptism, a church membership, and a Bible on the shelf are good gifts; none of them is faith. Whose works are you doing?

#### John 8:42–45 · Your Father the Devil
**Says:** "Jesus said unto them, If God were your Father, ye would love me: for I proceeded forth and came from God; neither came I of myself, but he sent me." (John 8:42) "Why do ye not understand my speech? even because ye cannot hear my word." (John 8:43) "Ye are of your father the devil, and the lusts of your father ye will do. He was a murderer from the beginning, and abode not in the truth, because there is no truth in him. When he speaketh a lie, he speaketh of his own: for he is a liar, and the father of it." (John 8:44) "And because I tell you the truth, ye believe me not." (John 8:45) *Lusts* means desires.

**Means:** The test of whether God is your Father is simple: 'ye would love me'. God's children love God's Son, because 'I proceeded forth and came from God', *exēlthon* (G1831, John 8:42). Then Jesus diagnoses their deafness. 'Why do ye not understand my speech', *lalian* (G2981, John 8:43), his way of speaking, 'even because ye cannot hear my word', *logon* (G3056, John 8:43), his message? They cannot follow his words because they will not receive his message. The inability is real, and it is moral, not intellectual.

Then the hardest sentence in the chapter: 'Ye are of your father the devil', *diabolou* (G1228, John 8:44). 'And the lusts', *epithumias* (G1939, John 8:44), desires, 'of your father ye will do', *thelete* (G2309, John 8:44, V-PAI-2P), which means 'you want to do': the family likeness is in the will. Jesus names two marks of the devil. He was 'a murderer', *anthrōpoktonos* (G443, John 8:44), a man-killer, 'from the beginning', when his lie brought death into the world (Genesis 3:4; Romans 5:12) and when Cain, 'who was of that wicked one', killed his brother (1 John 3:12). And he is 'a liar', *pseustēs* (G5583, John 8:44), 'and the father of it', with 'no truth in him'. The crowd shows both marks: they seek to kill Jesus (John 8:37, 40), and they will not receive the truth (John 8:45).

This is not a statement about the Jewish people. It is spoken to particular men who are seeking to kill him, and it is the same diagnosis Scripture makes of every human being apart from grace, Jew or Gentile: we 'were by nature the children of wrath, even as others' (Ephesians 2:3), and 'In this the children of God are manifest, and the children of the devil' (1 John 3:10). Jesus said 'Get thee behind me, Satan' to Peter himself (Matthew 16:23). And the tragedy is in John 8:45: 'because I tell you the truth, ye believe me not'. Sinful hearts reject Christ not in spite of his truth but because of it.

**Asks:** Every one of us was born into the wrong family, and the family likeness shows in what we want and in how we handle the truth. The only way out is a new birth into God's family (John 1:12–13; 3:3). Where do you still see the old family likeness in your desires, or in how you treat the truth?

#### John 8:46–47 · Which of You Convinceth Me of Sin?
**Says:** "Which of you convinceth me of sin? And if I say the truth, why do ye not believe me?" (John 8:46) "He that is of God heareth God's words: ye therefore hear them not, because ye are not of God." (John 8:47) *Convinceth* means proves guilty, convicts.

**Means:** 'Convinceth' is *elegchei* (G1651, John 8:46, V-PAI-3S), the same verb as in John 8:9. That morning the accusers were convicted by their own consciences, one by one. Now Jesus invites the whole crowd to convict him, and no one can. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 8:46 under the sense 'to convict'. It is an open, public claim to be without sin, made to enemies who were looking for any fault, and it stands with his earlier word that 'no unrighteousness is in him' (John 7:18) and with the apostles' witness: "Who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth:" (1 Peter 2:22) The verb occurs 4 times in the Greek of John (John 3:20; 8:9, 46; 16:8): light convicts evil deeds, conscience convicts the accusers, no one convicts Jesus, and the Spirit will convict the world.

Verse 47 draws the conclusion. Children hear their father's voice. 'He that is of God heareth God's words'. Later Jesus will say it of his sheep: 'My sheep hear my voice' (John 10:27). Not hearing is the sign of not belonging.

**Asks:** The sinless Christ is the only safe place for sinners, because he has no sin of his own to answer for. And the sign that we belong to God is that we hear his words, gladly, even when they convict us.

#### John 8:48–50 · A Samaritan, and Hast a Devil
**Says:** "Then answered the Jews, and said unto him, Say we not well that thou art a Samaritan, and hast a devil?" (John 8:48) "Jesus answered, I have not a devil; but I honour my Father, and ye do dishonour me." (John 8:49) "And I seek not mine own glory: there is one that seeketh and judgeth." (John 8:50)

**Means:** Out of arguments, they reach for insults. 'A Samaritan', *Samaritēs* (G4541, John 8:48): to a Judean, a Samaritan was a half-breed and a heretic, and 'the Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans' (John 4:9). And he 'hast a devil', *daimonion* (G1140, John 8:48), a demon: he is mad or possessed. 'Say we not well' means 'Aren't we right to say so?', as they had said before (John 7:20).

Jesus lets the first insult pass. He had recently been welcomed as 'the Saviour of the world' by a Samaritan town (John 4:42), and he does not treat the name as a disgrace. He denies the second, because it slanders the source of his words: 'I have not a devil; but I honour my Father'. He does not return insult for insult (1 Peter 2:23), and he does not defend his own reputation: 'I seek not mine own glory: there is one that seeketh and judgeth'. The Father will vindicate the Son.

**Asks:** When we are insulted for our faith, the temptation is to defend ourselves, or to insult back. Jesus simply told the truth, honoured his Father, and left his reputation in the Father's hands. What would it look like for you to do the same?

#### John 8:51–53 · He Shall Never See Death
**Says:** "Verily, verily, I say unto you, If a man keep my saying, he shall never see death." (John 8:51) "Then said the Jews unto him, Now we know that thou hast a devil. Abraham is dead, and the prophets; and thou sayest, If a man keep my saying, he shall never taste of death." (John 8:52) "Art thou greater than our father Abraham, which is dead? and the prophets are dead: whom makest thou thyself?" (John 8:53)

**Means:** Into the storm of insult Jesus speaks a promise. 'If a man keep', *tērēsē* (G5083, John 8:51, V-AAS-3S), guard and obey, 'my saying, he shall never see death'. He does not mean that believers will not die; he will say at Lazarus' grave, 'though he were dead, yet shall he live' (John 11:25). He means that they will never experience death as its full reality: separation from God, condemnation, the end. The believer 'shall not come into condemnation; but is passed from death unto life' (John 5:24).

They hear only an impossible boast. 'Now we know that thou hast a devil'. They repeat his words with a small change, 'never taste of death', *geusetai* (G1089, John 8:52), a Hebrew idiom for dying. 'Abraham is dead, and the prophets'; 'Art thou greater than our father Abraham', they ask. It is the same question the Samaritan woman asked about Jacob (John 4:12), and in both places John expects his readers to answer yes. 'Whom makest thou thyself?' is the charge that runs through the Gospel: that he makes himself equal with God (John 5:18; 10:33; 19:7). The truth is the opposite: he is not making himself anything; he is.

**Asks:** Every human being faces death, and every philosophy has to say something about it. Jesus says that the one who keeps his word will never see it. Do you believe him, and does it change how you face your own death, and the deaths of those you love?

#### John 8:54–56 · Abraham Rejoiced to See My Day
**Says:** "Jesus answered, If I honour myself, my honour is nothing: it is my Father that honoureth me; of whom ye say, that he is your God:" (John 8:54) "Yet ye have not known him; but I know him: and if I should say, I know him not, I shall be a liar like unto you: but I know him, and keep his saying." (John 8:55) "Your father Abraham rejoiced to see my day: and he saw it, and was glad." (John 8:56)

**Means:** Jesus refuses to glorify himself: 'If I honour myself, my honour is nothing'. The Father honours him, the Father they claim as their God and do not know. And he refuses false modesty just as firmly: to say 'I know him not' would make him 'a liar like unto you'. He knows the Father and keeps his word, as the Son always does (John 8:29).

Then he answers their question about Abraham. 'Your father Abraham rejoiced', *ēgalliasato* (G21, John 8:56), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'to exult, rejoice greatly', 'to see my day: and he saw it, and was glad', *echarē* (G5463, John 8:56). When did Abraham see Christ's day? Faithful readers answer in several ways. Many point to Genesis 22, where Abraham told Isaac, "My son, God will provide himself a lamb for a burnt offering" (Genesis 22:8), received Isaac back 'in a figure' from the dead (Hebrews 11:19), and named the place for the LORD who provides (Genesis 22:14). Others point to the promise that in Abraham 'shall all families of the earth be blessed' (Genesis 12:3), or to the birth of Isaac, whose name recalls laughter (Genesis 17:17; 21:6). Others think of Abraham in God's presence seeing the day of Christ's coming from there. Hebrews unites the first two: the patriarchs saw the promises 'afar off' and embraced them (Hebrews 11:13). The text does not settle the moment. It does settle the point: Abraham, whom they claimed as father, looked forward to Jesus with joy.

**Asks:** The whole Old Testament looks forward to Christ, and the faithful of every age rejoiced to see his day. We see it far more clearly than Abraham did. Is your faith in him marked by Abraham's gladness?

#### John 8:57–59 · Before Abraham Was, I Am
**Says:** "Then said the Jews unto him, Thou art not yet fifty years old, and hast thou seen Abraham?" (John 8:57) "Jesus said unto them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Before Abraham was, I am." (John 8:58) "Then took they up stones to cast at him: but Jesus hid himself, and went out of the temple, going through the midst of them, and so passed by." (John 8:59)

**Means:** 'Not yet fifty' is a round number for a man in the prime of life, not old enough to be an elder, let alone a contemporary of Abraham. It does not tell us Jesus' age; Luke says he began his ministry at 'about thirty years of age' (Luke 3:23). They twist his words: he had said that Abraham saw his day; they ask whether he has seen Abraham.

Then the climax of the chapter, and one of the highest claims in the Bible. 'Verily, verily, I say unto you, Before Abraham was', *prin* (G4250, John 8:58) Abraham *genesthai* (G1096, John 8:58, V-2ADN), 'before Abraham came to be', an aorist infinitive of the verb of becoming, 'I am', *egō* (G1473, John 8:58) *eimi* (G1510, John 8:58, V-PAI-1S), a present of the verb 'to be'. Jesus does not say *before Abraham was, I was*, which would claim only that he is very old. He sets Abraham's coming-into-being against his own simple being. Abraham began; Jesus is. The words recall the name God gave Moses at the burning bush: "And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM: and he said, Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, I AM hath sent me unto you." (Exodus 3:14) They also recall the 'I am he' of Isaiah (Isaiah 41:4; 43:10), which the Greek Old Testament renders with the same two words, *egō eimi* (a mainstream observation; verify the particular passages). The phrase 'I am he' occurs 7 times in the KJV of John (John 8:24, 28; 9:9; 13:19; 18:5, 6, 8); once it is the healed blind man's ordinary 'That's me' (John 9:9), and in John 18:6 the soldiers fall to the ground at it.

The crowd understood him. 'Then took they up stones', *lithous* (G3037, John 8:59), the penalty the law set for blasphemy: "And he that blasphemeth the name of the LORD, he shall surely be put to death, and all the congregation shall certainly stone him: as well the stranger, as he that is born in the land, when he blasphemeth the name of the LORD, shall be put to death." (Leviticus 24:16) Later they will say it outright: 'thou, being a man, makest thyself God' (John 10:33). The chapter that began with a woman about to be stoned ends with the sinless Son about to be stoned, by the same crowd's leaders. 'But Jesus hid himself', *ekrubē* (G2928, John 8:59, V-2API-3S), a passive form that can mean he was hidden or that he withdrew from sight, 'and went out of the temple, going through the midst of them, and so passed by'. The last clause stands in the Textus Receptus and is lacking in many other manuscripts (verify); it recalls Luke's account at Nazareth, "But he passing through the midst of them went his way," (Luke 4:30). His hour had not yet come. And the I AM walks out of his own temple, unrecognised by those who served in it.

**Asks:** Jesus' claim leaves only two responses: take up stones, or fall down and worship. There is no room for 'a good teacher' and nothing more. Which response is yours, and does your life show it?

### 🧨 Cultural Landmines and Misconceptions
**The first stone as a motto of relativism.** The saying has passed into everyday speech as a way of saying that no one may ever call anything wrong. But Jesus spoke it to hypocritical accusers who were using the law as a trap, and in the same scene he told the guilty woman to 'sin no more' (John 8:11). He did not deny that adultery is sin; he denied that the accusers could condemn it while hiding their own. The first stone saying humbles the judge; it does not abolish judgment.

**'The truth shall make you free' carved over doorways.** John 8:32 is inscribed on university buildings, libraries, and government offices (the lobby of the headquarters of the United States Central Intelligence Agency is a well-known example, verify). Detached from John 8:31 and 8:36, it becomes a motto for learning or for intelligence gathering. In John the truth is the word of Jesus, and the freedom is freedom from sin, and the one who frees is the Son.

**'Ye are of your father the devil' and the Jewish people.** John 8:44 has been quoted through the centuries, to the church's shame, to justify contempt and violence against Jews. That reading is a grave misuse. Jesus spoke to particular men in the temple who were seeking to kill him (John 8:37, 40); he and his disciples were Jews, and he said elsewhere that 'salvation is of the Jews' (John 4:22). The Bible makes the same diagnosis of every human being apart from grace, Gentile and Jew alike (Ephesians 2:2–3; 1 John 3:10). The Council's third question treats this at length.

**The woman as Mary Magdalene, or as a prostitute.** Popular art and some later tradition have identified the woman taken in adultery with Mary Magdalene, or pictured her as a prostitute (verify the history of the identification). John gives her no name and says only that she was taken in adultery. Mary Magdalene is introduced elsewhere as a woman from whom seven devils had gone out (Luke 8:2), and nothing in Scripture connects the two.

**'I am' as ordinary English.** In everyday English 'I am' is the most ordinary of phrases, and 'I am he' sounds like 'That's me'. Sometimes in John it means just that (John 9:9, where the healed man says it of himself). But when Jesus says it absolutely, and at the end says 'Before Abraham was, I am', his hearers heard the name of God, and reached for stones (John 8:59). A modern reader who hears only grammar misses what the crowd heard.

**Adultery as a private matter.** Our culture tends to treat adultery as a private choice between adults, or as a tragedy that simply happens. Scripture treats it as the breaking of a covenant made before God (Malachi 2:14), a sin against a spouse, a family, and the Lord (Psalm 51:4). John 8 is not soft on adultery; it is merciful to an adulteress, which is a very different thing.

## 🔴 Tier 3 — Expert
### 🏗️ Theological Framework
John 8 is the most concentrated controversy in the Gospel, and read through the Reformed conviction that Scripture interprets Scripture, it bears on six areas of systematic theology.

*The deity of the Son and the doctrine of God.* The absolute 'I am' sayings (John 8:24, 28, 58) place Jesus within the identity of the one God who revealed his name at the bush (Exodus 3:14) and declared through Isaiah, 'I am he' (Isaiah 43:10). At the same time the chapter distinguishes the Son from the Father who sent him, bears witness to him, teaches him, and glorifies him (John 8:16, 18, 28, 54). The Son is not the Father, and the Son is God. That is the shape of the doctrine of the Trinity as the church confessed it at Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381), which taught that the Son is of one substance with the Father (summarized, not quoted); the Council of Chalcedon (451) later took up a different question, how the one Christ is truly God and truly man. John 8 is one of the chief biblical foundations of the Trinitarian confession: the Son eternally is, while Abraham came to be (John 8:58; compare John 1:1–3; 17:5).

*Christ as the light: revelation and illumination.* 'I am the light of the world' (John 8:12) teaches that God is known savingly only in Christ (John 1:18; 14:9), and that the knowledge of God is not merely information but life: 'the light of life'. Reformed theology distinguishes the light of general revelation, which leaves all people without excuse (Romans 1:20), from the saving light of Christ, which the Spirit gives by opening blind eyes (2 Corinthians 4:4–6). John 8 shows the second clearly: the crowd has the light standing in front of them and cannot see, because they love darkness (John 3:19; 8:43).

*The bondage of the will.* 'Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin' (John 8:34); 'ye cannot hear my word' (John 8:43); 'the lusts of your father ye will do' (John 8:44). The chapter is a primary text for the Reformed doctrine that fallen man, though he acts willingly and is responsible for what he does, is not free to choose God: his will is bound by what he loves. Luther built his argument in *The Bondage of the Will* (1525) on texts of this kind (verify the particular use of John 8), and the Westminster Confession teaches that fallen man has wholly lost all ability of will to any spiritual good accompanying salvation (chapter 9, section 3, paraphrased, verify). The chapter's 'cannot' is moral, not physical: they cannot because they will not, and they will not because they are 'not of God' (John 8:47).

*Freedom in Christ.* Against the bondage stands the freedom that only the Son gives (John 8:36). The Reformers taught that this freedom has several parts: freedom from the guilt of sin and the condemnation of the law (Romans 8:1), freedom from the dominion of sin (Romans 6:14, 18), and freedom of conscience from the commandments of men (Westminster Confession, chapter 20, paraphrased, verify). It is not freedom to sin, which is only the old slavery (Galatians 5:13).

*Law and gospel.* John 8:1–11 is a small drama of law and gospel. The law is not set aside: the adulteress is guilty, and the accusers are convicted by the law's own demand that the witnesses be first (Deuteronomy 17:7). The law exposes everyone in the courtyard. Then the gospel speaks, from the only one who has kept the law: no condemnation, and a new life. The law then returns in its third use, as a rule of life for the forgiven: 'go, and sin no more' (John 8:11). Calvin and the Reformed tradition call this the principal use of the law for believers (*Institutes* 2.7.12, verify).

*The true children of Abraham and the covenant.* 'If ye were Abraham's children, ye would do the works of Abraham' (John 8:39). The chapter distinguishes natural descent from spiritual kinship, as Paul does: "Know ye therefore that they which are of faith, the same are the children of Abraham." (Galatians 3:7) Reformed covenant theology reads this not as God abandoning Israel but as the promise to Abraham always having been received by faith (Romans 4:11–12; 9:6–8), and fulfilled in Christ, Abraham's true seed (Galatians 3:16, 29). Abraham's joy at Christ's day (John 8:56) shows that the covenant of grace is one in substance in both Testaments.

### ⚔️ Debate Corner
**The question:** What does Jesus claim when he says, "Jesus said unto them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Before Abraham was, I am." (John 8:58)?

**Position 1 · A claim to exist before Abraham as a created being, not to the divine name.** The Watch Tower Society (Jehovah's Witnesses) renders the verse in the New World Translation with a past tense, 'I have been' (verify the current wording). Its scholars argue that a Greek present tense joined to an expression of past time can describe an action begun in the past and continuing into the present, and so should be translated with an English perfect (they call it a progressive present, or a present of past action still in progress). They note that the Greek Old Testament of Exodus 3:14 renders God's name with a participle, 'the one who is', not with *egō eimi* alone, so that the link to the burning bush is weaker than often claimed (verify). On this view Jesus claims to have existed before Abraham as God's firstborn creature, the one through whom God made all else, but not to be God. Holders of this view want to guard the oneness of God and the supremacy of the Father (John 14:28).

**Position 2 · A claim to pre-existence in God's plan.** Socinians in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries (verify), and some modern unitarian and liberal interpreters, read the verse as a claim that Jesus existed before Abraham in God's purpose and foreknowledge, as the Lamb 'slain from the foundation of the world' (Revelation 13:8), not personally. Abraham saw his day by faith in the promise (John 8:56); in the same way Jesus was 'before' Abraham as the goal of God's plan. Holders of this view want to take Jesus' full humanity seriously, and to avoid what they see as a later Greek metaphysics read back into a Jewish text.

**Position 3 · A claim to eternal, personal, divine existence, in the language of God's name.** The historic church, including the Reformed tradition (Calvin on John 8:58, verify; the Westminster Confession, chapter 8, section 2, paraphrased, verify), reads the verse as Jesus claiming the eternal being that belongs to God alone, and identifying himself with the I AM who spoke to Moses and through Isaiah. Abraham 'came to be'; Jesus simply 'is'.

**Most consistent with Reformed hermeneutics.** Scripture interpreting Scripture favours Position 3. (1) *The contrast of verbs.* John sets *genesthai* (G1096, John 8:58, V-2ADN), 'came to be', against *eimi* (G1510, John 8:58, V-PAI-1S), 'am', exactly as his prologue sets the Word who 'was' in the beginning against all things that 'were made' (John 1:1–3). Even if the present tense can sometimes carry an extended past sense, the contrast is between becoming and being, not between younger and older. (2) *The absolute 'I am' in this chapter.* Jesus has already said, with no predicate, 'if ye believe not that I am he' (John 8:24) and 'then shall ye know that I am he' (John 8:28), in words that echo Isaiah's 'that ye may know and believe me, and understand that I am he' (Isaiah 43:10); John 8:58 is the climax of a sequence, not an isolated grammatical puzzle. Whatever the wording of Exodus 3:14 in the Greek Old Testament, Isaiah's usage supplies the background plainly. (3) *The hearers' response.* They took up stones (John 8:59), the penalty for blasphemy (Leviticus 24:16), and in the parallel scene they explain: 'thou, being a man, makest thyself God' (John 10:33). A claim to be a very old creature, or to exist in God's plan, would have been strange, but it would not have been stoned as blasphemy. (4) *The rest of John.* The Son had glory with the Father 'before the world was' (John 17:5), which is personal and not merely planned; and the Word who was with God 'was God' (John 1:1). (5) *Against Position 2 in particular*, Jesus does not say that God planned him before Abraham, but that he himself is; and John 8:56 says that Abraham saw Christ's day, not that Christ existed in Abraham's hope.

Position 1 is right that the grammar alone should not carry the whole weight, and the Reformed reading does not rest on grammar alone. Position 2 is right to insist on Jesus' true humanity; the same chapter calls him 'a man that hath told you the truth' (John 8:40). But John holds both: the man who was not yet fifty is the I AM.

**Pastoral Warning:** This debate reaches into ordinary church life, because members meet Jehovah's Witnesses at their doors and unitarian ideas in popular books. A church can fail in two directions. It can use John 8:58 as a weapon, winning arguments with visitors and treating them with contempt, and so forget that the one who said 'I am' also said 'Neither do I condemn thee'. Or it can leave its members unable to say who Jesus is, so that they are unsettled by the first confident visitor with a different translation. Teach the verse plainly and patiently, show the whole chapter's sequence (John 8:24, 28, 58), and model gentleness with those who disagree (1 Peter 3:15). And do not let the argument replace the worship: the right response to 'I am' is not only to be correct about it, but to fall down before him, as Thomas did (John 20:28).

### 🔤 Original Language Deep Dive
Every Greek and Hebrew definition below comes from one source: the brief lexicon published by STEPBible.org (Tyndale House, Cambridge), whose Greek entries are drawn largely from G. Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament*. It is called 'the STEPBible lexicon' below.

**Taken in the very act.** *Kateilēmmenēn* (G2638, John 8:3, V-RPP-ASF) is a perfect passive participle of *katalambanō*, and *kateilēphthē* (G2638, John 8:4, V-2API-3S) is its aorist passive. The STEPBible lexicon gives the verb three senses: to lay hold of, seize; to overtake, of the darkness in John 1:5 and 12:35, and 'hence, to surprise, discover', citing John 8:3–4; and, of the mind, to apprehend or comprehend. So the same verb that John used of the darkness that did not overtake the light is used here of a woman overtaken in her sin. *Autophōrō* (G1888, John 8:4, A-DSM), 'in the very act': the lexicon explains the word as formed from a word for a thief, used properly of theft and then generally of being caught in the act, and notes that the Textus Receptus reads it as one compound word. The evidence was not in dispute.

**Without sin, and the conscience.** *Anamartētos* (G361, John 8:7, A-NSM), from the negative prefix and the verb 'to sin': the STEPBible lexicon gives the classical sense 'without missing, unerring' and the moral sense 'faultless, without sin', and lists John 8:7 as its only New Testament use. *Suneidēseōs* (G4893, John 8:9, N-GSF), 'conscience', which the lexicon defines as consciousness, and in the ethical sense 'innate discernment, self-judging consciousness, conscience', listing many uses in Paul, Hebrews, and Peter but no other in the Gospels. *Elegchomenoi* (G1651, John 8:9, V-PPP-NPM), from *elegchō*, which the lexicon defines as to convict, to reprove, and to expose, and for which it gives John 8:46 under 'to convict'. The verb occurs 4 times in the Greek of John: light exposes evil deeds (John 3:20); conscience convicts the accusers (John 8:9); no one can convict Jesus (John 8:46); and the Spirit will convict the world (John 16:8).

**Condemn.** *Katakrinō* (G2632, John 8:11, V-PAI-1S), a compound of *krinō*, 'to judge', with *kata*, 'against': to give judgment against, to condemn. It is present tense. Jesus does not say 'I have not condemned you' or 'I will not condemn you' but 'I do not condemn you'. The command that follows, *hamartane* (G264, John 8:11, V-PAM-2S), is a present imperative with *mēketi* (G3371, John 8:11), 'no longer': the natural sense is 'stop going on in sin', a call to a changed way of life, not merely to avoid one act.

**Light.** *Phōs* (G5457, John 8:12, N-NSN). The STEPBible lexicon defines it as light, 'opposite to τὸ σκότος, ἡ σκοτία', cites 2 Corinthians 4:6 among its first examples, and notes that in the Greek Old Testament it chiefly renders the Hebrew *or* (H216), the light of Genesis 1:3. The word 'light' occurs 24 times in the KJV of John, 2 of them in John 8:12, and the light theme runs from the prologue (John 1:4–9) through the man born blind (John 9:5) to Jesus' last public words (John 12:35–36, 46). The word *kosmou* (G2889, John 8:12), 'of the world', reaches beyond Israel, as the Servant was to be 'a light to the Gentiles' (Isaiah 49:6). *Akolouthōn* (G190, John 8:12, V-PAP-NSM), 'the one following', is a present participle, and *peripatēsei* (G4043, John 8:12, V-FAI-3S), 'shall walk', a future after the double negative.

**Witness.** *Martureis* (G3140, John 8:13, V-PAI-2S), 'you bear witness', and *marturia* (G3141, John 8:13, N-NSF), 'testimony, record'. Witness is one of John's key words; the whole of John 5:31–47 is built on it, and John 8:13–18 returns to the same legal setting. The KJV varies its English, 'record' in John 8:13–14 and 'witness' and 'testimony' in John 8:17–18, for the same family of Greek words.

**Truth and freedom.** *Alētheian* (G225, John 8:32, N-ASF), 'truth', and the related *alēthōs* (G230, John 8:31), 'truly, indeed'. 'Truth' occurs 7 times in the KJV of John 8, and the chapter sets the truth Jesus speaks against the lie the devil speaks (John 8:40, 44–45). *Eleutherōsei* (G1659, John 8:32, V-FAI-3S), 'shall make free', a future, and *eleutherōsē* (G1659, John 8:36, V-AAS-3S), an aorist subjunctive. The verb occurs 7 times in the Greek New Testament; the STEPBible lexicon defines it as 'to make free from sin' and lists every one of its New Testament uses in that sense (John 8:32, 36; Romans 6:18, 22; 8:2, 21; Galatians 5:1). The adjective is *eleutheroi* (G1658, John 8:36, A-NPM). The word 'free' occurs 4 times in the KJV of John 8. *Ontōs* (G3689, John 8:36, ADV), 'really, actually', built on the participle of the verb 'to be': the freedom the Son gives has real being, where the crowd's freedom was only a claim.

**Slave and household.** *Dedouleukamen* (G1398, John 8:33, V-RAI-1P), a perfect: 'we have never been in bondage'. *Doulos* (G1401, John 8:34, N-NSM), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as a slave, bond-man, 'opposite to ἐλεύθερος', and which in the Greek Old Testament nearly always renders the Hebrew *eved* (H5650), servant or slave. *Poiōn* (G4160, John 8:34, V-PAP-NSM), 'doing', a present participle: the one whose practice is sin. *Menei* (G3306, John 8:35, V-PAI-3S), 'abides', twice in one verse: the slave does not abide, the son abides for ever. It is the same verb as *meinēte* (G3306, John 8:31, V-AAS-2P), 'continue': the disciple who abides in Christ's word shares the Son's permanent place in the house.

**Seed, children, and fathers.** *Sperma* (G4690, John 8:33, N-NSN), 'seed', descent; *tekna* (G5043, John 8:39, N-NPN), 'children', which carries the idea of family likeness. Jesus grants the first and denies the second (John 8:37, 39). The word 'father' occurs 21 times in the KJV of John 8, and the chapter can be read as an argument about fathers: the Father who sent Jesus, Abraham, God, and the devil. The name Abraham occurs 11 times in the KJV of John, and 11 times in John 8: every mention of Abraham in the Gospel is in this chapter.

**Speech, word, and the devil.** *Lalian* (G2981, John 8:43, N-ASF), 'speech', the manner of speaking, and *logon* (G3056, John 8:43, N-ASM), 'word', the message. *Diabolou* (G1228, John 8:44, A-GSM), 'the devil', literally a slanderer or accuser, which is darkly fitting in a chapter that began with accusers. *Epithumias* (G1939, John 8:44, N-APF), 'desires, lusts'. *Thelete* (G2309, John 8:44, V-PAI-2P), 'you will, you want': the KJV's 'ye will do' is not a simple future but a statement of desire. *Anthrōpoktonos* (G443, John 8:44, N-NSM), 'man-slayer', which occurs 3 times in the Greek New Testament: here and twice in 1 John 3:15, where whoever hates his brother is called a murderer. *Pseustēs* (G5583, John 8:44, N-NSM), 'liar'.

**I am.** The absolute *egō eimi* appears three times in the chapter: *egō* (G1473, John 8:24, P-1NS) *eimi* (G1510, John 8:24, V-PAI-1S); again in John 8:28; and in *egō* (G1473, John 8:58, P-1NS) *eimi* (G1510, John 8:58, V-PAI-1S), where it stands against *genesthai* (G1096, John 8:58, V-2ADN). In John 8:12 the same two words introduce a predicate, 'the light of the world'. Behind the absolute use lies the Hebrew of Isaiah's 'I am he', *ani* (H589) *hu* (H1931), the pronoun 'I', which the STEPBible lexicon says is usually emphatic, and the pronoun 'he', which it lists as used 'with emphasis'. The divine name of Exodus 3:14 is built on the verb *hayah* (H1961), which the lexicon defines as 'to be, become, come to pass, exist'. The Greek Old Testament renders Isaiah's 'I am he' with *egō eimi* (Isaiah 43:10, and elsewhere in Isaiah 41–48, verify), and renders the name of Exodus 3:14 as 'I am the one who is' (verify). Readers who hear Exodus 3:14 in John 8:58 are hearing the whole Old Testament revelation of the name, of which Isaiah is the richest development.

**Lifted up.** *Hupsōsēte* (G5312, John 8:28, V-AAS-2P), an aorist subjunctive after 'when', from *hupsoō*, to lift up, exalt. John uses it for the serpent in the wilderness (John 3:14), for the Son of man lifted up (John 8:28; 12:32, 34), and John explains it of the manner of his death (John 12:33). The one word joins crucifixion and exaltation.

**Rejoiced.** *Ēgalliasato* (G21, John 8:56, V-ADI-3S), from *agalliaō*, which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'to exult, rejoice greatly', noting that in the Greek Old Testament it appears most often in the Psalms. It is joined with *echarē* (G5463, John 8:56), 'was glad'. Abraham's response to Christ's day was exultation, the opposite of the crowd's response to Christ himself.

**Textual notes.** (1) *John 7:53–8:11.* The KJV prints the account of the woman taken in adultery, and this study, following the owner's decision, teaches the text the KJV prints. The facts, as textual scholars report them (verify each against a standard textual commentary): the passage is absent from the earliest surviving Greek manuscripts of John, including P66 and P75 (about AD 200) and Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus (fourth century); it is present in Codex Bezae (about AD 400) and in the great majority of later Greek manuscripts; in some manuscripts it is marked with signs of doubt, and in others it stands after John 7:36, after John 7:44, after John 21:25, or after Luke 21:38; Jerome included it in the Latin Vulgate; several early Greek commentators on John, Chrysostom among them, do not comment on it; and the story, or one like it, seems to have been known early, since Eusebius reports that Papias told of a woman accused before the Lord of many sins (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39, verify), and a third-century church order, the *Didascalia Apostolorum*, appears to allude to it (verify). Scholars who doubt that John wrote it point to vocabulary and style they judge unusual for him (for example 'scribes and Pharisees', a pairing frequent in the other Gospels and found only here in John, verify) and to the way it interrupts John 7:52 and 8:12. Scholars who defend it point to its wide attestation in the later manuscripts and the Latin tradition, to Augustine's report that some removed it for fear of its seeming leniency, and to features that fit the setting at the close of the feast. Many who doubt its place in John believe the story is old and true. No doctrine rests on it alone: the holiness of the law, Christ's mercy to sinners, and his call to 'sin no more' are taught elsewhere (John 5:14; Romans 8:1). (2) *John 8:9.* The clause 'being convicted by their own conscience' stands in the Textus Receptus and is absent from some manuscripts that contain the passage (verify). (3) *John 8:59.* The words 'going through the midst of them, and so passed by' stand in the Textus Receptus and are absent from many early manuscripts (verify); many modern editions end the verse at 'went out of the temple'. These differences should be explained calmly, without scorn for either side, and none of them changes any doctrine.

### 🚷 Preaching Mistakes to Avoid
- **Information without life:** a sermon that spends its time on the manuscripts of John 7:53–8:11, the grammar of *egō eimi*, and the location of the treasury, and never brings the hearers to stand where the woman stood, to hear 'Neither do I condemn thee', and to follow the light.
- **Heat without light:** a sermon that thunders against 'the Pharisees', against adulterers, or against 'the children of the devil' out there, and never lets the question 'He that is without sin among you' fall on the preacher and the congregation.
- **Mercy without holiness:** preaching 'Neither do I condemn thee' and leaving out 'go, and sin no more', so that John 8 becomes a charter for tolerance of sin.
- **Holiness without mercy:** preaching 'sin no more' as the condition of forgiveness, or reading the woman's story as a warning to adulterers only, so that the mercy of Christ becomes a reward for reform.
- **Freedom without the Son:** preaching John 8:32 as a word about knowledge, self-discovery, or political liberty, and not about the Son who sets slaves of sin free (John 8:36).
- **Using John 8:44 against Jewish people:** any application of 'Ye are of your father the devil' to an ethnic group is a betrayal of the text and of the Jewish Messiah who spoke it.
- **Grammar instead of worship:** preaching John 8:58 as a debating point against other groups, and forgetting that the right response to the I AM is worship.

## 🟣 Tier 4 — Christocentric, Humility, Life & Light
### 👑 Every Answer in Jesus
John 8 demolishes every ground of self-glory. Moral superiority is no ground: the accusers came with stones and left one by one, convicted by their own consciences (John 8:9). Religious knowledge is no ground: the scribes knew what Moses commanded and used it as a trap (John 8:5–6). Heritage is no ground: 'We be Abraham's seed' did not keep them from being slaves (John 8:33–34). Claiming God is no ground: 'we have one Father, even God', they said, and sought to kill his Son (John 8:40–41). Even believing is no ground if it does not continue in his word (John 8:30–31, 59). And the chapter leaves no ground for despair either: the woman, whose sin was public and proved, heard the only words that could save her from the only one entitled to condemn her.

And every good thing the chapter holds is found in him. Light is in him: 'I am the light of the world' (John 8:12). The true witness is in him (John 8:14). The Father is known in him (John 8:19). Truth is in him, and freedom is his to give (John 8:32, 36). He is without sin (John 8:46). Life beyond death is in his word (John 8:51). The hope of Abraham was his day (John 8:56). And eternal being is his own (John 8:58). This is what Paul means when he says of Christ, "In whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." (Colossians 2:3) The chapter humbles us by showing that we were the accusers and the accused, slaves who thought ourselves free, children with the wrong family likeness. And it makes Christ glorious by showing the eternal I AM stooping in the dust of a courtyard, writing with the finger that wrote the law, refusing to condemn a sinner, and walking on toward the day when he would be lifted up.

## 📚 Doctrine
### Theology Proper · The Father Who Sends, Witnesses, and Glorifies
**Proposition:** God the Father is true. He sent the Son, bears witness to him, is always with him, teaches him, seeks his glory, and judges. He is known only in the Son, so that to claim God as Father while rejecting the Son is not to know God at all.

**Textual Anchor:** "I am one that bear witness of myself, and the Father that sent me beareth witness of me." (John 8:18) "And he that sent me is with me: the Father hath not left me alone; for I do always those things that please him." (John 8:29) 'it is my Father that honoureth me; of whom ye say, that he is your God' (John 8:54), with the warning 'if ye had known me, ye should have known my Father also' (John 8:19).

**Cross-References:** "No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him." (John 1:18); "Behold, what manner of love the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called the sons of God: therefore the world knoweth us not, because it knew him not." (1 John 3:1)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *People can know and love God truly while rejecting Jesus.* Religious pluralists hold this, wanting to honour sincere worshippers of every faith. The crowd in John 8 was sincere in claiming 'one Father, even God' (John 8:41), and Jesus answers, 'If God were your Father, ye would love me' (John 8:42). (2) *The Father and the Son are one person in two modes.* Oneness Pentecostals teach this, wanting to guard the oneness of God against any hint of three gods. But Jesus counts the Father and himself as two witnesses under the law (John 8:17–18), speaks of the Father who sent him and is with him (John 8:29), and distinguishes his honouring himself from the Father's honouring him (John 8:54). (3) *God has left the world to run by itself.* Deists hold this, wanting to honour God's greatness above creation. The Son says, 'the Father hath not left me alone' (John 8:29), and the Father seeks and judges (John 8:50).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Assurance, because the Father is with those who are in his Son. Humility, because being religious is not the same as knowing God. And worship, because the Father delights to honour the Son, and so should we.

### Christology · I Am: The Eternal Son
**Proposition:** Jesus Christ is the eternal Son of God, who is 'from above' and not of this world, and who existed before Abraham, not as a creature made earlier than others, but with the eternal being of God. He identifies himself with the I AM who revealed his name to Moses. Faith in him as 'he' is necessary for salvation.

**Textual Anchor:** "Jesus said unto them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Before Abraham was, I am." (John 8:58), with *genesthai* (G1096, John 8:58, V-2ADN) set against *eimi* (G1510, John 8:58, V-PAI-1S); 'for if ye believe not that I am he, ye shall die in your sins' (John 8:24); 'I am from above: ye are of this world; I am not of this world' (John 8:23); and the stones of John 8:59.

**Cross-References:** "And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM: and he said, Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, I AM hath sent me unto you." (Exodus 3:14); "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1); "Jesus Christ the same yesterday, and to day, and for ever." (Hebrews 13:8)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The Son existed before Abraham as God's first creation, but is not God.* Arius taught this in the fourth century, and Jehovah's Witnesses teach a form of it today, wanting to guard the supremacy of the one God. But John 8:58 contrasts Abraham's coming-to-be with the Son's being, not an older creature with a younger, and the hearers took it as blasphemy (John 8:59; 10:33). (2) *Jesus existed before Abraham only in God's plan.* Socinians and some modern unitarians hold this, wanting to guard his true humanity. But Jesus says 'I am', not 'I was planned', and elsewhere speaks of the glory he had with the Father 'before the world was' (John 17:5). (3) *Jesus never claimed to be God; his followers claimed it for him later.* Many modern sceptics hold this, wanting to separate the historical Jesus from the church's creeds. The claim is on his own lips, in public, before enemies who understood it well enough to reach for stones (John 8:58–59).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Worship, because the one who forgives us is the eternal God. Security, because the Saviour who holds us 'abideth ever' (John 8:35). And urgency in witness, because faith in him as 'he' is the difference between life and dying in our sins (John 8:24).

### Christology · The Light of the World, Without Sin
**Proposition:** Jesus Christ is the one light of the whole world, the source of the saving knowledge of God and of life itself. He is without sin; no one can convict him, and he always does what pleases the Father. Whoever follows him will never walk in darkness, and whoever refuses him remains in it.

**Textual Anchor:** "Then spake Jesus again unto them, saying, I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life." (John 8:12), with *phōs* (G5457, John 8:12) and *akolouthōn* (G190, John 8:12, V-PAP-NSM); 'Which of you convinceth me of sin?' (John 8:46), with *elegchei* (G1651, John 8:46, V-PAI-3S); 'for I do always those things that please him' (John 8:29).

**Cross-References:** "The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light: they that dwell in the land of the shadow of death, upon them hath the light shined." (Isaiah 9:2); "That was the true Light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world." (John 1:9); "Who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth:" (1 Peter 2:22)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus is one light among many; every religion is a path to the same summit.* Pluralists hold this, wanting to avoid arrogance toward others. Jesus does not say he is a light for some, but 'the light of the world' (John 8:12), and the alternative to following him is not another light but darkness. (2) *Every person has an inner light that is enough, if only they follow it.* Some mystical and liberal traditions hold this, wanting to honour the dignity of every person. John says that men love darkness rather than light (John 3:19), and in John 8 the religious crowd, confident in their own light, could not hear him (John 8:43); the light that saves is outside us, in Christ, and must shine into us (2 Corinthians 4:6). (3) *Jesus was a good man but a sinner like the rest of us.* Some modern writers hold this, wanting to take his humanity seriously. He challenged his enemies to convict him of sin, and they could not (John 8:46).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Hope for anyone in darkness, since the light has come into the world. Direction for daily life: follow him, and you will not walk in darkness. And confidence, because the one we follow has never taken a wrong step.

### Anthropology & Sin · The Slavery of Sin and the Father of Lies
**Proposition:** Everyone who practises sin is its slave, not its master. Apart from Christ, fallen human beings share the devil's family likeness, wanting his desires, living by lies and hostility toward the truth, and unable to hear God's word, not for lack of faculties but because they are 'not of God'. Religious privilege and descent do not free anyone from this bondage.

**Textual Anchor:** "Jesus answered them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin." (John 8:34), with *doulos* (G1401, John 8:34); 'Why do ye not understand my speech? even because ye cannot hear my word' (John 8:43); 'Ye are of your father the devil, and the lusts of your father ye will do' (John 8:44), with *thelete* (G2309, John 8:44, V-PAI-2P).

**Cross-References:** "Among whom also we all had our conversation in times past in the lusts of our flesh, fulfilling the desires of the flesh and of the mind; and were by nature the children of wrath, even as others." (Ephesians 2:3); "For we ourselves also were sometimes foolish, disobedient, deceived, serving divers lusts and pleasures, living in malice and envy, hateful, and hating one another." (Titus 3:3); "And that they may recover themselves out of the snare of the devil, who are taken captive by him at his will." (2 Timothy 2:26)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The human will is free and able to choose God unaided.* Pelagius taught this in the fifth century, and many popular forms of Christianity assume it, wanting to guard human responsibility. Jesus says the one who sins is sin's slave, and that his hearers 'cannot hear' his word (John 8:34, 43); responsibility is real, but freedom must be given by the Son (John 8:36). (2) *People are basically good; sin is a mistake or a sickness, not a bondage.* Much modern thought holds this, wanting to honour human dignity. Jesus speaks of slavery and of a father who is a murderer and a liar (John 8:34, 44), and his hearers were the most religious people in the land. (3) *The devil is a myth, a personification of evil.* Some modern theologians hold this, wanting to present the faith without ancient mythology (Rudolf Bultmann's programme of demythologizing is the best known, verify). Jesus speaks of the devil as a person with a history ('from the beginning'), a character ('abode not in the truth'), and a family (John 8:44), and he came to destroy his works (1 John 3:8).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Humility, because the slavery described is ours by nature. Compassion toward those in bondage, since we were there too (Titus 3:3). And honesty about the enemy, who works by lies, so that the believer's defence is the truth.

### Soteriology & Grace · No Condemnation, and a Call to Holiness
**Proposition:** Christ, who alone is without sin and alone has the right to condemn, does not condemn the sinner who stands before him, and his mercy comes first, before any amendment of life. That mercy does not excuse sin but breaks its hold: the one who is not condemned is sent away to 'sin no more'. Grace creates holiness; it never licenses sin.

**Textual Anchor:** "She said, No man, Lord. And Jesus said unto her, Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more." (John 8:11), with *katakrinō* (G2632, John 8:11, V-PAI-1S) and *hamartane* (G264, John 8:11, V-PAM-2S); 'He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her' (John 8:7), with *anamartētos* (G361, John 8:7).

**Cross-References:** "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit." (Romans 8:1); "Teaching us that, denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly, in this present world;" (Titus 2:12); "Who is a God like unto thee, that pardoneth iniquity, and passeth by the transgression of the remnant of his heritage? he retaineth not his anger for ever, because he delighteth in mercy." (Micah 7:18)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Since Christ does not condemn, sin does not matter.* Antinomians in several centuries have drawn this conclusion, wanting to guard the freeness of grace. Jesus' next words are 'go, and sin no more' (John 8:11), and Paul answers the same error: "God forbid. How shall we, that are dead to sin, live any longer therein?" (Romans 6:2) (2) *Forgiveness must be earned by reforming first.* Moralists of every age hold this, wanting to protect God's holiness. But the order in John 8:11 is mercy first, then the call to holiness; the woman did nothing to earn it. (3) *There is no condemnation for anyone, whatever they believe.* Universalists hold this, wanting to honour God's love. But the same Jesus warns in the same chapter, 'ye shall die in your sins' (John 8:24); the 'no condemnation' is spoken to one who stood before him and called him Lord, and Paul says it is for those 'in Christ Jesus' (Romans 8:1).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Freedom from shame for the forgiven. Courage to confess, because the one we confess to does not throw stones. And a real fight against sin, carried on in gratitude rather than fear.

### Soteriology & Grace · The Son Shall Make You Free
**Proposition:** The Son, who abides in the Father's house for ever, has the authority to set the slaves of sin free, and the freedom he gives is real, not nominal. He does it by the truth, which is his own word and himself, known by those who continue in it. This freedom is freedom from sin's guilt and dominion, not freedom to sin.

**Textual Anchor:** "If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed." (John 8:36), with *ontōs* (G3689, John 8:36); "And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free." (John 8:32), with *eleutherōsei* (G1659, John 8:32, V-FAI-3S); 'the servant abideth not in the house for ever: but the Son abideth ever' (John 8:35).

**Cross-References:** "Being then made free from sin, ye became the servants of righteousness." (Romans 6:18); "Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage." (Galatians 5:1); "For the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus hath made me free from the law of sin and death." (Romans 8:2)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Freedom means having no master and doing as I please.* Much of modern culture holds this, wanting to protect the individual from tyranny. Jesus says that whoever does as sin pleases is a slave (John 8:34), and Paul says the freed become servants of righteousness (Romans 6:18). (2) *Saving freedom comes through secret or superior knowledge.* The ancient Gnostics taught that liberating knowledge frees the spirit from the material world, and New Age teaching offers similar paths, wanting to honour the human search for truth. The truth of John 8:32 is known by continuing in Jesus' word, and in John 8:36 the one who frees is the Son, not the knower. (3) *We can free ourselves from sin by discipline or technique.* Self-help and moralism hold this, wanting to take human effort seriously. The slave cannot free himself; only the Son of the house can (John 8:35–36).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Hope for those caught in besetting sins and addictions: the Son has authority to free them. Gratitude, because freedom is a gift. And vigilance, to stand fast and not return to the yoke (Galatians 5:1).

### Eschatology · Never See Death, or Die in Your Sins
**Proposition:** Whoever keeps Christ's word will never see death, for death has lost its power to separate them from God; but whoever will not believe in him will die in his sins and cannot come where he has gone. The two destinies are settled by one's response to Christ in this life.

**Textual Anchor:** "Verily, verily, I say unto you, If a man keep my saying, he shall never see death." (John 8:51), with *tērēsē* (G5083, John 8:51, V-AAS-3S); 'I go my way, and ye shall seek me, and shall die in your sins: whither I go, ye cannot come' (John 8:21); 'if ye believe not that I am he, ye shall die in your sins' (John 8:24).

**Cross-References:** "Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:" (John 11:25); "And deliver them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage." (Hebrews 2:15); "He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life: and he that believeth not the Son shall not see life; but the wrath of God abideth on him." (John 3:36)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Everyone will be saved in the end.* Universalists hold this, wanting to honour God's love and victory. Jesus warns that those who will not believe 'shall die in your sins' and cannot come where he goes (John 8:21, 24). (2) *Death is simply the end for everyone.* Materialists hold this, wanting to face reality without illusion. Jesus says the one who keeps his saying 'shall never see death' (John 8:51), and he later proved his authority over death at Lazarus' grave and his own (John 11:43–44; 20:1–9). (3) *There will be another chance after death.* Some hold this, wanting to be fair to those who never heard clearly. John 8:21 places the seeking after it is too late: 'ye shall seek me, and shall die in your sins'.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Comfort at the graveside, because believers do not 'see death' as its victims. Urgency in evangelism, because the warning is real. And freedom from the fear of death, the last bondage (Hebrews 2:15).

### Christian Walk · Continue in My Word
**Proposition:** True disciples are those who continue in Christ's word, not those who have had a moment of believing. The family likeness of God's children is shown in hearing God's words, loving the Son, and doing the works of faith, as Abraham did; heritage and profession without these are not discipleship.

**Textual Anchor:** "Then said Jesus to those Jews which believed on him, If ye continue in my word, then are ye my disciples indeed;" (John 8:31), with *meinēte* (G3306, John 8:31, V-AAS-2P); 'If ye were Abraham's children, ye would do the works of Abraham' (John 8:39); 'If God were your Father, ye would love me' (John 8:42); 'He that is of God heareth God's words' (John 8:47).

**Cross-References:** "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me:" (John 10:27); "Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path." (Psalm 119:105); "Know ye therefore that they which are of faith, the same are the children of Abraham." (Galatians 3:7)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *A single profession of faith settles everything, whether or not a person continues.* Some forms of popular evangelism have implied this, wanting to guard salvation by grace alone. But Jesus tells those who had believed that continuing is the mark of real disciples (John 8:31), and these same hearers ended the day with stones (John 8:59); Reformed theology teaches that true believers persevere, and that perseverance is the evidence, not the price, of grace. (2) *Continuing in the word is how we earn our place with God.* Legalists hold this, wanting to take obedience seriously. Jesus says the continuing shows who his disciples are; it does not make them so, and the freedom is the Son's gift (John 8:36). (3) *Being born into a believing family or church makes a person a disciple.* Many cultural Christians assume this, as the crowd assumed it of Abraham (John 8:33, 39). Jesus looks for Abraham's works, not Abraham's blood.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Daily time in the word, not as a duty but as abiding. Honest self-examination without despair. And patience with young believers, whose continuing is the Lord's work in them.

### Assumed Here
- **Pneumatology:** John 8 does not speak of the Holy Spirit directly, though the new birth that makes a person 'of God' (John 8:47) is his work. Scripture teaches it plainly in John 3:5–8, John 7:37–39, and John 16:8–13, where the Spirit convicts the world, as conscience convicted the accusers.
- **Ecclesiology:** The chapter assumes a gathered people of God at the temple, and speaks of disciples and of Abraham's children, but it does not teach the nature or order of the church. Scripture teaches it plainly in Matthew 16:18, Acts 2:41–47, and Galatians 3:26–29, where all who are in Christ are Abraham's seed.

## ⚖️ The Council's Hard Questions
### ❓ Question 1 · Is the Woman's Story Scripture, and Did Jesus Set Aside Moses
**Moderator:** The KJV prints John 7:53–8:11, and this study teaches it as printed, but many modern Bibles bracket it. And within the story, Jesus seems to set aside the law of Moses, which commanded death for adultery. How should the group handle both questions?

**Philologist:** The textual facts first, stated calmly (verify each against a standard textual commentary). The passage is absent from the earliest surviving Greek manuscripts of John, among them P66, P75, Codex Sinaiticus, and Codex Vaticanus; it is present in Codex Bezae and in the great majority of later Greek manuscripts; in some copies it is marked with signs of doubt, and in others it stands in other places, after John 7:36, 7:44, or 21:25, or in Luke after Luke 21:38. Scholars differ on how to weigh this: many judge the passage a true story that was added to John later; others argue that it was part of John and was removed or displaced. Both sides include people who love the Scriptures.

**Church Historian:** The church has both received and questioned the passage for a very long time. Jerome put it in the Latin Vulgate. Augustine thought that some had cut it from their copies, fearing that it would seem to excuse adultery (*On Adulterous Marriages* 2.7, verify). Eusebius reports that Papias knew a story of a woman accused before the Lord of many sins (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39, verify), though whether it is this story is debated. The King James translators, working from the printed Greek editions of their day, printed it without comment. The owner's decision for this course is to teach the text the KJV prints, and to explain the question without scorn for either side.

**Historian:** On the second question, remember the setting. Rome did not allow the Jewish authorities to carry out executions on their own (John 18:31), and the law of Moses required the man to die as well as the woman (Leviticus 20:10), and required witnesses whose hands would be first against the guilty (Deuteronomy 17:7). The accusers brought a woman without the man, in a public place, to trap a teacher. This was not a trial; it was a set-up.

**Theologian:** So Jesus does not set aside the law; he fulfils it and turns it on the accusers. He holds the law to its own terms: let the witnesses, who must be first, be clean. He does not deny her guilt: 'sin no more' assumes it. And he does not deny the law's penalty in principle; he declines to be made a judge in a rigged case, having come not to condemn but to save (John 3:17). The deeper answer is the cross: the sinless one who could have thrown the first stone chose instead to bear the judgment himself (2 Corinthians 5:21). Mercy here does not cancel the law; it is paid for.

**Pastor:** In the group, two kinds of members will be unsettled. Some will be troubled that Bibles differ; tell them the truth plainly, and tell them that no doctrine rests on this passage alone, since Christ's mercy and his call to holiness are taught throughout the Gospel (John 3:17; 5:14). Others will be troubled by the story itself, because their own sin, or a family member's, is in it. Do not rush them. Then turn to Christ, as the story does.

**Resolution:** This lesson reads John 7:53–8:11 as the KJV prints it. The leader should say, calmly and without scorn for either side, that many modern editions bracket it because the earliest manuscripts lack it, that the KJV and the great majority of later manuscripts print it, and that no doctrine depends on it. Jesus did not set aside Moses; he exposed the accusers by the law's own demand, refused to condemn, and called the sinner to holiness. The Gospel says elsewhere why he could: "For God sent not his Son into the world to condemn the world; but that the world through him might be saved." (John 3:17)

### ❓ Question 2 · The Believers Who Took Up Stones
**Moderator:** John says that 'many believed on him' (John 8:30), and Jesus speaks 'to those Jews which believed on him' (John 8:31). Yet within the same conversation he says to his hearers, 'ye seek to kill me' and 'Ye are of your father the devil' (John 8:37, 44), and at the end they take up stones (John 8:59). Were these people believers or not?

**Literary Scholar:** John does not tell us that every hearer in John 8:33–59 is from the group of John 8:31; the crowd in the temple is mixed, and the conversation moves. But he does seem to intend a link: the 'They' of John 8:33 answer the words Jesus has just spoken to the believers. And John has prepared us for this. In John 2:23–25 'many believed in his name', and Jesus 'did not commit himself unto them'. In John 6 many disciples went back (John 6:66). John shows a kind of believing that is real as far as it goes, and stops.

**Philologist:** The verbs support that. John 8:30 has *episteusan* (G4100, John 8:30), 'they believed', a simple aorist. John 8:31 has *pepisteukotas* (G4100, John 8:31, V-RAP-APM), a perfect participle, 'those who had believed', and it takes a simple dative, *autō* (G846, John 8:31), 'believed him', where John often writes 'believed into him' with a preposition (as the KJV's 'believed on him' in John 8:30 renders). Some commentators think the change marks a weaker faith, believing what he said for the moment rather than trusting in him (verify); others doubt that the grammar can bear that weight. Either way, Jesus immediately tests their faith: 'If ye continue in my word'.

**Theologian:** And that is the answer. Jesus does not deny that they had believed in some sense; he says what marks true disciples: continuing. Their response to the offer of freedom shows that his word 'hath no place' in them (John 8:37). This fits the rest of the New Testament: 'They went out from us, but they were not of us' (1 John 2:19). The Reformed doctrine of perseverance holds both sides: true faith continues, because God keeps it (John 10:28–29); and a faith that falls away shows that it was not saving faith.

**Pastor:** That doctrine needs careful handling in the group. Some members will be frightened by it, wondering whether their own faith is real. Tell them that the evidence Jesus asks for is continuing in his word, not perfection, and that a person who is anxious to continue is showing the very sign he looks for. Others will be careless, resting on a moment long ago. For them, the story of believers who ended with stones in their hands is a needed warning.

**Teacher:** I would put the two verses side by side on a board, John 8:30–31 and John 8:59, and ask, 'What happened in between?' Then have the group trace it: the offer of freedom, the offence at being called slaves, the appeal to Abraham, the refusal of the truth. They will see that the turning point was the word 'free' (John 8:32–33). People who believe Jesus is helpful often turn when he tells them they are slaves.

**Resolution:** John describes a real but shallow believing that did not continue, and Jesus names the mark of the real thing: continuing in his word. The account is a warning to the careless and not a terror to the anxious, since the Son who makes free keeps those whom he frees. "Then said Jesus to those Jews which believed on him, If ye continue in my word, then are ye my disciples indeed;" (John 8:31)

### ❓ Question 3 · Your Father the Devil: Is John 8 Anti-Jewish
**Moderator:** In John 8:44 Jesus says to 'the Jews' in the temple, 'Ye are of your father the devil'. Over the centuries this verse has been used to vilify the Jewish people, with terrible results. Is the chapter itself anti-Jewish, and how should it be taught?

**Historian:** Start with who is speaking and to whom. Jesus is a Jew, speaking in the Jewish temple, at a Jewish feast, to fellow Jews, most of them Judeans, some of them leaders, who are seeking to kill him (John 8:37, 40). His disciples are Jews, and so is the Gospel writer. The argument is an argument within Israel about who Israel's true children are, like the prophets' arguments before him: Isaiah called Jerusalem's rulers 'rulers of Sodom' (Isaiah 1:10, verify the wording) without despising Israel. The Dead Sea Scrolls show Jewish groups of the period using equally sharp language about one another (verify).

**Church Historian:** And the church's later misuse must be named plainly. Christian preachers from the fourth century onward (John Chrysostom's sermons against the Jews are a notorious example, verify) and later writers, Luther's late treatise on the Jews among them (verify), used language like John 8:44 to stir up contempt, and it fed persecution that culminated in horrors in the twentieth century. That misuse is a sin against the Jewish people and against the Jewish Messiah whose words were twisted.

**Theologian:** The theology of the verse is not ethnic. The same diagnosis falls on every human being apart from grace: 'we all' were 'by nature the children of wrath, even as others' (Ephesians 2:3), and 'In this the children of God are manifest, and the children of the devil' (1 John 3:10), a sentence written to a mostly Gentile church (verify). Jesus names the family likeness by its fruits, murder and lies (John 8:40, 44), and those fruits can grow in any heart, including Gentile Christian hearts that hate Jews. And Jesus says 'salvation is of the Jews' (John 4:22).

**Literary Scholar:** Notice also how John uses 'the Jews'. In this very chapter, some of 'the Jews' believe (John 8:31). Elsewhere, 'the Jews' comfort Martha and Mary (John 11:19), and many of them believe (John 11:45). The phrase often means the Judean authorities or the hostile crowd, and it is always a character in a story, not a verdict on a people.

**Pastor:** In the group, say this plainly before you read John 8:44 aloud, especially if anyone present has Jewish family or friends. Then turn the verse where Jesus turned it: to the question of whose family likeness we bear. The one reading that must never be taught is the one that makes John 8:44 about someone else's ethnicity.

**Resolution:** John 8 is not anti-Jewish; it records an argument within Israel, spoken by a Jew to fellow Jews who sought to kill him, and its diagnosis applies to every unregenerate heart. Christians who have used it against the Jewish people have sinned, and the church should say so. The test Jesus gives is not blood but love for the Son: "Jesus said unto them, If God were your Father, ye would love me: for I proceeded forth and came from God; neither came I of myself, but he sent me." (John 8:42)

### 🔦 The Life & Light Audit
**Pastor:** If a hearer obeyed this lesson exactly, then tonight they would name to the Lord one sin that holds them as a slave, confess it, and read John 8:36 aloud; and they would pray Psalm 139:23–24 and confess one sin they have hidden while noticing others'. Tomorrow they would reach out to someone who is ashamed of their past, with courtesy and without mentioning the sin, and they would refuse to add a stone when a conversation turns to someone's failure. What would come alive is their own freedom, and hope for someone the world has already sentenced. The dark place lit would be their own accusing heart, first.

**Theologian:** The practices keep source discipline. Every Life practice begins from the Son who makes free (John 8:36) or the word he spoke to the woman (John 8:11); none asks the believer to free anyone by their own effort, and the prayer for the addicted person asks the Son to set them free. The Light practices turn first on the participant's own heart and on hidden sin, and the outward ones refuse to accuse; that keeps the Love Guard. The Abundance Guard is kept by distinguishing the Son's freedom from the freedom to do as we please (John 8:34), and by the reminder that the Jesus who gives it was slandered and stoned.

**Teacher:** A Level 1 participant can do the Inward Life practice tonight: name one sin, confess it, read one verse. The Outward practices name the kind of person to reach (someone ashamed of their past; someone caught in an addiction; a believer who has fallen publicly; a believer fighting a besetting sin; a friend who feels lost) and leave a blank for the name. The Light practices are concrete: one prayer tonight, one refusal to join the stones tomorrow, one verse shared today.

**Fix:** The Audit found three risks, and the unit addresses them. First, a lesson about an adulteress can turn into a session on other people's sexual sin, or can wound members who have lived it; so the Light questions start with the participant's own heart (Q26), the Common Errors table answers 'being light means exposing other people', the Leader's Map coaches the member who points the light outward, and the Shepherding Notes name the tender places. Second, 'Neither do I condemn thee' can be heard as permission; so every place that quotes it also quotes 'go, and sin no more', and the Doctrine block on grace and holiness refutes antinomianism. Third, John 8:44 can be heard as contempt for Jewish people; so Setting the Scene, the Cultural Landmines, the Council's third question, and the Shepherding Notes all address it. The naming round reads only the second row of the Light Planner, never the private first row.

### ✅ Answer Key
**Q1.** Jesus went to the mount of Olives, and early in the morning he came again into the temple, where all the people came to him; he sat down and taught them (John 8:1–2). The scribes and Pharisees brought a woman taken in adultery and set her in the midst (John 8:3).

**Q2.** They said she was taken in adultery 'in the very act', that Moses commanded such to be stoned, and asked what he said (John 8:4–5). John says they asked it tempting him, so that they might have grounds to accuse him (John 8:6).

**Q3.** He stooped down and wrote on the ground with his finger, as though he did not hear (John 8:6). When they kept asking, he stood and said that whoever was without sin among them should cast the first stone at her, and stooped and wrote again (John 8:7–8). Convicted by their own conscience, they went out one by one, beginning with the eldest, until Jesus was left alone with the woman (John 8:9).

**Q4.** He asked, 'where are those thine accusers?' and 'hath no man condemned thee?' (John 8:10). She said, 'No man, Lord'. He said that he did not condemn her either, and told her to go and sin no more (John 8:11).

**Q5.** He said, 'I am the light of the world', and promised that whoever follows him will not walk in darkness but will have the light of life (John 8:12). The Pharisees objected that he was bearing record of himself, so his record was not true (John 8:13).

**Q6.** He warns that they will die in their sins (John 8:21, 24, where it is said twice). It depends on whether they believe that he is he: 'if ye believe not that I am he, ye shall die in your sins' (John 8:24).

**Q7.** If they continue in his word, they are his disciples indeed; they will know the truth, and the truth will make them free (John 8:31–32). They answered that they were Abraham's seed, had never been in bondage to anyone, and asked how he could say they would be made free (John 8:33).

**Q8.** He said Abraham rejoiced to see his day, and saw it and was glad (John 8:56). When they asked how he could have seen Abraham, he said, 'Before Abraham was, I am' (John 8:57–58). They took up stones to throw at him, but he hid himself and went out of the temple (John 8:59).

**Q9.** God revealed his name as I AM (Exodus 3:14) and called his people to know that 'I am he', the only God (Isaiah 43:10). He promised a great light to people in darkness (Isaiah 9:2). Abraham trusted God to provide a lamb (Genesis 22:8, 14). Those who were slaves of sin are made free and become servants of righteousness (Romans 6:17–18), and walking in the light brings fellowship and cleansing by Christ's blood (1 John 1:7). Together they show that Jesus is the I AM, the promised light, the one Abraham looked for, and the Son who frees slaves of sin into a life of walking in the light.

**Q10.** To follow him is to keep trusting and obeying him as one follows a guide, as Israel followed the pillar of fire (Exodus 13:21; John 8:12). The light of life is the light that gives life and is life, the knowledge of God in Christ (John 1:4–5). John 3:19–21 shows why some refuse it: they love darkness because their deeds are evil, while those who do truth come to the light.

**Q11.** Sin feels like freedom while it enslaves, and the slave can grow so used to his master that he calls it freedom (John 8:33–34). Only the Son can free, because a slave has no permanent place in the house, but the Son abides for ever and has authority in the house to set slaves free (John 8:35–36).

**Q12.** A person's father is shown by what they do, whom they love, and whether they hear God's words (John 8:39, 42, 47). Abraham's children do Abraham's works of faith; God's children love his Son; the devil's children want his desires, murder and lies (John 8:44). Galatians 3:7 says that those who are of faith are Abraham's children.

**Q13.** Suggested answer: both views agree that Jesus neither called her sin nothing nor sent her back to it (John 8:11). View A fits Jesus' authority to forgive (Mark 2:10) and her address, 'Lord'. View B fits the legal setting, with the witnesses gone (Deuteronomy 17:6; John 8:9), and Jesus' mission not to condemn (John 3:17). Either way, the order matters: mercy first, then the call to holiness.

**Q14.** Answers will vary. We see others' sin easily because our own is familiar, excused, or hidden, and because judging others makes us feel better about ourselves (2 Samuel 12:5–7; Matthew 7:3–5). The accusers left without confessing; David stayed and said, 'I have sinned against the LORD' (2 Samuel 12:13). The difference is humility before God.

**Q15.** Answers will vary. Encourage members to name specific places, such as family conversations, group chats, or church gossip, and to hold together 'Neither do I condemn thee' and 'go, and sin no more' (John 8:11; Galatians 6:1).

**Q16.** A person can have health, success, and every outward good and still not know God, which is walking in darkness (John 8:12; 3:19). For Christ to rise on the soul is for him to shine into the heart with the knowledge of God (2 Corinthians 4:6). The response the hymn and the verse ask for is to follow him.

**Q17.** It matters who he is because only the eternal Son can forgive sins and give life; a good teacher cannot save anyone from dying in their sins (John 8:24). John 8:58 answers 'Who art thou?' (John 8:25): he is the I AM, who was before Abraham, the God who revealed his name to Moses (Exodus 3:14).

**Q18.** Answers will vary. The only one with the right to condemn refused to, which shows that his mercy is not ignorance of our sin but grace toward it (John 8:7, 11). Members may name places where they still carry guilt Christ has removed; point them to Romans 8:1.

**Q19.** Answers will vary. Many of us are accusers at times, silent onlookers at others, and all of us are sinners in need of mercy (John 8:3–11). The elders left first, perhaps because age and experience had taught them more of their own sin; growing older in the faith should make us gentler, not harsher (Galatians 6:1).

**Q20.** Answers will vary, but the step should be concrete: a confession tonight, a conversation with a trusted believer tomorrow, removing a source of temptation (John 8:34, 36; James 5:16). Humbling ourselves before God begins with naming the sin without excuse (1 John 1:9).

**Q21.** Model answer, not the only answer: we honour ourselves by defending our reputation, seeking credit, or insisting on being right. The Son, who deserved all honour, let the Father honour him (John 8:50, 54), and that makes him more glorious, not less (Philippians 2:5–7).

**Q22.** Model answer, not the only answer: a friend caught in drinking, gambling, pornography, or anger who insists they are in control; a relative who calls a destructive pattern freedom (John 8:33–34). Encourage members to write a specific name.

**Q23.** Model answer, not the only answer: the thief may have stolen their freedom, their dignity, their ability to hear God's word, or their hope in the face of death (John 8:34, 43–44, 51; John 10:10).

**Q24.** Model answer, not the only answer: Christ gives real freedom through his word and his own authority as the Son (John 8:31–36). It matters that the Son frees, because a slave cannot free himself, and programs and willpower cannot break a master's claim; only the one with authority in the house can.

**Q25.** Model answer, not the only answer: someone whose affair, arrest, divorce, or addiction became public; someone written off by family or church (John 8:3–5). They need to hear that Jesus came to save, not to condemn (John 3:17).

**Q26.** Model answer, not the only answer: a hidden sin, a secret resentment, or a habit we excuse while we notice others' faults (John 8:7, 9; Matthew 7:3–5). The honest answer belongs in the private first row of the Light Planner.

**Q27.** Model answer, not the only answer: a Christian upbringing, church attendance, Bible knowledge, good works, or a past decision (John 8:33, 39). Jesus' word exposes that none of these is faith in him, and invites us to trust the Son who alone makes free (John 8:36).

**Q28.** Model answer, not the only answer: a friend who believes God could never accept them, a relative who has rejected faith because of hypocrisy they saw, a coworker who believes lies about God (John 8:43–45). Carry light by admitting our own need of mercy first, then speaking the truth gently (Ephesians 4:15; John 8:11).

### 🗺️ Life & Light Leader's Map
**Worked Life Planner Row:** *Who:* 'Mark, my brother-in-law, whose drinking cost him his licence, and who says he can stop whenever he wants'. *What the thief has stolen:* his freedom, while he insists he is free, as the crowd did (John 8:33–34); and his family's trust. *What Christ gives instead:* 'If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed' (John 8:36). *What I will do:* on Thursday, call him just to talk, with no lecture; pray for him by name each morning; and if he mentions wanting to stop, tell him I know someone who frees people, and offer to go with him to a recovery group at church. *Done?* Tick it when done, and write one line about how it went.

**Worked Light Planner Row:** *Where is it dark (second row):* 'the way people at church have talked about the couple whose marriage broke up'. *What is hidden there:* gossip dressed as concern, and my own part in passing it on (John 8:3–7). *What light Christ shines:* 'He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her' (John 8:7), and 'Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more' (John 8:11). *How I carry it there:* on Sunday, when the talk starts, say quietly that I'd rather pray for them than talk about them; then send one of them a note saying I am praying, and nothing else.

**When Someone Has No One to Name:** Walk through their week with them: the coworker whose habit everyone jokes about, the relative whose life has come apart, the neighbour who has been in trouble with the law, the friend who has quietly stopped coming to church after a failure. Remind them that the woman in John 8 was someone everyone had already named for the wrong reasons; Jesus named her 'Woman' and gave her a future (John 8:10–11). If they still cannot name someone, ask them to pray tonight, 'Lord, show me one person who needs to hear that you do not condemn them', and to bring the name next week.

**When Someone Points the Light at Others:** When a member names another person's, another church's, or another group's sin as their dark place, bring them gently back to the first row of their Light Planner and to John 8:7. Say something like: 'The accusers had a real sin in front of them, and Jesus turned their eyes to their own. Let's start where he started.' Remind them that Jesus dealt with the woman privately, after the crowd had gone. If the member persists, speak with them privately after the session.

### 🐑 Shepherding Notes
- **Members touched by adultery.** Some in the group may have committed adultery, or been betrayed by a spouse, or grown up in a home broken by it. The story can open old wounds in either direction: shame for some, anger for others. Speak of the sin plainly and of Christ's mercy warmly, and never make the woman a joke or a type. Offer to talk privately after the session.
- **Members who have been publicly shamed.** Some will know what it is to be set 'in the midst' (John 8:3): a public failure, an arrest, a church discipline handled harshly. Let John 8:10–11 speak to them: Jesus sent the crowd away before he spoke to her about her sin.
- **Members caught in addiction or besetting sin.** 'Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin' (John 8:34) may describe their daily life. Do not let the lesson sound as if freedom is instant or easy; the Son's freedom is real, and it is often worked out over time, with help from the body of Christ. Point them to church pastoral care or recovery ministries where appropriate.
- **Members who fear their faith is not real.** The Council's second question may unsettle anxious believers. Reassure them that the mark Jesus names is continuing in his word, not perfection, and that their anxiety to continue is itself a sign of life.
- **Jewish members, friends, or family.** John 8:44 has been used to vilify Jewish people. Say plainly, before you read it, that it was spoken by a Jew to particular men seeking to kill him, and that the church has sinned when it has used it otherwise (see the Council's third question).
- **Members from Christian families.** 'We be Abraham's seed' (John 8:33) may press on members who have assumed that growing up in church made them Christians. Be gentle: the point is not to strip them of a good heritage, but to invite them to personal faith in the Son.
- **Members who meet Jehovah's Witnesses or unitarian ideas.** Some may be unsettled by different translations of John 8:58. Equip them patiently with the whole chapter's sequence (John 8:24, 28, 58) and encourage gentleness in conversation (1 Peter 3:15).
- **The Light Planner's first row.** It is private. Never ask anyone to read it aloud or to share it. If someone chooses to speak about their own heart, receive it with gentleness and pray for them; do not probe for details.

### 🎯 Teaching Angles
**For a Tier 1 Group:** Bring a torch or a lamp and turn down the room lights. Tell the story of the woman and the accusers simply, and let the group act it out without dialogue: the crowd, the stones (small pebbles), the dropped stones, the two left. Then turn on the torch, read John 8:12 aloud, and ask: 'What does a light do in a dark room? What does Jesus do in a dark heart?' End by reading 'Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more' (John 8:11) and giving each person a pebble to keep as a reminder not to throw stones, and that Jesus did not throw one at them.

**For a Tier 2 Group:** Trace three lines through the chapter. The line of fathers: the word 'father' occurs 21 times in the KJV of the chapter, and every mention of Abraham in John's Gospel is here; ask whose family likeness each speaker shows (John 8:38–44). The line of freedom: 'free' occurs 4 times in the KJV of the chapter (John 8:32–36); ask what kind of slavery and what kind of freedom Jesus means. The line of 'I am': set John 8:12, 24, 28, and 58 side by side with Exodus 3:14 and Isaiah 43:10, and ask why the crowd reached for stones. Then ask the group where they stand.

**Tough Question 1:** *Is the story of the woman taken in adultery really in the Bible?* It is in the King James Version and in the great majority of Greek manuscripts, and this course teaches it as printed. It is missing from the earliest surviving manuscripts of John, which is why many modern Bibles bracket it. Many scholars who doubt it belongs to John's original text believe the story is true and old. No doctrine rests on it alone: Christ's mercy and his call to holiness are taught throughout the Gospel (John 3:17; 5:14). See the Council's first question.

**Tough Question 2:** *Did Jesus say that adultery doesn't matter?* No. He said 'go, and sin no more' (John 8:11), which names adultery as sin. He refused to condemn her, and he exposed the accusers who had used her as a trap, but he did not deny God's law. His mercy comes first, and it leads to a changed life.

**Tough Question 3:** *Did Jesus really claim to be God in John 8:58?* Yes, in the strongest way his culture allowed. He contrasted Abraham's coming into being with his own eternal being, 'I am', echoing God's name in Exodus 3:14 and the 'I am he' of Isaiah 43:10. His hearers understood it, because they took up stones, the penalty for blasphemy (Leviticus 24:16; John 10:33). See the Debate Corner.
