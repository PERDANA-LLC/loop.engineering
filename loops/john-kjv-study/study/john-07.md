# John 7 · Rivers of Living Water (KJV)
> Unit john-07 · John 7:1–53 · loop john-kjv-study v2.0 · DRAFT for human doctrine review

## 🧭 At a Glance
### 🧵 Golden Thread
At the feast of tabernacles Jesus divides the crowd and invites every thirsty person to come and drink, promising the Spirit who flows out through believers.

### 🌗 Life & Light Line
John 7 answers the thirst of a people keeping a feast of water and booths while their own leaders plot murder, their pilgrims whisper 'for fear of the Jews' (John 7:13), and even the Lord's brothers do not believe (John 7:5). Christ gives instead the Spirit, rivers of living water flowing out of everyone who believes on him (John 7:38–39). It exposes the darkness of judging 'according to the appearance' (John 7:24): by a man's schooling, his hometown, and what the rulers think. The light he gives instead is 'righteous judgment' (John 7:24), which hears a man before condemning him (John 7:51) and comes to know that his doctrine is from God by being willing to do God's will (John 7:17).

### 🎯 Objectives
- Describe the division Jesus causes at the feast (John 7:12, 25–27, 31, 40–43), and explain from John 7:14–18 and 7:28–29 why every opinion about him comes down to one question: whence is he?
- Explain the promise of John 7:37–39 against the background of the feast of tabernacles and the Old Testament promises of water, and say who the living water is, who receives him, and why he was 'not yet given'.
- **Doing:** Within the next day, take one conversation where someone is being judged by appearance or by hearsay and ask for a fair hearing, as Nicodemus did (John 7:51), and tell one spiritually thirsty person Jesus' own invitation, 'If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink' (John 7:37).

## 🏛️ Core Foundations
### ✝️ Christological Root
**Gospel Foundation:** John 7 rests on what Jesus said in the temple at the feast of tabernacles, when he was being hunted and the crowd was split over him. First he told them where his teaching came from: "Jesus answered them, and said, My doctrine is not mine, but his that sent me." (John 7:16) Then he told them where he came from: "But I know him: for I am from him, and he hath sent me." (John 7:29) And on the last day of the feast, when the ceremonies of water and harvest were nearly over, he stood and offered himself: "In the last day, that great day of the feast, Jesus stood and cried, saying, If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink." (John 7:37) "He that believeth on me, as the scripture hath said, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water." (John 7:38) John tells us what he meant: "(But this spake he of the Spirit, which they that believe on him should receive: for the Holy Ghost was not yet given; because that Jesus was not yet glorified.)" (John 7:39)

**The Bridge:** The feast remembered God providing for Israel in the wilderness, water from the rock among it (Exodus 17:6), and it looked forward to the day when 'living waters shall go out from Jerusalem' (Zechariah 14:8). Jesus stands in Jerusalem, in the temple, at the climax of that feast, and says that he himself is where the thirsty must come, and that the living water is the Holy Spirit, whom he will give once he is glorified. The doctrines grounded here are the person of Christ, sent from the Father and speaking the Father's words (John 7:16, 28–29); the Father's sovereign timing of his hour (John 7:6, 30); the gift of the Spirit, which flows from the glorified Christ to every believer and out through them (John 7:38–39); and the free invitation of the gospel to 'any man' who thirsts (John 7:37).

**Canonical Echoes:** Jesus (John 7:37) → Promise (Isaiah 44:3; Isaiah 55:1; Zechariah 14:8) → Doctrine (Acts 2:33; 1 Corinthians 12:13) → Life (Revelation 22:17)

**What Gets Lost:** Taught without Christ at the centre, John 7 becomes a lesson about family tension ('even Jesus' brothers didn't get him'), a lesson in courage ('Nicodemus stood up for what was right'), or a lesson in media literacy ('don't judge by appearances'). Each of those is true as far as it goes, and none of them is the chapter. The chapter is about a man whose teaching is God's, whose origin is God, whose death is on God's timetable, and who stands in the temple and offers the Holy Spirit to anyone who is thirsty. Without him at the centre, the rivers of living water become a metaphor for personal fulfilment, and the warning that 'ye shall seek me, and shall not find me' (John 7:34) disappears.

### ⚔️ Spiritual Warfare
**The Lie:** You can settle who Jesus is by what you already know about him: where he grew up, what schooling he had, what the people in charge think of him, and what the crowd is whispering. If his hometown is ordinary and the experts are against him, you need not listen. Its quieter form sounds prudent: say nothing about him in public until you see which way the rulers go (John 7:13, 48).

**The Truth:** Jesus tells us how anyone comes to know whether his teaching is from God: "If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine, whether it be of God, or whether I speak of myself." (John 7:17) And he commands the kind of judgment that looks past appearances to the truth: "Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment." (John 7:24)

**The Battleground:** The reputation and the tongue. The mind that sorts people by their background and credentials ('How knoweth this man letters, having never learned?', John 7:15; 'out of Galilee ariseth no prophet', John 7:52). The tongue that murmurs in the crowd but will not speak openly (John 7:12–13). And the home, where Jesus' own brothers pushed him toward the stage and did not believe (John 7:3–5).

**The Weapon:** The Scriptures, searched honestly and heard to the end. The rulers told Nicodemus to 'Search, and look' (John 7:52), and had not searched far enough; the Scriptures they cited pointed to Bethlehem (John 7:42), and the Christ they wanted was standing in front of them. The believer fights with the word, received by a will that is ready to obey (John 7:17), and with the Spirit whom Christ gives (John 7:39): "the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God" (Ephesians 6:17).

### 🌱 Life Pathway
**The Theft:** In John 7 the thief steals life at a festival. For eight days Jerusalem is full of booths, branches, pilgrims, sacrifices, and rejoicing, and yet the people are thirsty, divided, and afraid. The thief steals by murder: 'the Jews sought to kill him' (John 7:1), and Jesus asks, 'Why go ye about to kill me?' (John 7:19). He steals by fear, so that 'no man spake openly of him for fear of the Jews' (John 7:13). He steals inside a family, where Jesus' own brothers 'did not believe in him' (John 7:5). And he steals by contempt, where the rulers call the ordinary people who listen to Jesus 'cursed' (John 7:49), as if life belonged only to the learned.

**The Source:** Christ himself, who stood on the great day and cried, 'If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink' (John 7:37). He came "that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly" (John 10:10). His means in this chapter are his doctrine, which is the Father's (John 7:16); his invitation to come and drink (John 7:37); and the Spirit, whom those who believe on him receive after he is glorified (John 7:39).

**The Channel:** The water does not stop with the one who drinks. 'Out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water' (John 7:38). John tells us that this is the Spirit (John 7:39), and the Spirit is never the believer's possession to control or produce. The believer is the riverbed, not the spring: the life flows from Christ, through the Spirit, out of the believer to others, in words spoken openly (unlike the whispering crowd), in fair dealing (like Nicodemus), and in the plain witness of the officers who could only say what they had heard. "For without me ye can do nothing." (John 15:5)

**The Abundance Guard:** Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, not prosperity, ease, or freedom from suffering. The feast celebrated a full harvest (Deuteronomy 16:13–15), but the rivers Jesus promised are the Spirit, not a better harvest. The Jesus who offered them was being hunted to death, was mocked as possessed (John 7:20), and would be glorified by way of a cross (John 12:23–24). The Spirit came to believers who would be hated by the world as he was (John 7:7; John 15:18–19). Rivers flow in drought as well as in plenty.

### 💡 Light Pathway
**The Darkness:** The darkness of John 7 is the darkness of confident misjudgment. The crowd thinks it knows 'whence he is' (John 7:27), and it is wrong twice: it does not know he is from God (John 7:28), and it does not know he was born in Bethlehem (John 7:42). The rulers assume that no one worth hearing could lack formal training (John 7:15), that no ruler has believed (John 7:48), and that no prophet could come from Galilee (John 7:52). They want to condemn him without hearing him (John 7:51). Beneath it lies a darkness Jesus names himself: the world hates him 'because I testify of it, that the works thereof are evil' (John 7:7).

**The Source:** Christ, who 'is true, and no unrighteousness is in him' (John 7:18), and who knows the Father (John 7:29). He is "the light of the world" (John 8:12). "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." (2 Corinthians 4:6)

**The Lamp:** In John 7 the lamp is plain, public speech and fair judgment. Jesus teaches openly in the temple and cries aloud (John 7:14, 28, 37); he tells the world its works are evil (John 7:7); he exposes the crowd's inconsistency on the sabbath with Moses' own law (John 7:22–23); and he calls for righteous judgment (John 7:24). The lamp for us is the same: the Scriptures searched honestly (John 7:52), the truth spoken openly and not in whispers, hidden prejudice confessed, the unfruitful works of darkness reproved (Ephesians 5:11), beginning with our own, and good works that glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16), like Nicodemus's simple request that a man be heard.

**The Love Guard:** Light without love is glare. Jesus told the world its works were evil, and in the same chapter he invited 'any man' who thirsts to come and drink (John 7:37), including the pilgrims who called him possessed and the Jerusalemites who knew the rulers wanted him dead. He did not answer the murmuring with contempt. The Pharisees' 'light' was all glare: 'this people who knoweth not the law are cursed' (John 7:49). We turn the lamp first on our own snap judgments, and when we must speak about another's, we do it as Nicodemus did, by asking that the person be heard.

### 🏺 Setting the Scene
Six months or so have passed since the Passover of John 6:4. The feast of tabernacles, called Sukkot in Hebrew, fell in the seventh month, in September or October, and lasted seven days, with an eighth day of solemn assembly (Leviticus 23:34–36). It was the last and most joyful of the three pilgrim feasts. The harvest was in, and every Israelite was to 'rejoice before the LORD your God seven days' (Leviticus 23:40). Families built booths of branches and lived in them for the week, to remember that God had made Israel 'to dwell in booths, when I brought them out of the land of Egypt' (Leviticus 23:43). The booth is *sukkah* (H5521), a rough, temporary shelter. The feast remembered the wilderness years, when God fed Israel with manna and gave them water from the rock (Exodus 17:6), and it looked ahead to the day when all nations would come to Jerusalem to keep it (Zechariah 14:16).

Later Jewish tradition, written down in the Mishnah about AD 200 (tractate *Sukkah*, chapters 4 and 5, verify), describes two ceremonies at the feast in the time of the second temple. Each morning a priest drew water from the pool of Siloam in a golden pitcher and poured it out at the altar while the people sang (the tradition connects the rite with Isaiah 12:3, 'with joy shall ye draw water', verify). And at night great lamps were lit in the temple's Court of the Women, so bright that, the Mishnah says, every courtyard in Jerusalem was lit by them (verify). John does not describe either ceremony, and we cannot be sure every detail was practised in Jesus' day; but against that background, Jesus' cry about living water (John 7:37–38) and his claim to be the light of the world (John 8:12) would have struck the crowds with great force.

Jesus had not been in Jerusalem since the healing at Bethesda, when the Judean authorities began seeking to kill him 'because he not only had broken the sabbath, but said also that God was his Father, making himself equal with God' (John 5:18). So John 7 opens with Jesus staying in Galilee, and closes in the temple with the council itself divided. John uses 'the Jews' here mainly for the Judean religious authorities who opposed Jesus, not the Jewish people as a whole: the pilgrims in the crowd are 'afraid of the Jews' (John 7:13), though they are Jews themselves, as Jesus, his brothers, and Nicodemus are. The chapter is full of voices: brothers, crowds, Jerusalemites, officers, Pharisees, chief priests, and one lone ruler. Listen for how each one answers the question the whole chapter asks: who is this man, and whence is he?

**View A:** The rivers of living water flow out of the believer. The KJV's punctuation reads it this way: 'He that believeth on me, as the scripture hath said, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water' (John 7:38). Whoever comes to Christ and drinks is not only satisfied but becomes a source of blessing to others, as Jesus told the woman of Samaria that his water would be 'in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life' (John 4:14). The Old Testament promises it: "and thou shalt be like a watered garden, and like a spring of water, whose waters fail not." (Isaiah 58:11) Many Greek-speaking early writers, such as Origen, and many later readers, including most Reformed commentators, read it so (verify).

**View B:** The rivers flow out of Christ himself. Some readers punctuate the Greek differently, joining 'he that believeth on me' to the invitation before it: *let him come unto me, and let him that believeth on me drink*; then *as the scripture hath said, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water* refers to Christ. The water comes from Jesus, as from the rock in the wilderness: "and that Rock was Christ." (1 Corinthians 10:4) It also foreshadows the blood and water from his pierced side (John 19:34) and the river 'proceeding out of the throne of God and of the Lamb' (Revelation 22:1). Some early Latin-speaking writers and several modern commentators hold this view (verify).

**Q13.** Which view does the text best support, and why? Before you answer, read John 7:39 closely: who is said to 'receive' the Spirit? And does either view deny that Christ is the only source of the water?

### 👣 Case Study · Scripture in Action
James was one of the brothers of Jesus (Matthew 13:55). In John 7 he appears only as one of 'his brethren', who urged Jesus to go and show himself off in Judea, and John adds the painful note: "For neither did his brethren believe in him." (John 7:5) Mark tells us that at one point Jesus' family went out to seize him, because people said he was 'beside himself' (Mark 3:21). James grew up in the same house as the Son of God, heard him teach, saw his works, and did not believe. Then something changed. Paul lists among the resurrection appearances: "After that, he was seen of James; then of all the apostles." (1 Corinthians 15:7) After the ascension, Jesus' brothers are in the upper room praying with the apostles (Acts 1:14), and James became a leader of the church in Jerusalem (Acts 15:13; Galatians 1:19). The letter that bears the name of James, traditionally ascribed to him, opens by calling its author a servant of the Lord Jesus Christ (James 1:1). The brother who once said 'shew thyself to the world' came to call himself Jesus' servant. His time came when Jesus' hour had come and gone: the cross and the empty tomb did what years of living alongside him had not.

### 🏙️ Modern Example
Dana worked as a nurse on a busy hospital ward. The Latin hymn *Veni Creator Spiritus* ('Come, Creator Spirit') is usually dated to the ninth century and is often attributed to Rabanus Maurus, a monk and later archbishop of Mainz (verify the attribution). ### 👑 Christocentric & Humility · Level 4
Every problem in John 7 is answered in Jesus. His brothers want him to seize the spotlight, and he waits for the Father's time. The authorities want him dead, and no one can lay a hand on him until his hour comes. The crowd is split and afraid, whispering in corners, and he goes up into the temple and teaches openly. The learned sneer that he never studied, and he tells them that his doctrine comes from the one who sent him. The people accuse him of having a devil, and he answers them with an argument from their own law. The Jerusalemites think they know where he comes from, and he tells them they do not know the Father who sent him. The feast is ending, the water has been poured, the lamps will soon go out, and people are still thirsty; he stands and cries that anyone who is thirsty may come to him and drink. The rulers are sure no prophet comes from Galilee, and the Christ of Bethlehem is standing in their temple.

Every truth in the chapter is found in him as well. The Father's doctrine is found in him (John 7:16). The truth is in him, and no unrighteousness (John 7:18). Knowledge of the Father is in him (John 7:29). The living water is his to give (John 7:37–38), and the Spirit is given from him once he is glorified (John 7:39). The chapter humbles us from every side. It humbles family, because his own brothers did not believe. It humbles the learned, because 'letters' did not help the rulers recognise him, while temple officers were undone by hearing him speak. It humbles the confident, because the people who knew the Scriptures best got his birthplace wrong. And it makes Christ glorious, because the one who had every right to show himself to the world chose instead to seek not his own glory 'but his that sent me' (John 7:16), and waited for an hour that would be the cross.

**Why this verse:** It is the Lesson Map's choice for this lesson and the climax of the chapter. It places Jesus at the high point of Israel's most joyful feast, standing (when teachers usually sat) and crying aloud (when most were whispering), and it gives the gospel in the simplest words in the Bible: *thirst*, *come*, *drink*. It excludes no one ('any man') and requires nothing but need. It echoes the Old Testament's great invitation to the thirsty (Isaiah 55:1), it looks forward to the Bible's last invitation (Revelation 22:17), and it leads straight to the promise of the Spirit in the next two verses. It serves the purpose John gives for his whole book: that believing we 'might have life through his name' (John 20:31).

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy
### 📌 Core Concept
Every autumn God's people in Israel had a big harvest party called the feast of tabernacles. For a whole week families lived outside in little huts made of branches, to remember how God looked after them in the desert long ago, giving them food and even water out of a rock. Some people wanted to kill Jesus, so he did not go to the feast with the big crowds. His own brothers teased him and did not believe in him. But halfway through the week Jesus went into the temple and began to teach. People argued about him. Some said he was good; some said he was tricking people. Some said he couldn't be anyone special, because they knew where he came from. The leaders sent temple guards to arrest him, but nobody could touch him, because it was not yet God's time. On the last and greatest day of the party, Jesus stood up and shouted, 'If anyone is thirsty, come to me and drink!' He meant that he would give the Holy Spirit, like rivers of water, to everyone who trusts him. The guards went back without him and said nobody had ever spoken like this man. One leader, Nicodemus, said it wasn't fair to judge Jesus without listening to him first. Then everybody went home.

### 📖 Key Verses
"Jesus answered them, and said, My doctrine is not mine, but his that sent me." (John 7:16) Jesus did not make up what he taught; it came from God his Father.

"Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment." (John 7:24) We should not decide about people by how they look or where they come from, but by what is true.

"In the last day, that great day of the feast, Jesus stood and cried, saying, If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink." (John 7:37) Anyone who feels empty inside can come to Jesus, and he will fill them.

"The officers answered, Never man spake like this man." (John 7:46) Even the men sent to arrest Jesus could tell that nobody else talked the way he did.

### 🪞 Analogy
Imagine a big family picnic on a boiling hot day. There is lots of food, games, music, and people everywhere, but somebody forgot the drinks, and everyone is thirsty. The party goes on, but people are getting grumpy and tired. On the last afternoon, someone stands up on a bench and calls out, 'If you're thirsty, come over here! I've got water, and there's enough for everybody!' That is a little like what Jesus did. The feast was full of good things, but people were still thirsty inside, for God. Jesus stood up and said that he was the one to come to. And the water he gives is special: it is God's own Holy Spirit living inside you, and it doesn't just fill you up. It overflows, so that you can help other thirsty people too.

- Have I ever felt empty or thirsty inside, even when I had lots of fun things around me?

### 🌗 Life and Light, Simply
Jesus gives life because he gives the Holy Spirit, who is like fresh water flowing into us and then out of us to other people. Jesus gives light because he shows us the truth about God and about people, so that we stop judging others by how they look and start seeing them the way God does.

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium
### 📜 Walkthrough
#### John 7:1–2 · Walking in Galilee, the Feast at Hand
**Says:** "After these things Jesus walked in Galilee: for he would not walk in Jewry, because the Jews sought to kill him." (John 7:1) "Now the Jews' feast of tabernacles was at hand." (John 7:2) *Jewry* is Judea, the southern region around Jerusalem.

**Means:** 'After these things' links the chapter loosely to John 6. Jesus has been in Galilee since the Passover of John 6:4, and John explains why: the Judean authorities had been seeking to kill him since the sabbath healing at Bethesda (John 5:16–18). The verb 'walked' pictures Jesus' travelling ministry, going about the towns of Galilee. He is not hiding out of fear; he 'would not' walk in Judea, because his death must come at the Father's time, not theirs (John 7:6, 30).

Then John names the feast: 'the Jews' feast of tabernacles'. The Greek is *skēnopēgia* (G4634, John 7:2), which the STEPBible lexicon explains as properly 'the setting up of tents or dwellings', the name the Greek Old Testament gives the feast in Deuteronomy 16:16 and Zechariah 14:16, 18–19, and it lists this verse as its only New Testament use. It was the autumn feast of harvest and booths (Leviticus 23:34–43), and after Passover (John 2:13; 6:4) and the unnamed feast of John 5:1, it is the next feast John marks. It will frame the chapter and much of the next: Jesus' words about water (John 7:37–38) and light (John 8:12) are spoken at this feast.

**Asks:** Jesus did not run ahead of the Father's timetable, even to avoid danger or to seize an opportunity. When we are pressed from outside to act, the question is not first 'Is it safe?' or 'Is it the moment?' but 'What is the Father's will?'. And we may keep the feasts of our own calendar, Christmas and Easter among them, while asking whether we have come to the one they point to.

#### John 7:3–5 · His Brethren's Dare
**Says:** "His brethren therefore said unto him, Depart hence, and go into Judaea, that thy disciples also may see the works that thou doest." (John 7:3) "For there is no man that doeth any thing in secret, and he himself seeketh to be known openly. If thou do these things, shew thyself to the world." (John 7:4) "For neither did his brethren believe in him." (John 7:5) *Hence* means from here; *shew* is the old spelling of 'show'.

**Means:** Jesus' 'brethren' are named elsewhere as 'James, and Joses, and Simon, and Judas' (Matthew 13:55). Christians have long disagreed about whether they were younger sons of Joseph and Mary, sons of Joseph by an earlier marriage, or cousins; the text of John 7 does not settle it, but it shows them as members of his household who knew him well. Their advice sounds sensible, even supportive: take your works to the capital, where your disciples (perhaps the ones who had drifted away in John 6:66) can see them. No one who wants to be known 'doeth any thing in secret'. The word 'openly' is *parrēsia* (G3954, John 7:4), which the STEPBible lexicon gives as 'freedom of speech, plainness, openness', and lists as meaning 'openly, plainly' in this verse and elsewhere in John; the word returns in John 7:13 and 7:26. 'Shew thyself' is *phanerōson* (G5319, John 7:4, V-AAM-2S), 'make yourself visible', an aorist command: do it now.

But John exposes their motive in one line: 'For neither did his brethren believe in him'. This is not faith urging Jesus forward; it is unbelief, perhaps half mocking, perhaps hoping for the family's advantage, and in either case treating Jesus as a performer who needs publicity. It is the same temptation Satan offered in the wilderness, to prove himself in a public spectacle at the temple (Matthew 4:5–6). Psalm 69, a psalm John quotes of Jesus in John 2:17, says, "I am become a stranger unto my brethren, and an alien unto my mother's children." (Psalm 69:8)

**Asks:** Unbelief can sound like encouragement. Advice that tells us to make ourselves known, to use our gifts for exposure, or to force God's hand should be tested by whether it comes from faith. And if your own family does not believe, you are not alone: neither did his.

#### John 7:6–9 · My Time Is Not Yet Come
**Says:** "Then Jesus said unto them, My time is not yet come: but your time is alway ready." (John 7:6) "The world cannot hate you; but me it hateth, because I testify of it, that the works thereof are evil." (John 7:7) "Go ye up unto this feast: I go not up yet unto this feast; for my time is not yet full come." (John 7:8) "When he had said these words unto them, he abode still in Galilee." (John 7:9) *Alway* means always; *abode still* means stayed on.

**Means:** Jesus answers with the word *kairos* (G2540, John 7:6), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'a fixed and definite period, time, season', and also 'opportune or seasonable time'. Outside the disputed verse John 5:4, John uses it only here, in John 7:6 and 7:8. It is close to 'hour' (John 2:4; 7:30), but not identical: the brothers are asking about the right moment to go up to the feast and make a public show, and Jesus answers that the right moment for his public self-disclosure is set by the Father, and it has not come. The brothers, by contrast, can go any time: 'your time is alway ready'. They are not on any divine timetable, because they are at home in the world.

That is the point of verse 7. 'The world cannot hate you' is not a compliment. The world, which in John means humanity organised in rebellion against God, has no quarrel with those who belong to it (John 15:19). It hates Jesus because he testifies 'that the works thereof are evil' (compare John 3:19–20). When Jesus goes public, it will be to die.

Verse 8 has puzzled readers since the early church. The KJV reads 'I go not up yet unto this feast', and then Jesus does go up (John 7:10). The word 'yet' is *oupō* (G3768, John 7:8), 'not yet', and the Textus Receptus behind the KJV has it here, as do many ancient manuscripts; some others read simply 'not' (see the Deep Dive and the Council's first question). With 'yet', there is no contradiction at all: Jesus does not go up with the pilgrim caravan, at their bidding, to show himself; he goes up later, quietly. Even with 'not', the next clause explains the sense: 'my time is not yet full come', *peplērōtai* (G4137, John 7:8, V-RPI-3S), 'has not been fulfilled'. He will not go up to this feast in the way they mean, as the moment of public self-manifestation. That going up will come at a later feast, the Passover of his death (John 12:12–13, 23).

**Asks:** Our time is 'alway ready' when we live for ourselves. The believer learns to wait for the Father's time, even when others think we are wasting our opportunity. And if the world is entirely comfortable with us, we should ask whether we are testifying to anything.

#### John 7:10–13 · In Secret, and the Murmuring Crowd
**Says:** "But when his brethren were gone up, then went he also up unto the feast, not openly, but as it were in secret." (John 7:10) "Then the Jews sought him at the feast, and said, Where is he?" (John 7:11) "And there was much murmuring among the people concerning him: for some said, He is a good man: others said, Nay; but he deceiveth the people." (John 7:12) "Howbeit no man spake openly of him for fear of the Jews." (John 7:13) *Nay* means no; *howbeit* means however.

**Means:** Jesus goes up, but 'not openly, but as it were in secret': the very opposite of what his brothers urged (John 7:4). He is not deceiving them; he is refusing their programme. Meanwhile 'the Jews', here the authorities, are looking for him: 'Where is he?' Their search is hostile.

The crowds murmur. The word is *gongusmos* (G1112, John 7:12), 'a murmuring, muttering', which the STEPBible lexicon notes the Greek Old Testament uses for Israel's murmuring in the wilderness (Exodus 16:7–9; Numbers 17:5, 10). At a feast that remembers the wilderness, the murmuring has come back. Two verdicts go round. 'He is a good man', *agathos* (G18, John 7:12): kind, well-meaning, but only a man. And 'he deceiveth the people', *plana* (G4105, John 7:12, V-PAI-3S), from *planaō*, 'to lead astray, deceive'. Deuteronomy 13:1–5 commanded death for a prophet who led Israel astray to other gods, and the charge of leading people astray is serious. Later Jewish writings remember Jesus as one who 'led Israel astray' (Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 43a, verify). Neither verdict is right: he is not merely good, and he is not a deceiver. And no one says either aloud: 'no man spake openly of him', *parrēsia* again, 'for fear of the Jews'.

**Asks:** Two failures are possible toward Jesus. One is to call him a deceiver; the other is to call him 'a good man' and stop there. And both can be held in whispers. Where does fear keep us from speaking openly of him?

#### John 7:14–18 · My Doctrine Is Not Mine
**Says:** "Now about the midst of the feast Jesus went up into the temple, and taught." (John 7:14) "And the Jews marvelled, saying, How knoweth this man letters, having never learned?" (John 7:15) "Jesus answered them, and said, My doctrine is not mine, but his that sent me." (John 7:16) "If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine, whether it be of God, or whether I speak of myself." (John 7:17) "He that speaketh of himself seeketh his own glory: but he that seeketh his glory that sent him, the same is true, and no unrighteousness is in him." (John 7:18) *Midst* means middle; *letters* here means learning.

**Means:** Halfway through the week, the man who went up in secret appears in the temple courts teaching. The authorities are astonished: 'How knoweth this man letters, having never learned?' 'Letters' is *grammata* (G1121, John 7:15), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as written characters and writings, and in this setting means learning, the kind of scholarship in the Scriptures that came from years at a recognised teacher's feet. 'Having never learned' is *memathēkōs* (G3129, John 7:15, V-RAP-NSM): he had not been a formal student. Peter and John met the same astonishment later, 'unlearned and ignorant men', and the council 'took knowledge of them, that they had been with Jesus' (Acts 4:13).

Jesus answers that his teaching did not come from a school, nor from himself: 'My doctrine is not mine, but his that sent me'. 'Doctrine' is *didachēs* (G1322, John 7:17), 'teaching, that which is taught'. Then he gives a test that turns the question around: 'If any man will do his will, he shall know'. 'Will do' is not a simple future; it is *thelē* (G2309, John 7:17, V-PAS-3S), 'if anyone wills, or wants', to do God's will. The condition for recognising God's truth is not first intelligence or training but a will that is ready to obey God. The knowing follows: *gnōsetai* (G1097, John 7:17, V-FDI-3S), 'he shall know'.

Verse 18 gives a second test: the direction of a teacher's glory. Whoever speaks from himself seeks his own glory; whoever seeks the glory of the one who sent him 'is true', *alēthēs* (G227, John 7:18), 'and no unrighteousness is in him', *adikia* (G93, John 7:18). Jesus speaks of himself in the third person, and the last clause is remarkable: no unrighteousness at all. It is a claim of sinlessness, and he makes it in public, to his enemies (compare John 8:46).

**Asks:** We come to know that Jesus' teaching is from God not by standing over it as judges but by being willing to do God's will. Ask not only 'Is this true?' but 'Am I willing to obey it if it is?'. And measure every teacher, including yourself, by one question: whose glory does this serve?

#### John 7:19–24 · Righteous Judgment
**Says:** "Did not Moses give you the law, and yet none of you keepeth the law? Why go ye about to kill me?" (John 7:19) "The people answered and said, Thou hast a devil: who goeth about to kill thee?" (John 7:20) "Jesus answered and said unto them, I have done one work, and ye all marvel." (John 7:21) "Moses therefore gave unto you circumcision; (not because it is of Moses, but of the fathers;) and ye on the sabbath day circumcise a man." (John 7:22) "If a man on the sabbath day receive circumcision, that the law of Moses should not be broken; are ye angry at me, because I have made a man every whit whole on the sabbath day?" (John 7:23) "Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment." (John 7:24) *A devil* means a demon; *every whit* means entirely.

**Means:** Jesus goes on the offensive. They pride themselves on Moses' law, and they are plotting murder, which the law forbids (Exodus 20:13). The crowd, many of them pilgrims who did not know the leaders' plans, reply, 'Thou hast a devil': you are possessed, or mad, to think anyone wants to kill you. John's readers know better (John 5:18; 7:1, 25).

'I have done one work' refers back to the healing at Bethesda on the sabbath (John 5:1–16), the 'one work' that set the authorities against him. Jesus answers with their own practice. Circumcision was commanded on the eighth day (Leviticus 12:3), and it went back before Moses to Abraham ('of the fathers', Genesis 17:10–12). When the eighth day fell on a sabbath, they circumcised anyway, so that the law of circumcision would not be broken; later rabbinic writings record this rule (Mishnah, *Shabbat* 18.3 and 19.2, verify). So the law itself allowed a work on the sabbath that dealt with one member of a man's body. Jesus argues from the lesser to the greater, a form of argument the rabbis also used: if circumcision may be done on the sabbath, how much more may a whole man be made well? 'Every whit whole' is *holon* (G3650, John 7:23) *anthrōpon* (G444, John 7:23) *hugiē* (G5199, John 7:23): a whole man healthy.

Then comes the command: 'Judge not according to the appearance', *opsin* (G3799, John 7:24), which the STEPBible lexicon lists as 'appearance', with this verse as its example of judging by appearance, 'but judge righteous judgment'. The first verb, *krinete* (G2919, John 7:24, V-PAM-2P), is a present imperative with 'not': stop judging by the surface. The second, in the Textus Receptus, is an aorist, *krinate* (G2919, John 7:24, V-AAM-2P): make a right judgment. 'Righteous judgment' is *dikaian* (G1342, John 7:24) *krisin* (G2920, John 7:24), a verdict that is just, one that matches the truth of the case and not its surface. The crowd judged by the surface: a man carrying his bed on the sabbath (John 5:10). Righteous judgment would have seen a man made whole by God. The command echoes the Old Testament's picture of the Messiah, who 'shall not judge after the sight of his eyes' (Isaiah 11:3), and the LORD's word to Samuel, "for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart." (1 Samuel 16:7) It is not a ban on judging, as 'Judge not' is sometimes misread (Matthew 7:1); it is a command to judge rightly.

**Asks:** It is possible to be scrupulous about rules and blind to mercy, to keep the letter and plan a murder. Before we condemn anyone, we must look past the surface of what they did to what God's law is actually for, and past what they seem to be to what is true.

#### John 7:25–27 · Is Not This He?
**Says:** "Then said some of them of Jerusalem, Is not this he, whom they seek to kill?" (John 7:25) "But, lo, he speaketh boldly, and they say nothing unto him. Do the rulers know indeed that this is the very Christ?" (John 7:26) "Howbeit we know this man whence he is: but when Christ cometh, no man knoweth whence he is." (John 7:27) *Lo* means look; *whence* means from where.

**Means:** Unlike the pilgrims of verse 20, the people of Jerusalem know about the plot: 'Is not this he, whom they seek to kill?' They are puzzled that he speaks 'boldly', *parrēsia* once more, and that no one stops him. Have the rulers privately concluded he is 'the very Christ'? The question expects the answer no, and they dismiss it at once: 'we know this man whence he is'. They mean Nazareth in Galilee (John 1:45–46). But there was a belief, which some later writings reflect, that the Messiah would be hidden until he suddenly appeared, so that 'no man knoweth whence he is' (compare Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 8, verify). A man with a known hometown did not fit.

The irony is thick. They think they know where he is from, and they know only half. John's readers know he was born in Bethlehem (John 7:42), and, far more, that he came from the Father (John 1:1, 14; 7:29). 'Whence' is *pothen* (G4159, John 7:28), and it is one of John's great questions: Pilate will ask Jesus the same thing, and get no answer (John 19:9).

**Asks:** It is easy to dismiss Jesus because we think we already know all about him: a teacher from Nazareth, a figure from Sunday school, a character in history. The question is whether we know where he really came from.

#### John 7:28–31 · I Am from Him
**Says:** "Then cried Jesus in the temple as he taught, saying, Ye both know me, and ye know whence I am: and I am not come of myself, but he that sent me is true, whom ye know not." (John 7:28) "But I know him: for I am from him, and he hath sent me." (John 7:29) "Then they sought to take him: but no man laid hands on him, because his hour was not yet come." (John 7:30) "And many of the people believed on him, and said, When Christ cometh, will he do more miracles than these which this man hath done?" (John 7:31)

**Means:** 'Then cried Jesus': *Ekraxen* (G2896, John 7:28, V-AAI-3S), from *krazō*, which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'to cry, call out with a loud voice', and notes is used 'of public teaching', citing John 1:15, where the Baptist 'cried' his witness. Jesus is not whispering; he is proclaiming. 'Ye both know me, and ye know whence I am' may be said with irony (you think you know), or as a concession (you know my earthly origin), and then he corrects them: 'I am not come of myself'. The one who sent him 'is true', *alēthinos* (G228, John 7:28), 'real, genuine', 'whom ye know not'. That is the sharpest word in the chapter to men who prided themselves on knowing God. Jesus alone knows him: 'for I am from him, and he hath sent me'.

They try to arrest him, 'but no man laid hands on him, because his hour was not yet come'. The word is *hōra* (G5610, John 7:30), and the verb is a pluperfect, *elēluthei* (G2064, John 7:30, V-2LAI-3S), 'had not yet come'. It is the narrator's explanation, and it is theological, not tactical: no one could touch him because God had not appointed it (John 2:4; 8:20; then 12:23; 13:1; 17:1). At the same time many believed, reasoning from his signs, *sēmeia* (G4592, John 7:31), which the KJV calls 'miracles': the Christ could hardly do more. Their verb is *episteusan* (G4100, John 7:31, V-AAI-3P), 'believed', and yet their faith rests on counting signs, the kind of faith Jesus did not trust in John 2:23–25.

**Asks:** Jesus' enemies were not in control, and neither are ours. The believer can rest in the Father's timing, knowing that nothing touches Christ or his people before the Father allows it. And the faith of the many in verse 31, built on miracles, is real but needs to grow into knowledge of where he came from.

#### John 7:32–36 · Yet a Little While
**Says:** "The Pharisees heard that the people murmured such things concerning him; and the Pharisees and the chief priests sent officers to take him." (John 7:32) "Then said Jesus unto them, Yet a little while am I with you, and then I go unto him that sent me." (John 7:33) "Ye shall seek me, and shall not find me: and where I am, thither ye cannot come." (John 7:34) "Then said the Jews among themselves, Whither will he go, that we shall not find him? will he go unto the dispersed among the Gentiles, and teach the Gentiles?" (John 7:35) "What manner of saying is this that he said, Ye shall seek me, and shall not find me: and where I am, thither ye cannot come?" (John 7:36) *Thither* means there; *whither* means to where.

**Means:** The Pharisees and 'the chief priests', the high-priestly families who controlled the temple, now act together and send 'officers', *hupēretai* (G5257, John 7:46), the temple police, whom the STEPBible lexicon describes as 'officers of the Synagogue or Sanhedrin'. Jesus answers not with fear but with a calm prophecy of his departure: 'Yet a little while am I with you, and then I go unto him that sent me'. He will go to the Father by way of the cross, the resurrection, and the ascension. 'Ye shall seek me, and shall not find me' is a solemn warning: there will come a time when they look for him, perhaps for a deliverer in the disasters to come, and he will be beyond their reach (compare John 8:21, where he adds 'and shall die in your sins').

The Jews misunderstand, as characters in John so often do: perhaps he will go 'unto the dispersed among the Gentiles', *diasporan* (G1290, John 7:35), the Jewish communities scattered through the Greek-speaking world, 'and teach the Gentiles', *Hellēnas* (G1672, John 7:35), the Greeks. They mean it as a sneer. John's readers know it came true: after Jesus went to the Father, his word did go to the Greeks, and some of them came asking to see him even before the cross (John 12:20–21). John's own Gospel, written in Greek, is part of the answer to their question.

**Asks:** There is a 'little while' in which Christ may be found (Isaiah 55:6). It is not endless. And the misunderstanding of Jesus' enemies can become, in God's hands, the very plan of salvation for the nations.

#### John 7:37–39 · If Any Man Thirst
**Says:** "In the last day, that great day of the feast, Jesus stood and cried, saying, If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink." (John 7:37) "He that believeth on me, as the scripture hath said, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water." (John 7:38) "(But this spake he of the Spirit, which they that believe on him should receive: for the Holy Ghost was not yet given; because that Jesus was not yet glorified.)" (John 7:39) *Belly* here means the inmost being; the *Holy Ghost* is the Holy Spirit.

**Means:** 'The last day, that great day of the feast' is either the seventh day, the climax of the seven days of the feast proper, or the eighth day, the 'holy convocation' and 'solemn assembly' that closed it (Leviticus 23:36; Nehemiah 8:18). Scholars are divided, and the text does not settle it (see the Deep Dive). Either way, the ceremonies of the week, which in later Jewish tradition included the daily pouring of water from Siloam (verify), are ending, and Jesus 'stood and cried'. Teachers usually sat (Luke 4:20; John 8:2); Jesus stands, like a herald, and cries aloud, *ekraxen* (G2896, John 7:37, V-AAI-3S), the same public cry as in John 7:28.

His words are an invitation to anyone: 'If any man thirst', *dipsa* (G1372, John 7:37, V-PAS-3S), which the STEPBible lexicon lists as used figuratively here, as in John 4:14 and 6:35 and in Revelation's last invitation (Revelation 22:17). 'Let him come', *erchesthō* (G2064, John 7:37, V-PNM-3S), 'and drink', *pinetō* (G4095, John 7:37, V-PAM-3S): two present imperatives. The only qualification is thirst. It is the invitation of Isaiah: "Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters, and he that hath no money; come ye, buy, and eat; yea, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price." (Isaiah 55:1) Jesus stands in the place of the LORD who spoke those words.

Then the promise: 'He that believeth on me', *pisteuōn* (G4100, John 7:38, V-PAP-NSM), the present participle of continuing trust; 'out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water'. 'Belly' is *koilias* (G2836, John 7:38); the STEPBible lexicon lists this verse under a third, metaphorical sense of the word, 'of the heart', like the Hebrew word for the inward parts. 'Rivers' is *potamoi* (G4215, John 7:38), which the lexicon lists as a figurative use in this verse; 'shall flow' is *rheusousin* (G4482, John 7:38, V-FAI-3P), a future; and 'living water' is *hudatos* (G5204, John 7:38) *zōntos* (G2198, John 7:38, V-PAP-GSN), literally 'water that lives', which in ordinary speech meant running spring water rather than water standing in a cistern (Jeremiah 2:13). The 'belly' may be the believer's (the KJV's punctuation) or Christ's (see the View A / View B panel and the Council). 'As the scripture hath said' points not to one verse but to a cluster of promises: water from the rock (Exodus 17:6; Psalm 78:16), water poured on the thirsty and the Spirit on Israel's seed (Isaiah 44:3), the believer 'like a spring of water' (Isaiah 58:11), the river flowing from the temple (Ezekiel 47:1–12), and living waters from Jerusalem (Zechariah 14:8), a chapter that goes on to speak of the nations keeping this very feast (Zechariah 14:16).

John then steps in with one of his narrator's explanations. 'This spake he of the Spirit, which they that believe on him should receive'. The living water is the Holy Spirit. And 'the Holy Ghost was not yet given; because that Jesus was not yet glorified'. In John, Jesus is 'glorified', *edoxasthē* (G1392, John 7:39, V-API-3S), in his death, resurrection, and return to the Father (John 12:16, 23; 17:1, 5). The Spirit had been at work throughout the Old Testament; but the Spirit as the gift of the glorified Christ, poured out on all his people, awaited the cross and the ascension (John 16:7; 20:22; Acts 2:33).

**Asks:** The only thing you need to bring to Christ is your thirst. Come, and keep coming; drink, and keep drinking. And do not imagine you are meant to be a cistern, holding the Spirit for yourself: the life Christ gives flows out through those who believe, to others.

#### John 7:40–44 · A Division Because of Him
**Says:** "Many of the people therefore, when they heard this saying, said, Of a truth this is the Prophet." (John 7:40) "Others said, This is the Christ. But some said, Shall Christ come out of Galilee?" (John 7:41) "Hath not the scripture said, That Christ cometh of the seed of David, and out of the town of Bethlehem, where David was?" (John 7:42) "So there was a division among the people because of him." (John 7:43) "And some of them would have taken him; but no man laid hands on him." (John 7:44)

**Means:** Jesus' cry forces a verdict. Some say, 'this is the Prophet', *prophētēs* (G4396, John 7:40), the Prophet like Moses whom Moses promised (Deuteronomy 18:15; compare John 1:21; 6:14). A man who promised water at the feast that remembered the wilderness would remind them of Moses at the rock. Others say, 'This is the Christ'. Others object from Scripture: the Christ is to be David's descendant (2 Samuel 7:12) and from Bethlehem (Micah 5:2), and this man comes from Galilee.

The objection is correct about the Scriptures and wrong about Jesus. Matthew and Luke tell us he was born in Bethlehem and was of David's line (Matthew 2:1–6; Luke 2:4). John does not stop to correct the crowd, and some modern scholars have concluded that John did not know of the birth in Bethlehem; but it is more likely that John is using irony again, as in John 7:27, trusting his readers to know what the crowd does not (see the Council's second question). The result is *schisma* (G4978, John 7:43), a 'rent', as in cloth, and figuratively a 'dissension, division', a word John uses again at John 9:16 and 10:19. Jesus divides people. Some want to seize him; but again, 'no man laid hands on him'.

**Asks:** No one can stay neutral about Jesus for long. And sincere knowledge of the Bible does not save anyone from misjudging him, if it is not humble enough to ask what it does not yet know.

#### John 7:45–49 · Never Man Spake Like This Man
**Says:** "Then came the officers to the chief priests and Pharisees; and they said unto them, Why have ye not brought him?" (John 7:45) "The officers answered, Never man spake like this man." (John 7:46) "Then answered them the Pharisees, Are ye also deceived?" (John 7:47) "Have any of the rulers or of the Pharisees believed on him?" (John 7:48) "But this people who knoweth not the law are cursed." (John 7:49)

**Means:** The officers, *hupēretai* (G5257, John 7:46), come back empty-handed. They were sent to arrest a man, and they heard him instead. Their answer is the best line in the chapter: 'Never man spake like this man'. They were not theologians, and they were risking their posts; they simply report what hearing him did to them. The Pharisees answer with contempt: 'Are ye also deceived?', the same verb as the crowd's charge in John 7:12, here *peplanēsthe* (G4105, John 7:47, V-RPI-2P), a perfect passive: 'have you too been led astray?' The STEPBible lexicon lists John 7:47 under the passive sense, 'to be led astray, to err'. Their argument is an appeal to authority: 'Have any of the rulers or of the Pharisees believed on him?' The answer, as the next verse shows, is yes: one of them is standing in the room (and later others, John 12:42).

Then comes their verdict on the crowd: 'this people who knoweth not the law are cursed'. The word is *epikataratoi* (G1944, John 7:49), 'accursed', the word Paul uses for those under the law's curse in Galatians 3:10. Later rabbinic writings speak with similar scorn of 'the people of the land', ordinary Jews who did not keep the Pharisees' strict traditions (verify the sources). The irony is severe: the rulers who curse the crowd for ignorance of the law are about to be shown ignorant of their own law (John 7:51) and of their own prophets (John 7:52).

**Asks:** Plain people who have heard Jesus often know more than experts who have not. Beware of any spirit, in church or out of it, that despises 'ordinary' believers, and of any argument whose only ground is 'none of the important people believe it'.

#### John 7:50–53 · Before It Hear Him
**Says:** "Nicodemus saith unto them, (he that came to Jesus by night, being one of them,)" (John 7:50) "Doth our law judge any man, before it hear him, and know what he doeth?" (John 7:51) "They answered and said unto him, Art thou also of Galilee? Search, and look: for out of Galilee ariseth no prophet." (John 7:52) "And every man went unto his own house." (John 7:53)

**Means:** Nicodemus is 'one of them', a Pharisee and a ruler (John 3:1). John reminds us that he once 'came to Jesus by night' (John 3:2). Now he speaks up, carefully: he does not confess faith, but he asks a question of law. 'Doth our law judge any man, before it hear him', *akousē* (G191, John 7:51), 'and know what he doeth?', *gnō* (G1097, John 7:51)? The law required judges to hear both sides and judge righteously (Deuteronomy 1:16–17), and to condemn only on the testimony of witnesses (Deuteronomy 17:6). The rulers who called the crowd ignorant of the law are breaking it. And Nicodemus's question echoes Jesus' command in John 7:24.

They answer with ridicule, not law: 'Art thou also of Galilee?', as though only a Galilean yokel could take Jesus seriously. 'Search, and look': *eraunēson* (G2045, John 7:52, V-AAM-2S), the same verb Jesus used in 'Search the scriptures' (John 5:39). 'For out of Galilee ariseth no prophet'. But their search was careless. Jonah came from Gath-hepher (2 Kings 14:25), a town in the tribe of Zebulun in Galilee (Joshua 19:13, where the KJV spells it Gittah-hepher), and Isaiah had promised that Galilee of the nations would see 'a great light' (Isaiah 9:1–2; Matthew 4:15–16). Some early manuscripts read 'the prophet' here, which would make the rulers' claim narrower, that the Prophet of Deuteronomy 18 could not come from Galilee (verify).

John 7:53, 'And every man went unto his own house', closes the scene. It is also the first verse of the passage about the woman taken in adultery (John 7:53–8:11), which the KJV prints and this study teaches as printed, though many early manuscripts do not contain it (see the Deep Dive and the Council's third question; the passage itself is studied in the next lesson). Read as it stands, the verse is quietly eloquent: the council breaks up with nothing decided, and every man goes home, while Jesus, as the next verse says, goes to the mount of Olives (John 8:1). The man who has no home of his own among them (Matthew 8:20) is the one who has just offered them living water.

**Asks:** Nicodemus's courage is small, but it is real, and it grows: he will later come openly with myrrh and aloes to bury Jesus (John 19:39). Ask for a fair hearing for Christ, and for others, even when it costs you a sneer. And do not be the council that goes home with its mind unchanged after the Son of God has spoken in the temple.

### 🧨 Cultural Landmines and Misconceptions
**Tabernacles as tents in a church.** When modern readers hear 'tabernacles', they may think of the tabernacle in the wilderness (the tent of meeting), or of a church building, or of a tabernacle on a Catholic altar. The feast of John 7:2 is the feast of booths: families living in rough shelters of branches for a week (Leviticus 23:42; Nehemiah 8:14–17). It was a harvest festival with branches, lights, music, and rejoicing, closer to a national week-long camp-out than to a solemn service, and it remembered God's care in the wilderness.

**'The Jews' as the Jewish people.** In John 7 the phrase usually means the Judean religious authorities: the crowds are afraid 'of the Jews' (John 7:13), though the crowds are Jewish too, and so are Jesus, his brothers, the officers, and Nicodemus. John is not blaming a race; he is describing a group of leaders and the unbelief of 'the world' (John 7:7), which is found in every nation. Christians who have used John's language to despise Jewish people have not read it rightly, and the Gospel's own Saviour is a Jew who says 'salvation is of the Jews' (John 4:22).

**Galilee as backwater.** Modern readers often picture Galilee as a remote, uneducated region. It was fertile, populous, and crossed by trade routes, but to Jerusalem's religious elite it could look provincial, mixed with Gentiles, and lax in keeping the law (Isaiah 9:1 calls it 'Galilee of the nations'). The rulers' sneer 'Art thou also of Galilee?' (John 7:52) is regional snobbery, not a sober claim of Scripture.

**'He never went to seminary.'** Some take John 7:15 as proof that education is unnecessary or suspect for Christian teachers. The point of the verse is not that learning is bad but that Jesus' teaching came directly from the Father (John 7:16). Paul was highly trained (Acts 22:3); Peter and John were not (Acts 4:13). God uses both; the test is whether the teacher seeks the glory of the one who sent him (John 7:18).

**The Holy Spirit as a force or a feeling.** Because John 7 pictures the Spirit as water, some think of him as an impersonal power or a religious feeling, something to be tapped or turned up. The same Gospel calls the Spirit 'he' and describes him teaching, testifying, and guiding (John 14:26; 15:26; 16:13). Water is a picture of what he does, bringing life, not a description of what he is.

**The Holy Spirit absent from the Old Testament.** 'The Holy Ghost was not yet given' (John 7:39) is sometimes read as if the Spirit did not exist or never worked before Pentecost. But the Spirit hovered at creation (Genesis 1:2), filled craftsmen and prophets, and David prayed, 'take not thy holy spirit from me' (Psalm 51:11). John means the Spirit given as the gift of the glorified Christ, in a new fullness and to all his people (see the Debate Corner).

## 🔴 Tier 3 — Expert
### 🏗️ Theological Framework
John 7 is a chapter of controversy, and its theology comes through Jesus' answers to questions about his authority, his origin, and his timing, and through the narrator's one great comment on the Spirit. Read through the Reformed conviction that Scripture interprets Scripture, it bears on six areas.

*The Son's relation to the Father.* 'My doctrine is not mine, but his that sent me' (John 7:16), and 'I am from him, and he hath sent me' (John 7:29). As in John 5:19–30, the Son does nothing and says nothing from himself, and this is not a sign of inferiority in nature but of the order of the persons within the one God: the Son is 'from' the Father, eternally begotten, and 'sent' by him in time. The Reformed confessions speak of the Son 'eternally begotten of the Father' and the Father 'of none' (Westminster Confession, chapter 2, section 3, paraphrased, verify). John 7:28–29 adds that true knowledge of the Father is found only in the Son: 'whom ye know not. But I know him' (compare John 1:18; Matthew 11:27).

*The sinlessness of Christ.* 'He that seeketh his glory that sent him, the same is true, and no unrighteousness is in him' (John 7:18). The principle is general, but Jesus applies it to himself, and no other teacher could claim it without qualification. Taken with John 8:46 and Hebrews 4:15, it undergirds the church's confession that Christ was without sin, the necessary qualification of a Saviour who dies for others' sins.

*Providence and the hour.* 'His hour was not yet come' (John 7:30; compare John 7:6, 8, 44). John presents the life and death of Christ as governed in every detail by God's decree, while those who plot against him act freely and are responsible for their plots (John 7:19). This is the pattern of Acts 2:23 and 4:27–28, and it is what the Westminster Confession, chapter 5, means by providence ordering all things, including the free acts of creatures, without God being the author of sin (paraphrased, verify).

*Pneumatology and redemptive history.* John 7:39 is one of the key texts for the relation between the Spirit's work before and after Pentecost. Reformed theology distinguishes the Spirit's saving work in all ages (every believer in every age is born of the Spirit, John 3:5–8, which Jesus expected Nicodemus, 'a master of Israel', to know, John 3:10) from the Spirit's being 'given' as the gift of the exalted Christ in the new covenant, poured out on all flesh (Joel 2:28; Acts 2:17, 33), abiding in believers (John 14:16–17), and uniting Jew and Gentile in one body (1 Corinthians 12:13). The difference is one of measure, manner, and scope, grounded in Christ's finished work, not a difference between salvation with the Spirit and salvation without him. The Debate Corner argues this.

*The knowledge of God and the will.* 'If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine' (John 7:17). Reformed theology has read this as teaching that the knowledge of God is not merely intellectual: sin darkens the understanding because it perverts the will (Romans 1:18, 21; Ephesians 4:18), and the Spirit who renews the will also opens the mind. Calvin writes of the Spirit's inward testimony by which believers are persuaded that Scripture is God's word (*Institutes* 1.7.4–5, verify). The text does not teach that obedience earns knowledge; it teaches that a will surrendered to God is the posture in which God's truth is recognised.

*Righteous judgment and the law.* John 7:19–24 and 7:51 set Jesus and Nicodemus as the true interpreters of Moses against those who claimed to be his defenders. The law's own intent (mercy, wholeness, fair hearing) is its right reading (Matthew 23:23). This is an early example of what the Reformers called the spiritual sense of the law, that it reaches the heart and aims at love, not merely at outward observance.

### 📚 Scholarly Insights
- **Augustine**, *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (Tractates 28–33, verify): reads Jesus' going up 'as it were in secret' as a picture of Christ hidden in the Old Testament's feasts and figures, and of Christ hidden in his humility; on John 7:17 he makes the point, in substance, that understanding is the reward of faith, so that we believe in order to understand (Tractate 29, verify); and on John 7:37–39 he identifies the living water with the love of God poured into the heart by the Holy Spirit (Romans 5:5) (verify the particulars).
- **John Chrysostom**, *Homilies on the Gospel of John* (Homilies 48–52, verify): presses the brothers' unbelief as a warning that kinship with Christ does not save, and praises the officers, who went to arrest him and were themselves taken captive by his words (verify).
- **Origen**, *Commentary on John* (verify the book): among the Greek writers who read John 7:38 of the believer as the source of rivers, once filled by Christ (verify).
- **Jerome**, *Dialogue against the Pelagians* (book 2, verify): reports that the pagan philosopher Porphyry accused Jesus of inconstancy or lying because he said he would not go up to the feast and then went (verify). The charge shows that some early copies read 'not' rather than 'not yet'.
- **John Calvin**, *Commentary on the Gospel According to John*: reads the brothers' advice as the voice of carnal ambition that wants Christ's glory to serve the family's standing; takes John 7:17 to mean that those who sincerely desire to obey God will not be left in doubt about Christ's teaching; and reads the rivers of John 7:38 as the Spirit's gifts flowing from the believer to others, though always from Christ as the fountain (verify each).
- **J. C. Ryle**, *Expository Thoughts on the Gospels* (John): calls John 7:37–39 one of the most wide and full invitations in the Gospels, stressing that the only qualification is thirst; and draws from John 7:5 the lesson that the grace of God does not run in families (verify).
- **B. F. Westcott**, *The Gospel According to St. John* (1880): discusses the feast's water and light ceremonies as the background to John 7:37–38 and 8:12, while noting that the Mishnah is later than the Gospel (verify).
- **Leon Morris**, *The Gospel According to John* (New International Commentary, 1971): argues that the great day is probably the seventh day, when the water ceremony reached its climax, while giving the case for the eighth (verify); defends the KJV's punctuation of John 7:37–38 (verify).
- **D. A. Carson**, *The Gospel According to John* (Pillar New Testament Commentary, 1991): discusses the punctuation of John 7:37–38 at length and prefers the reading in which the rivers flow from the believer, while acknowledging the strength of the christological reading (verify); reads John 7:39 as the redemptive-historical distinction between the old covenant and the new, not a denial that the Spirit worked earlier (verify).
- **Raymond E. Brown**, *The Gospel According to John* (Anchor Bible, 1966): prefers the christological punctuation, so that the rivers flow from Jesus, and connects it with John 19:34 (verify); argues that John 7:53–8:11 is not part of the original Gospel but may be an ancient and authentic story (verify).
- **Rudolf Bultmann**, *The Gospel of John* (German original 1941): proposed rearranging the order of chapters 5–7 so that John 7:15–24 follows John 5 (verify). No manuscript supports the rearrangement, and John 7:21–23 reads naturally as Jesus returning, at his first public appearance in Jerusalem since, to the controversy of his last.
- **Mishnah**, tractate *Sukkah* (compiled about AD 200), chapters 4–5: describes the water-drawing from Siloam, the libation at the altar, and the lamps in the Court of the Women, with the saying, in substance, that one who had not seen the joy of the water-drawing had never seen joy (verify). Because it was compiled more than a century after the temple fell, its details must be used with care as background to Jesus' day.

### ⚔️ Debate Corner
**The question:** What does John mean by 'the Holy Ghost was not yet given; because that Jesus was not yet glorified' (John 7:39), and what does it teach about how believers receive the Spirit?

**Position 1 · The Spirit received after believing, as a second gift of power.** Classical Pentecostal teaching (for example, the Assemblies of God's statement of fundamental truths, verify) and some Holiness traditions read John 7:39 alongside Acts 8:15–17 and 19:1–6 as showing that receiving the Spirit is distinct from, and may follow, believing: 'which they that believe on him should receive'. On this view, the disciples believed and were regenerate before Pentecost, yet had not 'received' the Spirit in the sense of John 7:39; the same pattern may be repeated in believers today, and the gift is a baptism of power for witness (Acts 1:8), often evidenced by tongues. They point to Paul's question, "Have ye received the Holy Ghost since ye believed?" (Acts 19:2) Holders of this view want to guard the church against a Spirit-less formalism and to press every believer to seek the Spirit's fullness.

**Position 2 · The Spirit did not dwell in believers before Pentecost.** Some writers, especially in dispensational traditions (verify particular authors), read John 7:39 with John 14:17 ('he dwelleth with you, and shall be in you') as teaching a sharp discontinuity: in the Old Testament the Spirit came upon particular people for particular tasks and could be withdrawn (1 Samuel 16:14; Psalm 51:11), but did not permanently indwell ordinary believers; from Pentecost onward he indwells every believer permanently. Holders of this view want to honour the newness of the new covenant and the uniqueness of the church.

**Position 3 · The Spirit given by the glorified Christ in new-covenant fullness to every believer.** Reformed theology (Calvin on John 7:39, verify; John Owen, *Pneumatologia*, verify; and many modern Reformed writers) holds that the Spirit regenerated and sanctified believers in every age, since no one in any age can see the kingdom without new birth (John 3:3, 5) and Jesus rebuked Nicodemus for not knowing it (John 3:10); but that the Spirit was 'not yet given' in the sense that the outpouring promised by the prophets (Isaiah 44:3; Joel 2:28) waited for Christ's glorification. When Christ was glorified, he received the Spirit from the Father and poured him out (Acts 2:33) on all his people, Jew and Gentile, in a fullness, permanence, and universality not known before. Every believer receives this gift in union with Christ: "Now if any man have not the Spirit of Christ, he is none of his." (Romans 8:9)

**Most consistent with Reformed hermeneutics.** Scripture interpreting Scripture favours Position 3. (1) John 7:39 grounds the 'not yet' in Christ, not in the believer: the Spirit was not yet given 'because that Jesus was not yet glorified'. The turning point is redemptive-historical, the cross and exaltation, not a second stage in each believer's experience. (2) In the same verse the recipients are simply 'they that believe on him', the same people as in John 7:38: the promise attaches to believing, not to a later experience. (3) Paul says that every believer has been 'made to drink into one Spirit' (1 Corinthians 12:13), the very language of John 7:37, and that anyone without the Spirit of Christ 'is none of his' (Romans 8:9). (4) The episodes in Acts 8 and 19 are unique moments in the gospel's spread to Samaria and to disciples of John the Baptist, and in Acts 2:38 and 10:44–47 the Spirit is given with faith; Acts presents them as the extension of Pentecost to new groups, not as a pattern for every believer. (5) Against Position 2, the Old Testament saints were born of the Spirit (John 3:10), David prayed that the holy spirit would not be taken from him (Psalm 51:11), and Paul says Abraham was justified by the same faith as we are (Romans 4:3, 23–24); what changed at Pentecost is the measure and manner of the Spirit's presence, as the gift of the glorified Mediator, not the Spirit's saving work itself.

Position 1 has a real insight, and the Reformed position keeps it: believers can grieve and quench the Spirit (Ephesians 4:30; 1 Thessalonians 5:19) and are commanded to 'be filled with the Spirit' (Ephesians 5:18), a present, repeated filling, so seeking the Spirit's fullness is right and necessary. Position 2 rightly honours the newness of Pentecost. But neither the fullness nor the newness divides believers into two classes.

**Pastoral Warning:** This debate can harm a congregation from either side. A church that holds Position 1 can divide believers into haves and have-nots, leave sincere Christians anxious that they have never 'received', and measure the Spirit by particular experiences rather than by faith in Christ and the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22–23). A church that holds Position 3 coldly can settle into a doctrine of the Spirit that is correct and dry: the rivers are affirmed on paper but nobody expects them to flow, nobody prays for the Spirit's fullness (Luke 11:13), and nobody is thirsty. Teach John 7:37–39 as Jesus spoke it: as an invitation to come and drink, to every believer, with the promise that the Spirit is given to all who believe, and with the command to keep coming, keep drinking, and let the rivers flow out to others. And do not question the salvation of brothers and sisters who describe their experience of the Spirit differently while trusting the same Christ.

### 🔤 Original Language Deep Dive
Every Greek and Hebrew definition below comes from one source: the brief lexicon published by STEPBible.org (Tyndale House, Cambridge), whose Greek entries are drawn largely from G. Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament*. It is called 'the STEPBible lexicon' below.

**The feast.** *Skēnopēgia* (G4634, John 7:2, N-NSF), a compound of *skēnē*, tent, and the verb for fixing or pitching. The STEPBible lexicon defines it as properly 'the setting up of tents or dwellings', and in the Greek Old Testament and the New Testament 'the feast of Tabernacles', noting that the Greek Old Testament uses it for the Hebrew *sukkah* (H5521) in Deuteronomy 16:16, 31:10, and Zechariah 14:16–19, and that it occurs in the New Testament only here. The Hebrew *sukkah* the lexicon glosses 'thicket, covert, booth', a rude or temporary shelter. John has already used the related verb for the incarnation: the Word 'dwelt among us' (John 1:14), literally 'pitched his tent'. At the feast of tents, the one who tabernacled among us stands in the temple.

**Time and hour.** *Kairos* (G2540, John 7:6, N-NSM) is, in the STEPBible lexicon, 'a fixed and definite period, time, season', and also 'opportune or seasonable time'. The word occurs 4 times in the Greek of John: once at John 5:4, and three in John 7:6–8. *Hōra* (G5610, John 7:30, N-NSF), 'hour', is John's word for the appointed hour of the cross (John 2:4; 12:23; 17:1). In John 7 both words say the same thing from two sides: the brothers want Jesus to seize an opportune moment; the narrator says the enemies could not seize him before God's appointed hour. The phrase 'not yet' occurs 5 times in this chapter (John 7:6, 8, 30, 39), and the chapter is full of waiting: for the time, for the hour, for the Spirit, for the glory.

**Openly.** *Parrēsia* (G3954, John 7:4, N-DSF), from *pas*, all, and *rhēsis*, speech: in the STEPBible lexicon 'freedom of speech, plainness, openness', and in the dative, used as an adverb, 'freely, openly, plainly', with John 7:13, 26 listed. 'Openly' occurs 3 times in the KJV of this chapter (John 7:4, 10, 13), and the Greek word stands behind the KJV's 'boldly' in John 7:26 as well. The chapter plays on it: the brothers want Jesus to act openly; he goes up 'not openly'; the crowd will not speak of him openly; and then he speaks 'boldly' in the temple (John 7:26). Openness at the wrong time, from the wrong motive, is display; openness at God's time, for God's glory, is witness. The same word describes the apostles' boldness after Pentecost (Acts 4:13, 29, 31), when the Spirit of John 7:39 had been given.

**Murmuring.** *Gongusmos* (G1112, John 7:12, N-NSM), 'a murmuring, muttering', occurs 4 times in the New Testament. The STEPBible lexicon notes its Greek Old Testament uses for the Hebrew words of Israel's murmuring in the wilderness (Exodus 16:7–9; Numbers 17:5, 10). The related verb appears at John 6:41, 43, 61 and again at John 7:32, where 'murmured' describes the crowd's whispered debates. The feast that remembered the wilderness saw the wilderness sin repeated.

**Letters and learning.** *Grammata* (G1121, John 7:15, N-APN), from *gramma*, 'that which is written', which the STEPBible lexicon gives as a written character, a document, and 'the sacred writings' in 2 Timothy 3:15; in John 7:15 it means learning in the Scriptures. *Memathēkōs* (G3129, John 7:15, V-RAP-NSM), a perfect participle of 'to learn': 'not having been a student'. The same verb appears in John 6:45, 'every man therefore that hath heard, and hath learned of the Father'. Jesus had not learned from the schools, but the Father's teaching is his own (John 7:16), and it is the only school that finally counts.

**Will.** *Thelē* (G2309, John 7:17, V-PAS-3S), 'wills, desires', with the noun *thelēma* (G2307, John 7:17), 'will'. The KJV's 'will do' is the English auxiliary; the Greek says 'if anyone wills to do his will'. The condition is the direction of the will, not the completion of obedience, which is why the verse cannot mean that knowledge is earned by works.

**True.** Two words for 'true' sit close together. *Alēthēs* (G227, John 7:18, A-NSM), 'true' as opposed to false: the one who seeks the sender's glory tells the truth. *Alēthinos* (G228, John 7:28, A-NSM), 'true' in the sense of real or genuine: the Father who sent Jesus is the real God, whom the rulers do not know (compare John 17:3). John's vocabulary lets him say both that Jesus' words are true and that the God he reveals is the true God.

**Cried.** *Ekraxen* (G2896, John 7:37, V-AAI-3S), from *krazō*. The STEPBible lexicon traces the word from the croak of a raven to any loud cry, and then 'to cry, call out with a loud voice', listing John 1:15 as a use 'of public teaching'. John uses it for the Baptist's witness (John 1:15) and for Jesus' public proclamations (John 7:28, 37; 12:44). The verb for 'stood' is a pluperfect, *heistēkei* (G2476, John 7:37, V-LAI-3S), 'had taken his stand': Jesus was standing, in place, as he cried out.

**Thirst, come, drink.** *Dipsa* (G1372, John 7:37, V-PAS-3S), 'thirsts', from *dipsaō*, which the STEPBible lexicon lists as used figuratively at John 4:14, 6:35, 7:37, and Revelation 7:16, 21:6, 22:17. The verbs of invitation are present imperatives, *erchesthō* (G2064, John 7:37, V-PNM-3S) and *pinetō* (G4095, John 7:37, V-PAM-3S): 'let him keep coming, and keep drinking' is a fair expansion of the tense, though the present imperative need not always stress repetition.

**Belly and rivers.** *Koilias* (G2836, John 7:38, N-GSF), from *koilia*, which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 1. the belly, 2. the womb, and 3. metaphorically, 'as Hebrew *beten*', 'of the heart', listing John 7:38 as its only example of the third sense. So 'belly' here is the inmost being, not the stomach. *Potamoi* (G4215, John 7:38, N-NPM), 'rivers', which the lexicon lists figuratively here, with 'rivers of living water'. *Rheusousin* (G4482, John 7:38, V-FAI-3P), 'shall flow', a future: the flowing lies ahead, after the Spirit is given (John 7:39). *Zōntos* (G2198, John 7:38, V-PAP-GSN), 'living', the participle of the verb 'to live'; with water, it is flowing water, as in the LORD's complaint that his people forsook 'the fountain of living waters' (Jeremiah 2:13), and it is the phrase of John 4:10.

**The punctuation of John 7:37–38.** The earliest manuscripts have little or no punctuation, so where one sentence ends and the next begins is a matter of interpretation. The KJV, with most Greek fathers (Origen among them, verify) and most English versions, ends the sentence after 'drink', so that 'He that believeth on me' begins verse 38 and the belly is the believer's. The alternative, found in some Latin fathers and early writers (Hippolytus and Cyprian have been cited, verify) and preferred by some modern scholars, ends the sentence after 'he that believeth on me', so that the invitation reads *let him come unto me, and let him that believeth on me drink*, and the belly is Christ's. In favour of the KJV's reading: the phrase 'he that believeth on me' begins sentences elsewhere in John (John 6:35; 12:44; 14:12); the Old Testament promises that God's people will become springs (Isaiah 58:11); and verse 39 speaks of the Spirit that believers 'should receive', not of Christ as the source. In favour of the other: it keeps a neat parallel ('if any man thirst, let him come; he that believeth, let him drink'), and it matches John's picture of water flowing from Christ (John 4:14; 19:34; Revelation 22:1). The two readings agree that the water comes from Christ and is the Spirit; they differ only on whether John 7:38 speaks of the Spirit's flowing out of Christ to the believer or out of the believer to others, and John 7:39 fits the KJV's reading more naturally.

**Which scripture?** No single Old Testament verse says 'out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water'. The KJV's 'as the scripture hath said' is *graphē* (G1124, John 7:38), singular, and John often uses the singular for a passage or for the Scripture's teaching as a whole. The likeliest background is a cluster of texts, many of them associated with the feast: the rock in the wilderness (Exodus 17:6; Numbers 20:11; Psalm 78:16, 'caused waters to run down like rivers'), the promise of water and the Spirit (Isaiah 44:3), the believer as 'a spring of water, whose waters fail not' (Isaiah 58:11), the river from the temple's threshold (Ezekiel 47:1–12), and the living waters of Zechariah 14:8, which the Mishnah associates with the feast (verify). Those who read the belly as Christ's add that Jesus is the new temple (John 2:19–21) from whom Ezekiel's river flows.

**Glorified.** *Edoxasthē* (G1392, John 7:39, V-API-3S), aorist passive of *doxazō*. The STEPBible lexicon lists as its third sense, 'in LXX and NT', 'to clothe with splendour, glorify', and cites John 7:39 as a use 'of Christ'. In John the glorification of the Son is his hour of death, resurrection, and return to the Father (John 12:16, 23; 13:31–32; 17:1, 5). The Spirit's giving waits on it.

**Division.** *Schisma* (G4978, John 7:43, N-NSN), from the verb 'to split'. The STEPBible lexicon gives 'a rent' (Matthew 9:16; Mark 2:21), and metaphorically 'a dissension, division', listing John 7:43, 9:16, 10:19 and the divisions at Corinth (1 Corinthians 1:10; 11:18; 12:25). It occurs 8 times in the New Testament. The word 'schism' comes from it. In John, the division is not a failure of Jesus' ministry but its effect: light divides those who come to it from those who hate it (John 3:19–21).

**Officers and cursed.** *Hupēretai* (G5257, John 7:46, N-NPM), which the STEPBible lexicon traces to 'an under rower', and then 'a servant, attendant, minister', including 'officers of the Synagogue or Sanhedrin', listing John 7:32, 45–46. *Epikataratoi* (G1944, John 7:49, A-NPM), 'accursed', the reading of the Textus Receptus; the STEPBible lexicon's entry lists Galatians 3:10, 13, where it describes those under the curse of the law, and many modern editions read a closely related word here with the same meaning (verify).

**Search.** *Eraunēson* (G2045, John 7:52, V-AAM-2S), from *ereunaō*, 'to search, examine', which the STEPBible lexicon lists at John 7:52 and at John 5:39 ('Search the scriptures'). The rulers' command to Nicodemus uses the very verb Jesus had used of them in John 5:39: they searched, and missed the one of whom the Scriptures testify.

**Textual notes.** Three places in this chapter read differently in many modern editions. (1) *John 7:8.* The KJV, with the Textus Receptus and many ancient manuscripts (including, it is reported, the early papyri P66 and P75 and Codex Vaticanus, verify), reads 'I go not up yet'. Some other important manuscripts (Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Bezae among them, verify) read 'I go not up', and many modern critical editions prefer that reading on the principle that the harder reading is more likely original, since a scribe would more likely add 'yet' to remove an apparent contradiction than delete it. Porphyry's attack, reported by Jerome (verify), shows that the shorter reading was known early. Either reading is consistent with Jesus' truthfulness (see the Council's first question). (2) *John 7:39.* The Textus Receptus, as the tools print it, reads literally 'for not yet was Holy Spirit'; the KJV's 'given' makes the sense plain in English. Some manuscripts write a participle meaning 'given' into the Greek, and others add 'upon them' (verify). All agree on the meaning: the Spirit was not yet present as the gift of the glorified Christ. (3) *John 7:53–8:11.* The KJV prints the passage about the woman taken in adultery, beginning with John 7:53. It is absent from the earliest Greek manuscripts of John that survive (among them P66, P75, Codex Sinaiticus, and Codex Vaticanus, verify), and in some later manuscripts it appears after John 7:36, after John 21:25, or after Luke 21:38 (verify); it is present in Codex Bezae (fifth century, verify) and in the great majority of later manuscripts, and Jerome included it in the Latin Vulgate. Augustine suggested that some had removed it from their copies for fear that it would seem to excuse adultery (*On Adulterous Marriages* 2.7, verify). Many modern editions print it in brackets or with a note; others, and those who hold to the Textus Receptus or the majority text, defend its place. This study, following the owner's decision, teaches the text the KJV prints, and treats the passage fully in unit 08. The differences should be explained calmly, without scorn for either side, and none of them changes any doctrine.

### 🚷 Preaching Mistakes to Avoid
- **Information without life:** a sermon full of water-pouring rituals, Mishnah quotations, and debates about punctuation that never stands up, as Jesus did, and calls the thirsty in the room to come to him and drink.
- **Heat without light:** a sermon that thunders against the Pharisees' snobbery, the crowd's cowardice, or 'religious elites' in other churches, and never asks the hearers where they judge by appearance, or where they are silent for fear.
- **The Spirit as a feeling:** preaching 'rivers of living water' as an emotional experience to be worked up, or as the overflow of a happy life, instead of the Holy Spirit himself, given by the glorified Christ to every believer.
- **Timing as technique:** preaching 'my time is not yet come' as a lesson in strategic patience or good career timing, and missing the Father's sovereign appointment of the cross.
- **Nicodemus as hero:** making Nicodemus the model of courage without noticing that his request was small and his faith still hidden; the chapter's hero is Christ, and Nicodemus is a man slowly coming to the light (John 3:2; 19:39).
- **Using 'Judge not' as a gag:** preaching John 7:24 or Matthew 7:1 as a ban on moral discernment, instead of Jesus' call to righteous judgment that looks past appearances.
- **Setting the textual question at the centre:** turning John 7:53 into a lecture on manuscripts, or a denunciation of those who hold a different view of them, instead of letting the verse close the scene and pointing on to Christ.

## 🟣 Tier 4 — Christocentric, Humility, Life & Light
### 👑 Every Answer in Jesus
John 7 demolishes every ground of self-glory. Family is no ground: the Lord's own brothers did not believe (John 7:5). Learning is no ground: the rulers had the letters and missed the Christ, while temple guards, with no theology at all, were undone by his words (John 7:15, 46). Religious zeal is no ground: they kept the sabbath scrupulously and plotted murder (John 7:19–23). Knowledge of Scripture is no ground: they quoted Micah and got the birthplace of the Messiah wrong (John 7:42), and told a ruler to search the Scriptures that they themselves had not searched far enough (John 7:52). Majority opinion is no ground: 'Have any of the rulers or of the Pharisees believed on him?' (John 7:48). Even religious joy is no ground: a whole city kept a week-long feast and was still thirsty.

And every good thing the chapter holds is found in him. The Father's teaching is found in him (John 7:16). Truth without any unrighteousness is found in him (John 7:18). The knowledge of the true God is in him, and nowhere else (John 7:28–29). The timing of our salvation is in his Father's hands, and no enemy can hurry or hinder it (John 7:30). Living water for every thirsty person is found in him (John 7:37), and the Spirit is given by him once he is glorified (John 7:39). This is what Paul means when he says of Christ, "In whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." (Colossians 2:3) The chapter humbles us by showing that we, like the crowd, are thirsty, divided, and quick to judge by appearance, and it makes Christ glorious by showing him hunted, mocked, and still crying out in the temple for the thirsty to come to him.

### 🙇 Humility Practices

- Tomorrow, when someone in the group, or in your family, says something you are tempted to dismiss because of who they are, ask them one more question and listen to the answer before you respond, as the officers listened and heard what the rulers could not (John 7:46).
- Today, pray by name for one member of your own family who does not yet believe, confessing before God that living near Jesus did not make his own brothers believe (John 7:5) and that only the risen Christ changed James (1 Corinthians 15:7).

### 🌱 Life Practices
- **Inward:** Tomorrow morning, before your phone or your coffee, read John 7:37–39 and pray in your own words, 'Lord, I am thirsty; I come to you; I drink', confessing that "without me ye can do nothing" (John 15:5).
- **Outward:** Today, contact one person in your church or neighbourhood who is going through a dry season of grief, illness, or loneliness (__________), and arrange to visit or call on a day you name, bringing one verse, 'If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink' (John 7:37).
- **Outward:** Tomorrow, write a short note to one believer who is serving quietly and feels unseen (__________), thanking them for one way the Spirit's life has flowed through them to you, since out of the believer 'shall flow rivers of living water' (John 7:38).

### 💡 Light Practices
- **Inward:** Tonight, ask yourself honestly whom you have judged 'according to the appearance' this week (John 7:24), and bring the answer to the Lord in prayer, since "He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy." (Proverbs 28:13)
- **Outward:** Tomorrow, when you hear someone dismissed because of where they come from or what they lack (__________), say one fair and true thing about them, or ask whether they have been heard, as Nicodemus asked, 'Doth our law judge any man, before it hear him' (John 7:51).
- **Outward:** Today, speak of Christ by name to one person with whom you have been keeping quiet about him (__________), gently and without argument, remembering that the officers simply told what they had heard: 'Never man spake like this man' (John 7:46).
