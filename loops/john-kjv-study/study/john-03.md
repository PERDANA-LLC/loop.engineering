## 🧭 At a Glance
### 🧵 Golden Thread
No one sees the kingdom of God without a new birth from above, which the Spirit gives, and the Son lifted up is God's gift of love to all who believe.

### 🌗 Life & Light Line
John 3 answers the death hidden inside a respectable religious life, flesh that can only give birth to flesh, with the new birth the Spirit gives and the everlasting life that comes by looking to the lifted-up Son. It exposes the darkness men love because their deeds are evil, and it gives the light of the Son who came not to condemn the world but to save it, so that whoever does truth comes to the light.

## 🏛️ Core Foundations
### ✝️ Christological Root
**Gospel Foundation:** The chapter turns on two things Jesus said to Nicodemus: that he must be born again, and that the Son of man must be lifted up. The first names the need; the second names the only answer. "And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up:" (John 3:14) The purpose follows at once: "That whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have eternal life." (John 3:15) And the chapter's best-known verse says where this came from: "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." (John 3:16)

**The Bridge:** The new birth and the cross are joined in the same conversation. No one can make himself born from above, and no one bitten by the serpents could cure himself; in both cases life comes from outside, from God, and it is received by looking and believing. The doctrines grounded here are regeneration by the Spirit (John 3:5–8) and substitutionary atonement: the Son lifted up on the cross in the place of the dying, so that whoever believes in him does not perish (John 3:14–16).

**Canonical Echoes:** Jesus (John 3:14) → Promise (Numbers 21:8; Ezekiel 36:26) → Doctrine (2 Corinthians 5:21) → Life (Galatians 2:20)

**What Gets Lost:** Taught without Christ at the center, John 3 becomes a lesson in self-improvement ('turn over a new leaf'), a slogan ('born-again Christian' as a brand), or a debate about baptism. Nicodemus is told to be born again, but the chapter never tells him to do it himself; it points him to the Spirit who gives birth and to the Son who is lifted up. Remove Christ and the chapter becomes an impossible command. Keep him at the center and it becomes the gospel.

### ⚔️ Spiritual Warfare
**The Lie:** You are good enough as you are. Your upbringing, your church, your learning, your morality, or your position already make you right with God, so you need a teacher, not a new birth. (Its twin says the opposite: you are too far gone ever to be made new.)

**The Truth:** Jesus says it to the most respectable man in the room: "Marvel not that I said unto thee, Ye must be born again" (John 3:7). And to the worst: "whosoever believeth in him should not perish" (John 3:16).

**The Battleground:** The conscience of the respectable, and the fear of exposure. Nicodemus comes by night; the chapter ends its first half with people who will not come to the light "lest his deeds should be reproved" (John 3:20). The fight is over whether we will let the light show us as we are.

**The Weapon:** The word of the gospel, heard and believed: the Son lifted up, looked to in faith. Peter says believers are "born again, not of corruptible seed, but of incorruptible, by the word of God" (1 Peter 1:23). The Spirit gives the new birth through that word, so the weapon is to hear it, speak it, and look to the one it lifts up.

### 🌱 Life Pathway
**The Theft:** In John 3 the thief steals by counterfeit. Nicodemus has everything a religious life can offer: he is a Pharisee, a ruler, a teacher of Israel, and a man impressed by Jesus' signs. The thief's trick is to let him think that this is life. But "That which is born of the flesh is flesh" (John 3:6), and a man can be learned, devout, and respected and still "shall not see life" (John 3:36). A second theft appears in the Baptist's camp: rivalry. His disciples lose their joy because 'all men come to him' (John 3:26), and envy would have stolen John's gladness too if he had let it.

**The Source:** The Spirit of God, who gives birth from above, and the Son of man, lifted up, "That whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have eternal life" (John 3:15). His means in this chapter are his word ("We speak that we do know" (John 3:11)), his Spirit, and his cross. He came "that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly" (John 10:10).

**The Channel:** Moses did not heal anyone; he lifted up what God told him to lift up, and the people looked. John the Baptist did not give life; he pointed and then stepped aside, like the friend of the bridegroom who 'rejoiceth greatly because of the bridegroom's voice' (John 3:29). A believer passes life on the same way: by lifting up Christ in words someone can hear, and then getting out of the way.

**The Abundance Guard:** Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, not prosperity, ease, or freedom from suffering. The Israelites who looked at the brass serpent still lived in the wilderness; the life they received was life, not comfort. Everlasting life in John 3 is the life of knowing God through his Son (John 17:3), which begins now and cannot be taken, even in poverty, illness, or grief.

### 💡 Light Pathway
**The Darkness:** John names it plainly: "men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil" (John 3:19). This darkness is not ignorance; it is preference. People stay away from the light because they know what it would show. In the chapter's first scene a good man comes by night, and whatever his reasons, the setting hangs over the conversation.

**The Source:** The light that "is come into the world" (John 3:19), the Son whom God sent "not... to condemn the world; but that the world through him might be saved" (John 3:17). "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts" (2 Corinthians 4:6).

**The Lamp:** Coming to the light: letting our deeds "be made manifest" (John 3:21) before God, confessing what we have hidden, and so discovering that the light which exposes is also the light which saves. Scripture opened honestly, as Jesus opened it to Nicodemus; the unfruitful works of darkness reproved, beginning in our own lives (Ephesians 5:11); and good works that glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16), done in the open because they are 'wrought in God' (John 3:21).

**The Love Guard:** Light without love is glare. The Son came into the world not to condemn it but to save it (John 3:17), and his light is carried in the same spirit. We turn the lamp on our own deeds before anyone else's, and when we speak to others about the dark, we speak as people who were rescued from it, not as judges.

### 🏺 Setting the Scene
The chapter follows straight on from the Passover crowds of John 2:23–25, who believed when they saw the signs, and from John's remark that Jesus 'knew what was in man' (John 2:25). The very next words introduce one such man. Nicodemus is a Pharisee, a member of the strict, respected movement devoted to careful keeping of the law of Moses, and 'a ruler of the Jews', most likely a member of the Sanhedrin, the ruling council in Jerusalem (later he speaks up in a council meeting, John 7:50–51). Jesus calls him 'a master of Israel', that is, a teacher of Israel. He is the kind of man everyone would have expected to be closest to God.

He comes by night. John does not say why: perhaps caution, perhaps to talk without crowds, perhaps because teachers often studied late. But John is the Gospel of light and darkness (John 1:5), and he mentions the night again each time Nicodemus appears (John 7:50; John 19:39). By the end of the conversation Jesus is speaking of those who come to the light and those who stay away.

The second half moves to the Judean countryside, where both Jesus' disciples and John the Baptist are baptizing, before John's imprisonment (Mark 1:14 tells when Jesus' Galilean preaching began). The site of Aenon near Salim is not certain. John's disciples are troubled that the crowds are going to Jesus, and the Baptist answers with a picture from a Jewish wedding: the friend of the bridegroom, whose whole joy is the bridegroom's joy.

**View A:** 'Born of water and of the Spirit' (John 3:5) describes one birth: the cleansing and renewing work of the Spirit that God promised through Ezekiel, where clean water and a new spirit go together: "Then will I sprinkle clean water upon you, and ye shall be clean" (Ezekiel 36:25). That is why Jesus expects the teacher of Israel to understand (John 3:10).

**View B:** 'Water' means baptism, and 'the Spirit' the gift given in it: to be born again is to be baptized and receive the Spirit. Compare Peter's call, "Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost" (Acts 2:38), and Paul's phrase "the washing of regeneration" (Titus 3:5).

### 👣 Case Study · Scripture in Action
Saul of Tarsus had every credential Nicodemus had and more. He was brought up in Jerusalem "at the feet of Gamaliel" (Acts 22:3), himself a Pharisee on the council (Acts 5:34). By his own account he was "as touching the law, a Pharisee" (Philippians 3:5), and 'touching the righteousness which is in the law, blameless' (Philippians 3:6). He was zealous enough to hunt down Christians (Acts 9:1). Then the risen Jesus met him on the road to Damascus, and everything he had counted as gain he now counted as loss (Philippians 3:7). He did not reform himself; he was stopped, blinded, and made new. For the rest of his life he wanted only to "be found in him, not having mine own righteousness, which is of the law, but that which is through the faith of Christ, the righteousness which is of God by faith" (Philippians 3:9).

**Q12.** What did Saul have to stop trusting before he could receive life, and how is his story an answer to Nicodemus's question, 'How can these things be?' (John 3:9)?

### 🏙️ Modern Example
Harold had sung in the same church choir for forty years. He knew the hymns by heart, served as treasurer, and could quote John 3:16 before he could read. When a new pastor preached on Nicodemus, Harold went home unsettled. He had always assumed that 'born again' meant a certain kind of emotional Christian, not a man like him. That week he read John 3 slowly, twice, and saw that Jesus had said it to a teacher, not to a criminal. What struck him most was verse 14: the Israelites were not told to try harder, to understand the serpent, or to feel better; they were told to look. Harold sat at his kitchen table and prayed a plain prayer he had never prayed before, confessing that he had been trusting his record and not the Son lifted up. Nothing dramatic happened. But he told his pastor on Sunday, and a few months later he said that the hymns he had sung for forty years had begun to sound as if they were about someone he knew.

### 📜 Voices from Church History
The Canons of Dort (1618–1619), in the section on the Third and Fourth Heads of Doctrine (verify the article numbers, which are usually given as articles 11 and 12), describe the new birth in terms drawn largely from John 3. They teach that regeneration is not brought about merely by preaching from outside or by moral persuasion, as if God did his part and then waited on the sinner. It is a work of God inside the person, wholly supernatural, which the synod compared to creation and to the raising of the dead. The Spirit opens the closed heart, softens the hard one, and gives a new will, so that the one made alive really does believe and repent. The Canons add that this work is beyond our full understanding, as the wind is (John 3:8), and that no one should despair of it or presume upon it, but use the means God gives: his word, prayer, and the church's ministry.

### ⛪ Doctrine in My Own Words
- **Theology Proper:** God's love for the world is shown in a gift: he gave his only begotten Son (John 3:16).
- **Christology:** Jesus is the Son of man who came down from heaven; he is above all, and the Father has given all things into his hand (John 3:31, 35).
- **Pneumatology:** The Holy Spirit gives the new birth freely and sovereignly, like the wind, which no one controls (John 3:8).
- **Anthropology & Sin:** What is born of the flesh is only flesh, and by nature people love darkness rather than light (John 3:6, 19).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** No one can see or enter the kingdom of God without being born from above (John 3:3, 5).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** The Son of man was lifted up on the cross, like the serpent in the wilderness, so that dying sinners who look to him may live (John 3:14–15).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** Everlasting life is received by believing in the Son, not by works, rank, or ritual (John 3:16).
- **Eschatology:** The verdict of the last day is already given now: the believer has everlasting life, and the unbeliever is condemned already (John 3:18, 36).
- **Christian Walk:** A servant of Christ finds joy in Christ's increase and his own decrease (John 3:30).

### 👑 Christocentric & Humility · Level 4
Every problem in John 3 is answered in Jesus. Nicodemus cannot see the kingdom, and Jesus gives the Spirit who makes men see. Flesh can only give birth to flesh, and the Spirit gives birth to spirit. No one has gone up to heaven to bring back its secrets, and the Son of man has come down from heaven to speak what he has seen. The world lies bitten and dying, as Israel did in the wilderness, and the Son is lifted up so that whoever looks may live. Men love darkness, and the light has come into the world, not to condemn but to save. The Baptist's disciples fret over who is winning, and the Bridegroom is here. The wrath of God abides on unbelief, and whoever believes on the Son has everlasting life now. Every truth in the chapter is found in him too: the love of God, the life of God, the words of God, the Spirit without measure, and the judgment of God are all located in the Son.

The chapter humbles us from both sides. It humbles the respectable, because the man Jesus tells he must be born again is a teacher of Israel, not a tax collector. It humbles the proud in ministry, because the greatest prophet born of women (Luke 7:28) says that he must decrease. Birth is the humblest picture Jesus could have chosen: no one contributes to his own birth. We receive life, as we receive everything, "except it be given him from heaven" (John 3:27).

**Humility Practices**
- Tonight, before you sleep, thank God aloud for three good things in your life that you did not earn, confessing with John that "A man can receive nothing, except it be given him from heaven" (John 3:27).
- Tomorrow, when someone else's work or ministry is praised, say something that adds to the praise, and pray privately that Christ would be seen in it, because "He must increase, but I must decrease" (John 3:30).
- Today, name to God one thing (a qualification, a family heritage, a religious habit) that you have leaned on to feel right with him, and lay it down in prayer as Paul counted his gains as loss (Philippians 3:7).

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy
### 📌 Core Concept
A very important teacher named Nicodemus came to see Jesus one night. He thought Jesus was a good teacher sent from God. But Jesus told him something surprising: to be part of God's family and God's kingdom, you have to be born again. That doesn't mean being a baby a second time. It means God has to give you a brand-new life on the inside, and only God's Holy Spirit can do it. Then Jesus told how God would do it: long ago, when snakes bit the people of Israel, God told Moses to put a bronze snake on a pole, and everyone who looked at it lived. In the same way, Jesus would be lifted up on the cross, and everyone who trusts him will live forever, because God loves the world so much.

### 📖 Key Verses
"Jesus answered and said unto him, Verily, verily, I say unto thee, Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." (John 3:3) Everyone needs God to give them a new life on the inside.

"For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." (John 3:16) God loves us so much that he gave us Jesus, and anyone who trusts Jesus lives forever.

"For God sent not his Son into the world to condemn the world; but that the world through him might be saved." (John 3:17) Jesus came to rescue us, not to punish us.

"He must increase, but I must decrease." (John 3:30) Jesus should be the most important one, not me.

### 🪞 Analogy
A caterpillar can try very hard to fly. It can climb to the top of a tall plant and stretch as far as it can, but it still can't fly, because it doesn't have wings. It needs to become something new. Trying harder won't do it. In the same way, being good or going to church can't make us part of God's kingdom by itself. We need the new life that God gives. And just as a caterpillar doesn't make itself a butterfly, we don't make ourselves born again; God does it.

### 🔎 Diagnostic Check
- Do I think God should let me into heaven because I'm a pretty good person?
- Have I ever told Jesus that I trust him to save me, and not my own good behaviour?
- Is there something I do that I hope nobody finds out about?
- Am I happy when someone else does well, or do I feel jealous?
- Do I believe that God really loves me, even when I've done something wrong?

### ✅ Action Step
Tonight, before bed, say John 3:16 out loud, and then say it again with your own name in place of 'the world'. Then thank Jesus for being lifted up on the cross for you, and tell one person tomorrow that God loves them too.

### 🌗 Life and Light, Simply
Jesus gives life like a brand-new birthday on the inside, a life that God's Spirit gives and that never ends. Jesus gives light because he came to rescue people, not to scold them, so we don't have to hide anything from him.

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium
### 📜 Walkthrough
#### John 3:1–2 · A Ruler Comes by Night
**Says:** "There was a man of the Pharisees, named Nicodemus, a ruler of the Jews:" (John 3:1) "The same came to Jesus by night, and said unto him, Rabbi, we know that thou art a teacher come from God: for no man can do these miracles that thou doest, except God be with him." (John 3:2) *Rabbi* means my teacher; *miracles* here translates the word John uses for signs.

**Means:** The chapter division hides a link. John 2 ended, "for he knew what was in man" (John 2:25), and John 3 begins, literally, 'there was a man'. The Greek repeats the word *anthrōpos* (G444, John 3:1): Nicodemus is Exhibit A of what Jesus knows is in man. He is not a villain. He is the best that Israel's religion could produce: a Pharisee, one of the movement most serious about keeping the law; a ruler, *archōn* (G758, John 3:1), most likely a member of the Sanhedrin, since later he speaks as "being one of them" (John 7:50); and a recognized teacher (John 3:10).

He comes 'by night', *nuktos* (G3571, John 3:2). John does not explain why, and readers have suggested caution, privacy, or the habit of study at night. What John does make clear is that he wants us to remember it: he recalls Nicodemus as the man "which at the first came to Jesus by night" (John 19:39). In a Gospel where "the light shineth in darkness" (John 1:5) and Judas goes out into the dark, "and it was night" (John 13:30), the setting is a quiet signal. By the end of the conversation Jesus is speaking of those who come to the light and those who do not (John 3:19–21).

His opening is generous. He calls Jesus 'Rabbi', and a teacher 'come from God'. He speaks for others, 'we know', perhaps for the many in Jerusalem who believed when they saw the signs (John 2:23). He reasons from the signs, *sēmeia* (G4592, John 3:2), to the conclusion that God is with Jesus. That is true as far as it goes. But it puts Jesus in a category Nicodemus already has, a teacher like himself, and it assumes that Nicodemus can assess Jesus from where he stands.

**Asks:** Do you come to Jesus with a compliment, or with your need? It is possible to admire him, even to call him a teacher from God, and still be standing outside the kingdom. Come as you are, and let him tell you what you need.

#### John 3:3–4 · Born Again, or Born from Above
**Says:** "Jesus answered and said unto him, Verily, verily, I say unto thee, Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." (John 3:3) "Nicodemus saith unto him, How can a man be born when he is old? can he enter the second time into his mother's womb, and be born?" (John 3:4) *Verily* means truly; *except* means unless.

**Means:** Jesus does not answer the compliment; he answers the man. 'Verily, verily' is the Hebrew word *amēn*, doubled. In the KJV the phrase 'verily, verily' occurs 25 times in the New Testament, and every one of them is in John, always on the lips of Jesus, always introducing a solemn truth. This is the first of three in this conversation (John 3:3, 5, 11).

The key word is *anōthen* (G509, John 3:3), translated 'again'. The STEPBible lexicon (Tyndale House, following Abbott-Smith) gives it two senses: 'from above', and 'from the first', whence 'anew, again'. It notes that most take John 3:3 and John 3:7 as 'again', though not all. John himself uses the word in this chapter in the plain sense of 'from above': "He that cometh from above is above all" (John 3:31), and elsewhere of the veil torn "from the top" (Matthew 27:51) and power given "from above" (John 19:11). Many readers think Jesus chose a word that carries both: a second birth, and a birth from above. Nicodemus hears only 'a second time', *deuteron* (G1208, John 3:4). That misunderstanding is typical of John: people take Jesus on a merely earthly level (the temple in John 2:20, the water in John 4:11, the bread in John 6:52), and the misunderstanding becomes the doorway to the truth.

Notice what Jesus says a man cannot do without this birth: he cannot even *see* the kingdom of God. The phrase 'kingdom of God' occurs 2 times in John, and both are in this conversation (John 3:3, 5). Nicodemus is waiting for the kingdom, as devout Israelites were (Mark 15:43 says it of Joseph of Arimathaea). Jesus tells him that without new birth he would not recognize it if it stood in front of him, and it does.

Nicodemus's reply may be puzzled literalism, or it may be a wistful question from an older man: how can someone start over this late? Either way he has understood one thing: this is not a matter of improving, but of beginning again, and no one can do that to himself.

**Asks:** Have you assumed that you could see God's kingdom from where you already stand, by upbringing, study, or effort? Jesus says no one can. Let that humble you, and let it drive you to ask the only one who can give the birth from above.

#### John 3:5–6 · Water and the Spirit
**Says:** "Jesus answered, Verily, verily, I say unto thee, Except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God." (John 3:5) "That which is born of the flesh is flesh; and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit." (John 3:6)

**Means:** Jesus restates verse 3. 'Born again' becomes 'born of water and of the Spirit'; 'see' becomes 'enter'. So the second saying explains the first: the birth from above is a birth 'of water and of the Spirit'.

What does the water mean? Christians have given several answers (see the View A / View B panel and the Debate Corner). The strongest clue is in the text. In the Greek a single preposition, *ek*, governs both nouns, water, *hudatos* (G5204, John 3:5), and Spirit, *pneumatos* (G4151, John 3:5), which suggests one birth described two ways, not two births. And in John 3:10 Jesus expects the teacher of Israel to know this already, so it must be something the Old Testament taught. It did. Through Ezekiel God promised: "Then will I sprinkle clean water upon you, and ye shall be clean: from all your filthiness, and from all your idols, will I cleanse you." (Ezekiel 36:25) And then: "A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you" (Ezekiel 36:26), "And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes" (Ezekiel 36:27). Water and Spirit together: cleansing from sin and a new heart given by God. Isaiah joins them in the same way: "I will pour water upon him that is thirsty... I will pour my spirit upon thy seed" (Isaiah 44:3). John 3 also sits between the purifying water of Cana (John 2:6) and the question 'about purifying' (John 3:25).

Verse 6 gives the reason the new birth is necessary. Like produces like. 'Flesh', *sarx* (G4561, John 3:6), here means human nature in its weakness and fallenness, not the body as evil. Human nature can only reproduce itself; it cannot give birth to the life of God. The Spirit gives birth to what is spirit: life that shares God's own nature. That is why no religious upbringing, no pedigree, no discipline can substitute. John has already said that God's children are born "not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God" (John 1:13).

**Asks:** Stop trying to raise the flesh to the level of the Spirit. Ask God for what only he can give: cleansing from sin and a new heart. And hear the promise under the command: what Jesus requires here, God promised long ago to give.

#### John 3:7–8 · The Wind Bloweth Where It Listeth
**Says:** "Marvel not that I said unto thee, Ye must be born again." (John 3:7) "The wind bloweth where it listeth, and thou hearest the sound thereof, but canst not tell whence it cometh, and whither it goeth: so is every one that is born of the Spirit." (John 3:8) *Marvel* means be astonished; *listeth* means wishes or pleases; *whither* means to where.

**Means:** The old English pronouns preserve something modern English hides. 'I said unto *thee*' is singular: Nicodemus. '*Ye* must be born again' is plural: everyone. The Greek has the same shift, and it matters. Jesus is not singling out Nicodemus as a special case; he is stating the rule for all people, and Nicodemus is included. 'Must' is *dei* (G1163, John 3:7), a word of necessity. It appears three times in this chapter (John 3:7, 14, 30), and each is a necessity set by God: we must be born again, the Son of man must be lifted up, and Christ must increase.

Then the illustration. The Greek *pneuma* (G4151, John 3:8) means both 'wind' and 'spirit', and in this verse it is both: 'The wind bloweth', and 'born of the Spirit'. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 3:8 under the sense 'wind', and the Hebrew *ruach* (H7307) has the same range: wind, breath, spirit. Ezekiel's valley of dry bones shows it: "Come from the four winds, O breath, and breathe upon these slain, that they may live" (Ezekiel 37:9). You cannot command the wind, schedule it, or see it; you hear it and see what it does. So is the new birth. The Spirit acts freely, 'where it listeth'. No one can manufacture it, and no one can prevent it. The Preacher said it long before: "As thou knowest not what is the way of the spirit... even so thou knowest not the works of God who maketh all" (Ecclesiastes 11:5).

This does not make the new birth unknowable. We 'hear the sound thereof': its effects can be recognized in a life that now believes in the Son (1 John 5:1) and comes to the light (John 3:21).

**Asks:** Do not despair of anyone: the wind blows where it will, including into hearts you think are closed. And do not try to control the Spirit's work by technique; pray, speak the word, and watch for the sound of the wind.

#### John 3:9–12 · The Teacher of Israel
**Says:** "Nicodemus answered and said unto him, How can these things be?" (John 3:9) Jesus replies, "Art thou a master of Israel, and knowest not these things?" (John 3:10) Then: "We speak that we do know, and testify that we have seen; and ye receive not our witness." (John 3:11) "If I have told you earthly things, and ye believe not, how shall ye believe, if I tell you of heavenly things?" (John 3:12) *Master* means teacher.

**Means:** Nicodemus's last words in the chapter are a question, and then he fades from the scene. Jesus' answer is pointed: 'a master of Israel' is, in the Greek, *the* teacher, *didaskalos* (G1320, John 3:10), with the article. Whether that means Nicodemus was especially eminent or simply 'you, a teacher of Israel', the rebuke is the same: the Scriptures he taught spoke of this. Ezekiel 36 promised the new heart and the Spirit; Jeremiah 31:33 promised the law written in the heart; Moses promised that "the LORD thy God will circumcise thine heart" (Deuteronomy 30:6). A teacher of Israel should have recognized the promise.

Verse 11 moves into the plural: 'We speak... ye receive not'. The 'we' may be Jesus joining his witness with the Baptist's and his disciples', or answering Nicodemus's 'we know' of verse 2 with a 'we know' of his own. The 'ye' is plural too: Nicodemus stands for a whole circle that does not receive the witness. This is John's language of testimony, *marturia* (G3141, John 3:11), and it echoes the Prologue: "He came unto his own, and his own received him not" (John 1:11).

'Earthly things' probably means what Jesus has just said about new birth: it happens on earth, in people, and it can be illustrated from birth and wind. 'Heavenly things' are what he is about to say: the Son of man from heaven, his lifting up, the love of God. If the first is too much for them, the second will be harder still, unless they believe.

**Asks:** Knowledge of the Bible is not the same as knowing what it means. Ask God to keep you teachable, especially in the areas where you think you already know. And receive the witness of the one who speaks what he has seen.

#### John 3:13–15 · The Son of Man Lifted Up
**Says:** "And no man hath ascended up to heaven, but he that came down from heaven, even the Son of man which is in heaven." (John 3:13) "And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up:" (John 3:14) "That whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have eternal life." (John 3:15)

**Means:** Why can Jesus speak of heavenly things? Because no one else has been there to report on them. Agur asked long ago, "Who hath ascended up into heaven, or descended?" (Proverbs 30:4). Jesus' answer is that the only one who knows heaven is the one who came from it. 'The Son of man' is Jesus' favourite title for himself, drawn from Daniel's vision of one "like the Son of man" (Daniel 7:13) who receives from God "dominion, and glory, and a kingdom" (Daniel 7:14). John has already shown the Son of man as Jacob's ladder, the link between heaven and earth (John 1:51). The KJV's text ends 'which is in heaven', which presents the Son as belonging to heaven even while he speaks on earth; many modern editions lack those words (see the Original Language Deep Dive).

Then Jesus reaches back to Numbers 21. Israel complained against God and Moses, "And the LORD sent fiery serpents among the people, and they bit the people; and much people of Israel died." (Numbers 21:6) The people confessed their sin, and God's remedy was strange: "Make thee a fiery serpent, and set it upon a pole: and it shall come to pass, that every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live." (Numbers 21:8) Moses made one of brass, "and it came to pass, that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived." (Numbers 21:9) The pole in Hebrew is *nes* (H5251), a standard or banner, something raised high to be seen.

Notice the parallels. The people were dying under a judgment they deserved. The cure took the form of the very thing that was killing them, raised up. It was God's provision, not their idea. It was received by looking, nothing more. And it was for 'every one that is bitten'. So with the Son of man. Sin is killing us; the Son is lifted up, made 'sin for us' (2 Corinthians 5:21) in the likeness of the curse; God provided him; we receive life by believing, which is the soul's looking; and it is for 'whosoever'.

'Lifted up', *hupsōthēnai* (G5312, John 3:14, V-APN), is a double word in John. It means raised physically, and it can mean exalted: the STEPBible lexicon gives both. In John, Jesus' lifting up on the cross is also his exaltation. He will say, "And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me" (John 12:32), and John adds, "This he said, signifying what death he should die" (John 12:33). The 'must' is God's necessity: this is the only way.

**Asks:** Stop looking at the snakes, and stop trying to treat the bite yourself. Look to the Son lifted up. Faith is not a feeling to work up; it is looking away from ourselves to him.

#### John 3:16 · God So Loved the World
**Says:** "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." (John 3:16)

**Means:** 'For' ties this verse to the serpent: the lifting up of the Son is not the plan of a reluctant God but the gift of a loving one. 'So' translates *houtōs* (G3779, John 3:16), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'in this way, so, thus'. It most naturally means 'in this way' (this is how God loved the world: he gave), pointing back to the 'even so' of verse 14. That does not shrink the love; it shows its size by showing what it did.

'The world', *kosmos* (G2889, John 3:16), in John is usually not a compliment. It is humanity in rebellion against its Maker: "the world knew him not" (John 1:10), and it will hate his disciples (John 15:18). God loved *that*. The wonder of the verse is not that the world was big enough to deserve such love, but that it was bad enough to make such love astonishing. (Christians differ on how this love relates to God's saving purpose for particular people; see the Council's Hard Questions.)

'He gave': the aorist *edōken* (G1325, John 3:16) points to one act of giving. Coming after verse 14, it includes giving the Son up to death: "He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all" (Romans 8:32). 'His only begotten Son', *monogenēs* (G3439, John 3:16), is the Son who is unique, the only one of his kind; the STEPBible lexicon gives 'only, only begotten', and cites its use for only children (Luke 7:12) and for Isaac (Hebrews 11:17). The echo of Abraham is deliberate for many readers: "Take now thy son, thine only son Isaac, whom thou lovest" (Genesis 22:2). What God stopped Abraham from doing, he did himself.

The purpose clause has two outcomes and no third: perish, or have everlasting life. 'Whosoever believeth in him' is, in the Greek, 'everyone who is believing into him', a present participle with *eis*, faith that goes out of itself and rests on the person of the Son. 'Everlasting life' is the same phrase as 'eternal life' in verse 15; the KJV varies the English. In John it is the life of the age to come, received now, whose heart is knowing God: "And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent." (John 17:3)

Readers differ on whether Jesus' words end at verse 15 and John the evangelist comments from verse 16, since the Greek has no quotation marks. Nothing in the meaning depends on it: whether spoken by Jesus or written by his apostle, it is the word of God.

**Asks:** Measure God's love by his gift, not by your circumstances. And do not leave this verse as a slogan: it asks whether you are believing in the Son now.

#### John 3:17–18 · Not to Condemn, Yet Condemned Already
**Says:** "For God sent not his Son into the world to condemn the world; but that the world through him might be saved." (John 3:17) "He that believeth on him is not condemned: but he that believeth not is condemned already, because he hath not believed in the name of the only begotten Son of God." (John 3:18)

**Means:** The Son's mission was rescue. A Jewish reader might have expected the Messiah to come in judgment on the nations. Instead God sent his Son 'that the world through him might be saved'. Jesus will say it again: "I came not to judge the world, but to save the world" (John 12:47).

But verse 18 shows why rescue was needed. The world was not neutral, waiting to be judged; it was already under condemnation. 'Condemned already' is *kekritai* (G2919, John 3:18, V-RPI-3S), a perfect passive: 'has been judged', a verdict already given and still standing. The Son did not come to pass the sentence; he came into a courtroom where the sentence had already been passed, to offer a pardon. The believer 'is not condemned', present tense: the verdict of the last day has been given in his favour already (John 5:24; Romans 8:1).

Why is the unbeliever condemned? 'Because he hath not believed in the name of the only begotten Son of God'. Not believing is not a neutral lack of information. It is refusing the one remedy God has provided. A man bitten in the camp of Israel who would not look at the serpent died of the bite, but he died having refused the cure. 'The name' means the person as he has revealed himself: the Son whom God gave.

**Asks:** If you have believed on him, hear that you are not condemned: not on probation, not half-pardoned. If you have not, hear that you do not need to wait for judgment to know where you stand; the remedy is offered today.

#### John 3:19–21 · Light Is Come into the World
**Says:** "And this is the condemnation, that light is come into the world, and men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil." (John 3:19) "For every one that doeth evil hateth the light, neither cometh to the light, lest his deeds should be reproved." (John 3:20) "But he that doeth truth cometh to the light, that his deeds may be made manifest, that they are wrought in God." (John 3:21) *Reproved* means exposed or convicted; *manifest* means shown openly; *wrought* means done.

**Means:** Now John explains 'condemned already'. The judgment, *krisis* (G2920, John 3:19), is not first a sentence handed down from outside but a choice revealed from inside. Light has come; people had the choice; and they 'loved darkness rather than light'. The verb is *ēgapēsan* (G25, John 3:19), the same verb as 'God so loved' in verse 16. God loved the world; the world loved the dark. The coming of the light shows what each person loves. 'Light' occurs 5 times in John 3, all in these three verses, and the light is Jesus himself, "the true Light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world" (John 1:9), who will say, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12).

Why do people hate the light? Not because they cannot see it, but 'lest his deeds should be reproved'. The verb is *elegchthē* (G1651, John 3:20, V-APS-3S), which the STEPBible lexicon places under the sense 'to expose' and cites with Ephesians 5:11 and Ephesians 5:13. Paul says, "all things that are reproved are made manifest by the light" (Ephesians 5:13). Light exposes. Job describes people "that rebel against the light" (Job 24:13) because they prefer to do their work unseen.

Verse 21 is the other side. 'He that doeth truth' is a Hebrew way of speaking: doing truth means living in faithfulness to God, acting honestly, and not hiding. Such a person 'cometh to the light'. Notice what is shown when he does: 'that they are wrought in God'. Even the good deeds that come out into the light are shown to be God's work in him, not his own achievement. This fits the chapter's beginning: the one who comes to the light is the one born of the Spirit.

This is where Nicodemus's night visit takes on its meaning. He came by night; will he come to the light? John leaves that question hanging, and then answers it gradually through his Gospel (John 7:50–51; John 19:39).

**Asks:** What do you keep in the dark, and why? Come to the light: it exposes, but it is the light of the one who came to save. And when good is seen in you, let it be seen as wrought in God, not in you.

#### John 3:22–24 · Baptizing in Judaea
**Says:** "After these things came Jesus and his disciples into the land of Judaea; and there he tarried with them, and baptized." (John 3:22) "And John also was baptizing in Aenon near to Salim, because there was much water there: and they came, and were baptized." (John 3:23) "For John was not yet cast into prison." (John 3:24) *Tarried* means stayed.

**Means:** The scene shifts from Jerusalem to the Judean countryside. For a time, Jesus' ministry ran alongside John the Baptist's. John will later add that "Jesus himself baptized not, but his disciples" (John 4:2): the baptism was done by Jesus' disciples under his authority, so the narrator can say both that he baptized and that he did not. Aenon probably means 'springs', and 'much water' explains why John chose it; its site is uncertain. An old tradition, recorded by Eusebius (verify), placed it in the Jordan valley south of Scythopolis (Beth-shean); others place it in Samaria near Shechem.

Verse 24 is a narrator's aside, and a revealing one. John assumes his readers already know that John the Baptist was imprisoned, the story told in Mark 6:17–18. He also shows that what he is describing happened before the Galilean ministry that Mark begins "after that John was put in prison" (Mark 1:14). John's Gospel fills in an early Judean period the other Gospels pass over. That is not a contradiction but a supplement, told by someone who knew the order of events.

**Asks:** Faithful ministry often runs side by side with other faithful ministry. Before you ask 'whose is bigger?', ask 'is Christ being lifted up?'

#### John 3:25–28 · A Question about Purifying
**Says:** "Then there arose a question between some of John's disciples and the Jews about purifying." (John 3:25) "And they came unto John, and said unto him, Rabbi, he that was with thee beyond Jordan, to whom thou barest witness, behold, the same baptizeth, and all men come to him." (John 3:26) John answers, "A man can receive nothing, except it be given him from heaven." (John 3:27) "Ye yourselves bear me witness, that I said, I am not the Christ, but that I am sent before him." (John 3:28)

**Means:** The 'question... about purifying' uses *katharismou* (G2512, John 3:25), the same word as the jars at Cana, 'after the manner of the purifying of the Jews' (John 2:6). John does not tell us what the debate was, but with two baptisms going on at once, the question of what really cleanses was bound to arise. John's Gospel answers it: not jars, not rival washings, but the one who is 'the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world' (John 1:29).

The disciples' complaint shows its heart in its words. They do not even name Jesus: 'he that was with thee', 'to whom thou barest witness', as though Jesus owed his start to John. And 'all men come to him' is an exaggeration born of alarm. This is the theft of rivalry: the joy of seeing people come to Christ lost in the worry of losing followers.

John's answer begins with a principle: 'A man can receive nothing, except it be given him from heaven.' It applies both ways. If Jesus' ministry is growing, heaven has given it. If John's is shrinking, heaven gave John what he had, and he has no claim on more. Paul says the same to a church divided by loyalties: "neither is he that planteth any thing, neither he that watereth; but God that giveth the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:7). Then John reminds them of his own testimony: he had said from the start, "I am not the Christ" (John 1:20). He was 'sent before him', a forerunner, and forerunners are supposed to be overtaken.

**Asks:** What have you received that was not given to you from heaven (1 Corinthians 4:7)? If everything is a gift, rivalry makes no sense and gratitude makes perfect sense.

#### John 3:29–30 · The Friend of the Bridegroom
**Says:** "He that hath the bride is the bridegroom: but the friend of the bridegroom, which standeth and heareth him, rejoiceth greatly because of the bridegroom's voice: this my joy therefore is fulfilled." (John 3:29) "He must increase, but I must decrease." (John 3:30)

**Means:** John reaches for a wedding, and every Israelite knew what the picture meant. The LORD is Israel's husband: "For thy Maker is thine husband" (Isaiah 54:5), and "as the bridegroom rejoiceth over the bride, so shall thy God rejoice over thee" (Isaiah 62:5). God promised, "I will betroth thee unto me for ever" (Hosea 2:19). So when John calls Jesus 'the bridegroom', *numphios* (G3566, John 3:29), he is making a claim about who Jesus is: the one to whom God's people belong. Jesus uses the same image of himself (Matthew 9:15), and the Bible ends with "the marriage of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:7). Coming right after Cana, where Jesus supplied the wedding's wine, the picture is fitting.

The friend of the bridegroom, in Jewish custom as later sources describe it, helped arrange the marriage and stood by at the wedding. His job was to bring bride and groom together and then step back. He did not want the bride for himself. His joy was hearing the bridegroom's voice. John says, 'this my joy therefore is fulfilled', a perfect passive, *peplērōtai*: made full and staying full.

Then the most humble sentence in the chapter: 'He must increase, but I must decrease.' 'Must' is *dei* (G1163, John 3:30) again: the same divine necessity as the new birth (John 3:7) and the lifting up of the Son (John 3:14). John is not resigning himself to decline; he is embracing God's plan. These are his last recorded words in the Gospel. Jesus would say of him, "Among those that are born of women there is not a greater prophet than John the Baptist" (Luke 7:28), and the greatest of prophets found his fullest joy in becoming less.

**Asks:** Whose voice is your joy? If it is the Bridegroom's, you can rejoice when others are drawn to him through someone else. Decrease is not loss when it means Christ is seen more.

#### John 3:31–33 · He That Cometh from Above
**Says:** "He that cometh from above is above all: he that is of the earth is earthly, and speaketh of the earth: he that cometh from heaven is above all." (John 3:31) "And what he hath seen and heard, that he testifieth; and no man receiveth his testimony." (John 3:32) "He that hath received his testimony hath set to his seal that God is true." (John 3:33) *Set to his seal* means put his stamp of confirmation on, certified.

**Means:** Whether these closing verses continue the Baptist's words or are the evangelist's reflection, as many think, they gather up the whole chapter. 'From above' is *anōthen* (G509, John 3:31), the very word of John 3:3. The birth Nicodemus needs is 'from above' because the one who gives it is 'from above'. Everyone else, even John the Baptist, is 'of the earth' and speaks as one from the earth; that is not an insult, it is a limit. Only the Son speaks as an eyewitness of heaven, 'what he hath seen and heard' (compare John 3:11–13).

'No man receiveth his testimony' is a sweeping statement, like 'all men come to him' in verse 26, and the next verse shows that John means it generally, not absolutely: some do receive it. The general rule is rejection (John 1:11); the exceptions are those born of God (John 1:12–13).

The one who receives it 'hath set to his seal', *esphragisen* (G4972, John 3:33, V-AAI-3S). The STEPBible lexicon gives, among its senses, sealing for authentication and cites this verse. A seal on a document certifies that it is true. The believer, by believing Jesus, publicly certifies 'that God is true'. The reverse is sobering: "he that believeth not God hath made him a liar" (1 John 5:10). Believing the Son is not a private opinion; it is taking sides on whether God tells the truth.

**Asks:** Have you put your seal on God's testimony? Receiving Jesus' words is not a small thing: it is agreeing with God about himself, about his Son, and about you.

#### John 3:34–36 · The Spirit Without Measure
**Says:** "For he whom God hath sent speaketh the words of God: for God giveth not the Spirit by measure unto him." (John 3:34) "The Father loveth the Son, and hath given all things into his hand." (John 3:35) "He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life: and he that believeth not the Son shall not see life; but the wrath of God abideth on him." (John 3:36) *Abideth* means remains.

**Means:** Why are the Son's words the words of God? Because God gives him the Spirit without limit. *Metrou* (G3358, John 3:34) is a measure; prophets had the Spirit for particular times and tasks, but the Son has him without measure. Isaiah foresaw it: "And the spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him" (Isaiah 11:2). The Baptist had seen the Spirit "descending, and remaining on him" (John 1:33). The Greek of this clause does not name the one who receives; the KJV reads 'unto him', taking the receiver to be the Son, which fits the next verse. Some read it as the Son giving the Spirit to others without measure; either way the Spirit who gives the new birth (John 3:5) comes through the Son.

'The Father loveth the Son' is the eternal love behind the love of verse 16. God gave the world his Son; he has given the Son 'all things'. Jesus will act on the same knowledge at the foot-washing: "Jesus knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands" (John 13:3). Everything, life, judgment, the Spirit, the kingdom, is in his hand.

So the chapter ends as it must: with a choice between two people, the believer and the one who 'believeth not'. The first 'hath everlasting life', present tense, now. The second, *apeithōn* (G544, John 3:36, V-PAP-NSM), is literally 'the one who disobeys'. The STEPBible lexicon gives 'to disobey, be disobedient' and cites this verse. Unbelief in John is not honest doubt but refusal: saying no to the Son. Such a person 'shall not see life', echoing 'cannot see the kingdom' in verse 3. 'The wrath of God', *orgē* (G3709, John 3:36), is God's settled and holy opposition to sin; the STEPBible lexicon lists this verse under its effect. And it 'abideth', *menei* (G3306, John 3:36, V-PAI-3S): it remains, it is not a new anger arriving later but the old verdict not yet lifted. The only way it is lifted is the Son lifted up.

**Asks:** The chapter that holds the Bible's most loved verse ends with its most sobering. Both are true. Do not soften either. Believe on the Son, and plead with others to believe, while it is today.

### 🧨 Cultural Landmines and Misconceptions
**'Born-again Christian' as a label.** In many places the phrase names a political or social group, or a style of Christianity. Jesus used it for something every Christian must experience, whatever their tradition. Rescue the phrase from the label: in John 3 it describes the Spirit's work, not a subculture.

**John 3:16 as a sign at a football game.** The verse is so familiar that it can be heard without being heard. Read it in its setting: it follows the story of dying people and a brass serpent (John 3:14–15), and it is followed by condemnation and wrath (John 3:18, 36). Its love is the love of a God who rescues the doomed.

**Nicodemus as a coward.** He is often preached as a secret believer too timid to come in daylight. John's text does not say why he came at night, and it later shows him speaking up for Jesus before the council (John 7:50–51) and bringing a costly gift for his burial (John 19:39). He is better seen as a man on a journey from night toward light. Whether he became a believer John does not state in so many words, but his last appearance is hopeful.

**The Pharisees as hypocrites, full stop.** The Pharisees were a serious renewal movement that loved the law. Some opposed Jesus bitterly; some, like Nicodemus, were drawn to him; Paul was one. John 3 is not a story about a bad man, but about a good man who still needed to be born again. That should make every good church member uncomfortable.

**The brass serpent as magic.** Israel later made the serpent itself an object of worship, and Hezekiah "brake in pieces the brasen serpent that Moses had made: for unto those days the children of Israel did burn incense to it" (2 Kings 18:4). The power was never in the metal but in God's promise. Crosses, rituals, and objects can be treated the same way.

## 🔴 Tier 3 — Expert
### 🏗️ Theological Framework
John 3 is one of Scripture's clearest windows onto the order in which God saves, and it is Trinitarian from end to end. The Father loves the world and gives and sends the Son (John 3:16–17), and he loves the Son and has given all things into his hand (John 3:35). The Son descends from heaven, speaks what he has seen, is lifted up, and gives life (John 3:13–15, 32). The Spirit gives the new birth freely, like the wind (John 3:5–8), and rests on the Son without measure (John 3:34). Read through the Reformed conviction that Scripture interprets Scripture, the chapter locates salvation entirely in the work of the triune God, while calling every hearer to believe.

*Regeneration and the order of salvation.* In the Reformed ordering, the Spirit's effectual calling and regeneration come first, and faith and repentance flow from the new life given; justification rests on faith alone, and sanctification follows. The Westminster Confession, in its chapter on effectual calling (chapter 10), teaches that in this work a person is altogether passive until quickened and renewed by the Holy Spirit, and is thereby enabled to answer the call. John 3 supplies the picture behind that teaching: no one contributes to his own birth, and the Spirit blows where he wills (John 3:8). John 1:12–13 and 1 John 5:1 make the same connection between being born of God and believing. Arminian and Wesleyan theology, by contrast, holds that God's prevenient grace enables every hearer to believe or refuse, and that regeneration follows faith; its holders stress John 3:16's 'whosoever' and the real responsibility of John 3:18. The question is argued in the Council's Hard Questions.

*Covenant.* The new birth is new-covenant language. Jesus expects the teacher of Israel to know it (John 3:10) because the prophets promised it: the circumcised heart (Deuteronomy 30:6), the law written within (Jeremiah 31:33), the clean water, new heart, and indwelling Spirit (Ezekiel 36:25–27), the wind that breathes on dry bones (Ezekiel 37:9–10). John 3 announces that the time of fulfilment has come in Jesus.

*Atonement.* The serpent lifted up is one of the Bible's richest types of the cross. Its logic is substitution: the image of what was killing the people was lifted up in their sight, and the dying lived by looking. In the Reformed reading, the Son was made "sin for us, who knew no sin" (2 Corinthians 5:21), and became "a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13), bearing the judgment of those who believe. John 3:14 grounds that doctrine in Jesus' own words, and John 3:16 grounds it in the Father's love, so that the atonement is never pictured as a loving Son appeasing an unloving Father.

*The love and wrath of God.* The chapter holds God's love (John 3:16) and God's wrath (John 3:36) side by side, and neither cancels the other. The Reformed tradition distinguishes God's general benevolence to all his creatures and his sincere offer of the gospel to all from his particular, effectual love for his elect, and John 3:16 has been read within both. What the text requires every tradition to affirm is that God's love is the source of the Son's mission, and that wrath remains on those who refuse the Son.

*Judgment realized and future.* John's eschatology is 'already' as well as 'not yet'. The believer 'hath everlasting life' now (John 3:36) and 'is not condemned' (John 3:18); the unbeliever is 'condemned already'. Yet the final resurrection and judgment are still to come (John 5:28–29). The last day will reveal and confirm the verdict that faith or unbelief already shows.

### ⚔️ Debate Corner
**The question:** What does Jesus mean by 'born of water and of the Spirit' (John 3:5)?

**Position 1 · Water means Christian baptism, the means of new birth.** This is the dominant reading of the early church (Justin Martyr's *First Apology* connects John 3:3–5 with baptism, verify) and the teaching of the Roman Catholic Church and of the Lutheran and some Anglican traditions: in baptism the Spirit regenerates. Its holders would stress that John 3 sits between two scenes of baptizing (John 1:25–33; John 3:22–23), that the early Christian reader would inevitably hear 'water' as baptism, that Paul speaks of "the washing of regeneration" (Titus 3:5), and that Peter joins baptism and the gift of the Spirit (Acts 2:38). They do not usually teach that baptism works apart from God; they hold that God has bound himself to work through it.

**Position 2 · Water means natural birth; Spirit means the second birth.** Some read 'water' as physical birth (the waters of the womb, or procreation), so that Jesus says: you have been born once, of water; you must be born again, of the Spirit. Its holders point to Nicodemus's question about the mother's womb (John 3:4) and to the pairing in verse 6: 'born of the flesh' and 'born of the Spirit'. Its weakness is that a condition beginning 'except' would then require something every human being already has, and the ancient evidence for 'water' as a term for natural birth is thin.

**Position 3 · Water and Spirit are one birth: the cleansing and renewal God promised in Ezekiel.** Calvin (verify) and many Reformed and evangelical commentators, Carson among them (verify), read 'water and the Spirit' as one act of God described two ways, as in Ezekiel 36:25–27: clean water sprinkled to cleanse from sin, and a new heart and God's own Spirit put within. (A related view takes the water as John's baptism of repentance and the Spirit as what Jesus adds, John 1:33; it shares much of this reasoning.) Its holders point to four features of the text: a single preposition governs both nouns (John 3:5); the following verses speak only of the Spirit (John 3:6, 8), which fits if the water is an aspect of the Spirit's work; Jesus rebukes Nicodemus for not knowing this (John 3:10), which requires an Old Testament background, not a sacrament not yet instituted; and the whole Gospel treats water as a picture of what the Spirit gives (John 4:14; John 7:38–39).

**Most consistent with Reformed hermeneutics.** The Reformed rule that Scripture interprets Scripture points first to the Scriptures Jesus says Nicodemus should have known, and there the prophets join water and Spirit as the two sides of one promised renewal. Position 3 best explains John 3:10 and the single preposition, and it keeps the chapter's emphasis on the sovereign freedom of the Spirit (John 3:8), which cannot be bound to any rite. This does not despise baptism. The Westminster Confession (chapter 28, verify the section) teaches that baptism is a sign and seal of this very washing, to be received in obedience, while holding that grace is not so tied to it that no one can be regenerated without it, or that everyone baptized is regenerated. Christians who read John 3:5 as baptism are right that the sign and the thing signified belong together in God's appointment; the Reformed reading holds that the thing signified, the Spirit's cleansing, is what Jesus requires here, and that it is received by faith in the lifted-up Son (John 3:14–15).

**Pastoral Warning:** This debate has real effects in a congregation. If the water of John 3:5 is taken to mean that everyone baptized has been born again, churches stop asking their own members the question Jesus asked a religious leader, and many grow up assuming a life they do not have. If, in reaction, baptism is treated as a mere ceremony, churches lose a sign Christ commanded and a comfort he gave. Do not let the argument become a party badge. Teach the chapter so that every hearer, baptized or not, faces Jesus' words 'Ye must be born again', and is pointed to the Son lifted up.

### 🔤 Original Language Deep Dive
Every Greek and Hebrew definition below comes from one source: the brief lexicon published by STEPBible.org (Tyndale House, Cambridge), whose Greek entries are drawn largely from G. Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament*. It is called 'the STEPBible lexicon' below.

**'Again' or 'from above'.** *Anōthen* (G509, John 3:3) is an adverb that occurs 13 times in the Textus Receptus of the New Testament, 5 of them in John (John 3:3, 7, 31; John 19:11, 23). The STEPBible lexicon gives two senses: (a) 'from above', listing John 3:31 and John 19:11 with the meaning 'from heaven'; and (b) 'from the first', and thence 'anew, again', listing John 3:3 and John 3:7 there, with a note that most take it so but not all. In John 19:23 it describes Jesus' coat woven 'from the top'. So the KJV's 'again' is a defensible rendering, and 'from above' is too. Because John himself uses the word for 'from above' three verses from the end of this chapter, and because the chapter contrasts what is 'of the earth' with what is 'from heaven' (John 3:12, 31), many interpreters conclude that John intends both: a second birth whose source is above. Nicodemus hears 'a second time', *deuteron* (G1208, John 3:4). It would be overreach to say the word 'literally means' either sense alone.

**The verb of birth.** Every 'born' in John 3 is a form of *gennaō*, which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'to beget' of a father and 'to bring forth, bear' of a mother, and in the passive 'to be begotten, to be born' (it lists John 3:4 and John 3:6). In John 3:3, *gennēthē* (G1080, John 3:3, V-APS-3S) is an aorist passive subjunctive: 'unless someone is born'. The passive is the point: birth is something that happens to you. In John 3:6 and 3:8 the participle is perfect passive, *gegennēmenon* (G1080, John 3:6, V-RPP-NSN) and *gegennēmenos* (G1080, John 3:8, V-RPP-NSM): 'the one who has been born', a past event with a lasting state. 'Born' occurs 8 times in John 3. The same perfect appears in 1 John 5:1, "Whosoever believeth that Jesus is the Christ is born of God" (1 John 5:1), where 'believeth' is a present participle and 'is born' is *gegennētai* (G1080, 1 John 5:1, V-RPI-3S), 'has been born'. Reformed interpreters see here that ongoing faith is the evidence of a prior new birth; others reply that the perfect describes a state, not an order of events. Both observations are grammatical; the conclusion is theological and is argued in the Council's Hard Questions.

**Wind, breath, Spirit.** *Pneuma* (G4151, John 3:8, N-NSN) is listed by the STEPBible lexicon first as 'air in motion', 'wind', with John 3:8 as its example, and then as breath and spirit. The Hebrew *ruach* (H7307) has the same range: wind, breath, mind, spirit. Jesus' saying therefore works as a play in either language: 'The wind (*pneuma*) bloweth where it listeth... so is every one that is born of the Spirit (*pneuma*).' The verb 'listeth' is *thelei*, 'wills' or 'wishes'; the wind has its own will, as the Spirit does.

**'Lifted up'.** *Hupsōthēnai* (G5312, John 3:14, V-APN), an aorist passive infinitive, depends on *dei* (G1163, John 3:14), 'must'. The STEPBible lexicon defines *hupsoō* as 'to lift or raise up', listing John 3:14, John 8:28, and John 12:32, 34, and metaphorically 'to exalt' (Acts 2:33). In the Greek Old Testament it chiefly renders the Hebrew for 'to be high, to raise'. Many point out that the Greek Old Testament uses this verb in Isaiah 52:13, where the servant "shall be exalted and extolled, and be very high" (Isaiah 52:13), just before the song of his suffering (verify the Septuagint wording). The pole of Numbers 21:8 is *nes* (H5251), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'something lifted up, standard, signal, signal pole, ensign, banner'. The serpent itself is *nachash* (H5175).

**'Only begotten'.** *Monogenēs* (G3439, John 3:16) is defined in the STEPBible lexicon as 'only, only begotten', used of only sons and daughters (Luke 7:12; Luke 8:42; Luke 9:38), of Isaac (Hebrews 11:17), and of Christ (John 1:14, 18; John 3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9). In the Greek Old Testament it translates the Hebrew *yachid* (H3173), 'only, only one', used of Jephthah's daughter, "his only child" (Judges 11:34). Scholars debate whether the word's parts point to 'only-begotten' (from the verb for birth) or 'one of a kind' (from the noun for kind). Either way it marks the Son as unique and beloved; the church's doctrine of the eternal generation of the Son rests on the whole witness of Scripture to the Father and the Son, not on this word alone.

**'So'.** *Houtōs* (G3779, John 3:16) is defined by the STEPBible lexicon as 'in this way, so, thus'. Its ordinary sense is manner: 'in this way God loved the world, that he gave'. In John 3:16 it follows 'even so' (the same word) in John 3:14. The English 'so' can mean 'in this way' or 'so much'; the manner reading is the more natural Greek, and the manner revealed, giving the only Son, shows the greatness.

**Judgment already.** 'Condemned already' is *kekritai* (G2919, John 3:18, V-RPI-3S), a perfect passive: the verdict stands. The STEPBible lexicon lists 'to judge, adjudge' among the verb's senses, and in context the judging is condemnation. 'Condemnation' in John 3:19 is *krisis* (G2920, John 3:19), judgment, and the verse defines it as the revealed preference for darkness.

**Exposed.** *Elegchthē* (G1651, John 3:20, V-APS-3S) is an aorist passive subjunctive: in plain words, so that his deeds are not exposed. The STEPBible lexicon gives the senses 'to convict', 'to reprove, rebuke', and 'to expose', and places John 3:20 under 'to expose' with Ephesians 5:11 and Ephesians 5:13. The same verb appears for the Spirit's work, "he will reprove the world of sin" (John 16:8). What the unbeliever flees in John 3:20, the Spirit brings to bear in mercy in John 16:8.

**Disobedience and wrath.** In John 3:36 the two sides are *pisteuōn* (G4100, John 3:36, V-PAP-NSM), 'the one believing', and *apeithōn* (G544, John 3:36, V-PAP-NSM), 'the one disobeying'. The STEPBible lexicon defines *apeitheō* as 'to disobey, be disobedient', and lists John 3:36. The KJV's 'believeth not' is a fair rendering, because in John unbelief toward the Son is disobedience to God. *Orgē* (G3709, John 3:36) is listed by the STEPBible lexicon under God's anger in the sense of its effect, and *menei* (G3306, John 3:36, V-PAI-3S) is present: it remains.

**A textual note on John 3:13.** The KJV, following the Textus Receptus and the great majority of Greek manuscripts, ends John 3:13 with 'even the Son of man which is in heaven'. Several early Greek manuscripts lack the last words, and many modern editions and translations omit them or print them in a note (verify the particular manuscripts). Those who defend the words point to their wide attestation and to their difficulty: a scribe would be more likely to drop a hard phrase than invent one. Those who omit them judge that they were added as an explanation. This study teaches the text the KJV prints. Both readings confess that the Son of man came down from heaven, and the longer reading states in addition what John 1:18 says of the Son 'which is in the bosom of the Father'.

### 🚷 Preaching Mistakes to Avoid
- **Information without life:** a sermon on Pharisees, the Sanhedrin, Numbers 21, and the Greek of *anōthen* that never asks the hearers whether they have been born again or points them to the Son lifted up.
- **Heat without light:** thundering 'Ye must be born again' as a threat, with no Son lifted up, no love of God, and no invitation. John 3:7 without John 3:14–16 is a door slammed shut.
- **Decisionism:** telling people that saying a prayer or walking an aisle makes them born again. Jesus says the Spirit gives the birth; our call is to proclaim Christ and urge people to believe on him, and then to look for the sound of the wind in their lives, not to certify it by a technique.
- **Sacramentalism by default:** assuming every baptized, churchgoing hearer is born again because of the ceremony, so that John 3:3 is preached only to outsiders.
- **John 3:16 without John 3:36:** preaching a love that saves from nothing. The love of God in John 3 rescues from perishing and from wrath that abides.
- **Making Nicodemus a villain:** the point is not that a bad man needed changing, but that a good one did.

## 🟣 Tier 4 — Christocentric, Humility, Life & Light
### 👑 Every Answer in Jesus
John 3 demolishes every ground of self-glory. Religious rank is no help: the ruler of the Jews cannot see the kingdom. Learning is no help: the teacher of Israel does not understand. Heritage is no help: what is born of the flesh is flesh. Effort is no help: no one gives birth to himself. Even a great ministry is no ground of boasting: the greatest prophet born of women says he must decrease. Every human being in the chapter is shown to be dependent, and the chapter's closing verdict divides the whole race by one question only: what have you done with the Son?

And every good thing the chapter holds is found in him. The new birth comes from above, and he is the one from above. The Spirit gives it, and the Spirit rests on him without measure. Heaven's truth is spoken by the only one who came from heaven. Life for the dying comes by looking to him lifted up. The love of God is measured by the gift of him. The light that has come into the world is he. The Bridegroom whose voice fills his friend's joy is he. All things are in his hand, and everlasting life belongs to whoever believes on him. This is what Paul means when he says of Christ, "In whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." (Colossians 2:3) The chapter humbles us by showing that we bring nothing to our salvation but a snakebite and a need, and it makes Christ glorious by showing that he is lifted up for exactly such people.

## 📚 Doctrine
### Theology Proper · The Love of God Measured by His Gift
**Proposition:** God's love for a rebellious world is not a feeling that stays in heaven but a free and costly act: the Father gave his only begotten Son. That love is not caused by anything lovely in the world. It rises from God himself, and it rests first and eternally on the Son.

**Textual Anchor:** "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son" (John 3:16); "The Father loveth the Son, and hath given all things into his hand." (John 3:35) The 'world' John has in view is the world that "knew him not" (John 1:10), and the manner of the love, *houtōs* (G3779, John 3:16), is shown by the gift.

**Cross-References:** "In this was manifested the love of God toward us, because that God sent his only begotten Son into the world, that we might live through him." (1 John 4:9); "But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us." (Romans 5:8); "He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all, how shall he not with him also freely give us all things?" (Romans 8:32)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *A God of love would never judge.* Many today would say: 'If God is love, then wrath, condemnation, and perishing are later human ideas projected onto him.' John 3 puts the gift of the Son and the wrath that abides (John 3:36) in the same breath; the love is shown precisely by rescue from real perishing. (2) *The Son had to win over an unwilling Father.* Some popular preaching of the cross pictures a loving Son pacifying an angry Father. John 3:16 makes the Father's love the source of the Son's coming, not its result. (3) *God loves us because we are lovable.* The world loved darkness (John 3:19); God loved it anyway. His love is free, as he told Israel: "The LORD did not set his love upon you... because ye were more in number" (Deuteronomy 7:7).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Assurance that is measured by the cross, not by circumstances. Worship that begins with the Father's heart and not only with our need. And a humility that stops asking 'why me?' in pride and starts asking it in wonder.

### Christology · The Son of Man from Heaven, Above All
**Proposition:** Jesus is the Son of man who came down from heaven, the only one who speaks of heavenly things as an eyewitness; he is above all, the Father has given all things into his hand, and faith or unbelief toward him decides every person's destiny.

**Textual Anchor:** "no man hath ascended up to heaven, but he that came down from heaven, even the Son of man which is in heaven" (John 3:13); "He that cometh from above is above all" (John 3:31); "what he hath seen and heard, that he testifieth" (John 3:32).

**Cross-References:** "For I came down from heaven, not to do mine own will, but the will of him that sent me." (John 6:38); "And Jesus came and spake unto them, saying, All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth." (Matthew 28:18); "that in all things he might have the preeminence" (Colossians 1:18)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus is one great teacher among many.* This is Nicodemus's own starting point, and it is widely held today: a 'teacher come from God' (John 3:2), to be ranked with other sages. John 3 answers that every other teacher, even the greatest prophet, 'is of the earth', while the Son alone 'cometh from heaven' and is 'above all' (John 3:31). (2) *Jesus became the Son of God at his baptism or resurrection.* Adoptionist views, ancient and modern, hold that a man was raised to divine sonship. The Son of man 'came down from heaven' (John 3:13); he did not rise to it from below. (3) *The Son is a created being.* The Watchtower Society holds that the Son is God's first creation. But the Son speaks the very words of God, has the Spirit without measure, and holds 'all things' in his hand (John 3:34–35), and John has already said that "all things were made by him" (John 1:3).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Confidence in his word: he has seen what he tells us. Worship: the one we trust is above all. And an end to shopping among teachers: there is no second source for heavenly things.

### Pneumatology · The Spirit Gives the New Birth Freely
**Proposition:** The Holy Spirit is the author of the new birth. He gives it sovereignly and freely, as the wind blows where it wills; it is not produced, controlled, or scheduled by human effort or ritual, though its effects are seen. The same Spirit rests on the Son without measure, so that the Spirit who gives life comes to us through Christ.

**Textual Anchor:** "Except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God." (John 3:5); "The wind bloweth where it listeth... so is every one that is born of the Spirit." (John 3:8); "for God giveth not the Spirit by measure unto him." (John 3:34)

**Cross-References:** "And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them." (Ezekiel 36:27); "It is the spirit that quickeneth; the flesh profiteth nothing" (John 6:63); "by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost;" (Titus 3:5)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The Spirit is an impersonal force.* Some groups, including the Watchtower Society, describe the holy spirit as God's active force rather than a person. John 3 speaks of the wind to picture the Spirit's freedom, not to reduce him to it: he 'listeth', he wills, and in Jesus' later teaching he teaches, testifies, and reproves (John 14:26; John 15:26; John 16:8). (2) *New birth can be produced by technique.* Some revival practice, following methods associated with Charles Finney (verify), taught that conversion follows when the right means are used in the right way. John 3:8 does not forbid preaching and pleading, but it denies that anyone controls the result. (3) *The Spirit is given only to a spiritual elite after conversion.* Every one born of God is born of the Spirit (John 3:8); the Spirit is not a later upgrade but the author of the first life.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Prayer, because the Spirit's work is his. Hope for the hardest heart, because the wind goes where it will. And patience in evangelism: we sow and water, and we listen for the sound of the wind.

### Anthropology & Sin · Flesh Begets Flesh, and Men Love Darkness
**Proposition:** Human nature, apart from the Spirit, can only reproduce itself; it cannot rise to the life of God. The root of this is not ignorance but a will turned away from God: people love darkness rather than light because their deeds are evil, and they avoid the light to escape exposure.

**Textual Anchor:** "That which is born of the flesh is flesh" (John 3:6); "men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil." (John 3:19); "For every one that doeth evil hateth the light, neither cometh to the light, lest his deeds should be reproved." (John 3:20)

**Cross-References:** "Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me." (Psalm 51:5); "Because the carnal mind is enmity against God: for it is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be." (Romans 8:7); "And you hath he quickened, who were dead in trespasses and sins;" (Ephesians 2:1)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *People are basically good and need only teaching.* Pelagius in the early church, and much modern moralism, holds that human nature is able to obey God if it is instructed and encouraged. Nicodemus had the best instruction in the world; Jesus says he needs a birth, not a lesson (John 3:3, 6). (2) *Unbelief is only a lack of evidence.* Many honest sceptics would say: 'Give me proof and I will believe.' John 3:19–20 does not deny that people have real questions, but it says the deepest reason people refuse the light is moral: they love what the light would expose. (3) *The religious are closer to life than the irreligious.* The flesh can be very religious; 'born of the flesh is flesh' was said to a teacher of Israel.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Honesty about ourselves. Compassion without naivety toward unbelievers, whose problem is ours too. And gratitude: if we have come to the light, it is because God brought us.

### Soteriology & Grace · No Entrance Without the New Birth
**Proposition:** Regeneration, the new birth from above, is necessary for every person without exception before he can see or enter the kingdom of God. It is God's work, in which he gives new life; its fruit is faith in Christ and coming to the light.

**Textual Anchor:** "Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." (John 3:3); "Marvel not that I said unto thee, Ye must be born again." (John 3:7) The necessity is *dei* (G1163, John 3:7), and the verb is passive: *gennēthē* (G1080, John 3:3, V-APS-3S), 'be born'.

**Cross-References:** "Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new." (2 Corinthians 5:17); "Of his own will begat he us with the word of truth, that we should be a kind of firstfruits of his creatures." (James 1:18); "Whosoever believeth that Jesus is the Christ is born of God" (1 John 5:1)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Everyone baptized as an infant is regenerate.* Baptismal regeneration, taught by the Roman Catholic Church and in the Lutheran tradition, holds that God has promised to give the new birth in baptism, and its holders would add that faith must be nourished for that life to continue. The Reformed churches honour baptism as a sign and seal, but John 3 addresses a man who bore the covenant sign of his day and tells him he must be born again; the sign does not guarantee the thing signified (see the Debate Corner). (2) *Being born again is for a certain kind of Christian.* Some treat it as the mark of one style of piety. Jesus says 'Ye must', plural, to all. (3) *Being born again is a decision we make.* In popular evangelism it is often equated with praying a prayer. Faith and repentance are real acts we perform, but birth is something done to us (John 1:13).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Humility, because no one contributes to his own birth. Urgency, because no one enters without it. And tenderness, because the one who needs it most may be sitting in the choir.

### Soteriology & Grace · The Son Lifted Up in the Place of the Dying
**Proposition:** The Son of man must be lifted up on the cross, as the brass serpent was lifted up in the wilderness, so that dying sinners who look to him in faith may live. The remedy is God's provision; it bears the likeness of the curse; and it saves by substitution, the Son made sin and a curse for those who believe.

**Textual Anchor:** "And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up:" (John 3:14); "That whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have eternal life." (John 3:15) The infinitive *hupsōthēnai* (G5312, John 3:14, V-APN) depends on the same 'must'.

**Cross-References:** "For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him." (2 Corinthians 5:21); "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13); "But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed." (Isaiah 53:5)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The cross is only an example of love.* The moral influence view, associated with Peter Abelard (verify) and much modern liberal theology, holds that the cross saves by moving us to love God in return. The serpent did not heal by inspiring the Israelites; it healed because God appointed it as the remedy for a deadly bite, and the Son is lifted up 'that whosoever believeth in him should not perish'. (2) *Looking is too simple; something must be added.* Some teach that faith must be completed by works of merit or by ritual before life is secure. The bitten Israelite 'beheld the serpent of brass' and 'lived' (Numbers 21:9). (3) *The lifting up is only exaltation, not death.* John tells us plainly what the lifting up signified: "what death he should die" (John 12:33).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Rest, because the remedy is finished and outside us. Evangelism that lifts up Christ rather than technique. And a daily habit of looking away from our wounds to him.

### Soteriology & Grace · Everlasting Life Through Believing
**Proposition:** Everlasting life is received through believing on the Son, not through works, rank, heritage, or ritual. Faith is the empty hand that receives the gift; it rests on the person of the Son; and it is offered to 'whosoever', without exception of class, nation, or past.

**Textual Anchor:** "that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." (John 3:16); "He that believeth on him is not condemned" (John 3:18); "He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life" (John 3:36).

**Cross-References:** "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God:" (Ephesians 2:8); "But to him that worketh not, but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness." (Romans 4:5); "Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved" (Acts 16:31)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Faith plus works.* The Council of Trent's decree on justification (1547) teaches that justification is increased by good works and can be lost by mortal sin; its holders would say this honours God's grace working in us. The Reformed churches reply that works are the fruit of new life and never its ground: John 3 makes believing the one condition of having life (John 3:16, 36). (2) *Faith as mere assent.* Some treat faith as agreeing that facts are true. John's phrase is believing 'in him', *eis*, trust that goes out and rests on a person, and it 'receiveth his testimony' (John 3:33). (3) *Universalism.* Some hold that all will finally have life whether they believe or not. John 3 sets two outcomes side by side, 'perish' or 'everlasting life', and says the one who believes not 'shall not see life' (John 3:36).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Simplicity in the gospel we speak. Freedom from the treadmill of earning. And confidence to offer Christ to anyone, since 'whosoever' leaves no one out who comes.

### Eschatology · The Verdict of the Last Day Given Now
**Proposition:** The judgment of the last day is anticipated in the present. The believer already has everlasting life and is not condemned; the unbeliever is condemned already, and the wrath of God remains on him. The final resurrection and judgment will reveal and confirm this verdict, not reverse it.

**Textual Anchor:** "he that believeth not is condemned already" (John 3:18), the perfect *kekritai* (G2919, John 3:18, V-RPI-3S); "He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life: and he that believeth not the Son shall not see life; but the wrath of God abideth on him." (John 3:36)

**Cross-References:** "He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life, and shall not come into condemnation; but is passed from death unto life." (John 5:24); "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1); "which delivered us from the wrath to come." (1 Thessalonians 1:10)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Eternal life begins only after death.* Many believers think of eternal life as a future reward only. John 3:36 says the believer 'hath' it now. (2) *Everything is decided only at the end, so no one can know.* Some traditions teach that assurance of final salvation is not ordinarily possible in this life. John 3:18 gives the believer a present verdict. (3) *Only a future judgment, nothing now.* Others, including some existentialist readings of John associated with Rudolf Bultmann (verify), keep the present judgment and drop the future one. John keeps both: John 5:24 and John 5:28–29 stand side by side.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Assurance for the believer, who need not live on probation. Seriousness in evangelism, because unbelief is not a waiting room but a verdict already standing. And hope, because today the sentence can still be lifted.

### Christian Walk · He Must Increase, but I Must Decrease
**Proposition:** The servant of Christ receives every gift and every field of service from heaven, rejoices in Christ's honour above his own, and willingly decreases so that Christ may increase. Rivalry in ministry is unbelief in the giver; joy in the Bridegroom is its cure.

**Textual Anchor:** "A man can receive nothing, except it be given him from heaven." (John 3:27); the friend of the bridegroom 'rejoiceth greatly because of the bridegroom's voice' (John 3:29); "He must increase, but I must decrease." (John 3:30)

**Cross-References:** "For who maketh thee to differ from another? and what hast thou that thou didst not receive?" (1 Corinthians 4:7); "For we preach not ourselves, but Christ Jesus the Lord; and ourselves your servants for Jesus' sake." (2 Corinthians 4:5); "But God forbid that I should glory, save in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ" (Galatians 6:14)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Ministry success proves God's favour, and decline proves his displeasure.* Church-growth thinking can slide into this, and its holders mean well: fruit matters. John's ministry was shrinking by God's plan, and he called it joy fulfilled. (2) *Humility means self-hatred.* John does not despise himself; he knows who he is ('sent before him', John 3:28) and who he is not. (3) *Loyalty to a leader is loyalty to Christ.* John's disciples defended their master's following. John himself sent them to the Bridegroom.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Freedom from comparison. Gladness when others are used. And leaders who point past themselves, and step aside when the time comes.

### Assumed Here
- **Ecclesiology:** The chapter assumes a gathered company of disciples who baptize under Jesus' authority (John 3:22; John 4:2), and its bride-and-bridegroom picture (John 3:29) points to the church as Christ's bride, but it does not teach the church's nature or order. That is taught plainly in Matthew 28:19–20, Ephesians 5:25–27, and Revelation 19:7–8.

## ⚖️ The Council's Hard Questions
### ❓ Question 1 · Whom Does God So Love, and What Does Whosoever Mean
**Moderator:** John 3:16 is the best-known verse in the Bible, and it is argued over as much as it is loved. Does 'the world' mean every person, and does God's love here intend the salvation of every person? And how does 'whosoever' fit with teaching elsewhere in John about the Father giving and drawing?

**Philologist:** Begin with the words. *Kosmos* (G2889, John 3:16) in John is usually humanity in its rebellion: the world that "knew him not" (John 1:10) and "loved darkness" (John 3:19). The stress is on the quality of what God loved, not first on its number. And 'whosoever believeth' is, in the Greek, 'every one who believes': *pas* (G3956, John 3:16) with the present participle *pisteuōn* (G4100, John 3:16, V-PAP-NSM). It is universal in its offer to every believer, and it says nothing either way about who will believe.

**Theologian:** The Reformed tradition reads John 3:16 as a sincere, universal offer grounded in real love: God loves the world, commands all to believe, and promises life to every one who does. Scripture also teaches that God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked: "As I live, saith the Lord GOD, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live" (Ezekiel 33:11). But John also teaches that "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him" (John 6:44), and Jesus says the good shepherd lays down his life "for the sheep" (John 10:15). So the Reformed hold together God's love for the world shown in the gift and offer of the Son, and his particular, effectual love that brings the elect to faith.

**Church Historian:** And it is fair to say that the Arminian and Wesleyan traditions, and much of the evangelical world, read the verse differently. They hold that God's love here intends the salvation of each person, that Christ died for all alike, and that prevenient grace enables every hearer to believe or refuse. They point to "Who will have all men to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4), to the Lord "not willing that any should perish" (2 Peter 3:9), and to Christ as the propitiation "for the sins of the whole world" (1 John 2:2). Lutheran theology has its own answer, holding both God's universal grace and unconditional election without resolving the tension. These are not careless readers; they are guarding the sincerity of the offer.

**Theologian:** And the Reformed would say that the sincerity of the offer is not at stake: every one who believes will have life, without exception, and no one who comes will be cast out (John 6:37). The difference is whether God's love in John 3:16 is a purpose to save every individual that can fail, or a love for the world that sends the Son and saves all who believe, where believing is itself God's gift (John 1:12–13; Ephesians 2:8).

**Pastor:** Whatever we conclude, a pastor must be able to look anyone in the eye and say: God so loved the world that he gave his Son, and if you believe on him you will not perish. Both traditions preach that. If our debate ever makes us hesitate to say it, we have misused the verse.

**Resolution:** John 3:16 teaches that God's love for a rebellious world is shown in the gift of his only begotten Son, and that every one who believes on him, without exception, will have everlasting life. Christians differ on whether this love is an equal saving intent toward each person (Arminian and Wesleyan) or a love for the world that stands alongside a particular, effectual love for the elect (Reformed). This study holds the Reformed reading, because John himself sets the Father's giving and drawing beside the 'whosoever' (John 6:37, 44), but the verse is to be preached to everyone, freely and sincerely, by all (Isaiah 45:22).

### ❓ Question 2 · Does New Birth Come Before Faith, or Faith Before New Birth
**Moderator:** In John 3 Jesus speaks first of new birth (John 3:3–8) and then of believing (John 3:15–18). John 1:12 says believers are given power to become sons of God. Which comes first: does the Spirit give new life so that we believe, or do we believe and then receive new life?

**Philologist:** The grammar will not settle it alone, but it points somewhere. In 1 John 5:1, 'believeth' is a present participle and 'is born', *gegennētai* (G1080, 1 John 5:1, V-RPI-3S), is perfect: "Whosoever believeth that Jesus is the Christ is born of God" (1 John 5:1). The one who is now believing has been born of God. That is at least consistent with birth as the source of faith. Those who disagree reply, fairly, that a perfect can describe a present state without fixing the order in which it began.

**Theologian:** The Reformed argue from the whole shape of John 3. Birth is passive: no one takes part in his own conception. Flesh can only give birth to flesh (John 3:6), and those who love darkness do not come to the light (John 3:20). If that is our condition, faith must be the first breath of a life God has already given. John 1:13 says the children of God were born "not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God" (John 1:13). The Westminster Confession (chapter 10, verify the section) and the Canons of Dort teach this order: regeneration, and then faith as its fruit. Logically, not necessarily in time: the two may happen in the same moment, as a baby's birth and first cry do.

**Church Historian:** The Arminian and Wesleyan traditions answer that John 1:12 puts receiving him before becoming sons: "But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name" (John 1:12). They hold that prevenient grace, given to all, enables the sinner to believe, and that God regenerates the one who believes. They would stress that John 3 commands belief and condemns unbelief (John 3:18), which assumes that hearers are responsible to respond.

**Literary Scholar:** Notice how John orders the chapter itself. Nicodemus asks 'How?' (John 3:9), and Jesus does not answer with a procedure. He points to the Son lifted up (John 3:14–15). The new birth is described from God's side; faith is called for from ours. John never tells Nicodemus to regenerate himself, and he never tells him to wait passively either. He tells him to look.

**Teacher:** For our group, the practical answer matters more than the order. A participant who asks, 'How do I get born again?' should be told what Jesus told Nicodemus: look to the Son lifted up and believe on him. If they believe, the Spirit has been at work; if they cannot yet, they can ask him to give what only he can give.

**Resolution:** Scripture teaches both that the new birth is God's sovereign work (John 3:5–8; John 1:13) and that believing on the Son is the hearer's real responsibility (John 3:18, 36). The Reformed hold that regeneration logically precedes and produces faith (1 John 5:1); Arminian and Wesleyan Christians hold that grace enables faith and regeneration follows it (John 1:12). Both call every hearer to believe now. This study holds the Reformed order, because John 3 describes birth as something done to us by the Spirit and faith as its fruit, while it keeps the call to look and live at the centre (John 3:14–15).

### ❓ Question 3 · Did Nicodemus Come to the Light
**Moderator:** Nicodemus's last words in John 3 are 'How can these things be?' (John 3:9). John never tells us in so many words that he believed. Was he born again, and what should we make of secret disciples?

**Historian:** Follow him through the Gospel. In John 7 the chief priests and Pharisees are furious that the officers did not arrest Jesus, and Nicodemus, 'being one of them', asks: "Doth our law judge any man, before it hear him, and know what he doeth?" (John 7:51) They answer with contempt: "Art thou also of Galilee?" (John 7:52) At the burial he comes with Joseph of Arimathaea and "brought a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about an hundred pound weight" (John 19:39). That was a vast amount, a burial fit for a king, given publicly at the moment when Jesus' followers had scattered.

**Literary Scholar:** And John frames each appearance with the night. In John 7:50 and John 19:39 he reminds us that this was the man who came 'by night'. That reminder works like a signpost: the man who came by night is now seen by day. In a Gospel where people are divided by whether they come to the light (John 3:19–21), Nicodemus's path runs from darkness toward light.

**Church Historian:** Some readers are less hopeful. They set Nicodemus beside the rulers of John 12:42–43, who believed but did not confess him "lest they should be put out of the synagogue" (John 12:42), "For they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God." (John 12:43) Joseph of Arimathaea is called a disciple "but secretly for fear of the Jews" (John 19:38). On this reading, Nicodemus's lavish spices honour a dead teacher rather than a risen Lord. Later Christian tradition, however, often counted him a believer, and some legends made him a martyr (verify); those legends are not Scripture.

**Pastor:** Whatever we conclude, John is asking the reader the question Jesus asked Nicodemus. I would not tell our group he was a coward, and I would not tell them he was a hero. I would tell them he was a man on the road from night to day, and ask them where they are on that road.

**Resolution:** John does not state that Nicodemus believed, and readers differ. The text shows him moving: from a night visit (John 3:2), to a public request for a fair hearing (John 7:51), to open and costly devotion at the burial (John 19:39), when being associated with Jesus was most dangerous. That movement fits John 3:21, the one who 'cometh to the light'. The warning of John 12:42–43 stands for secret believers in every age, and the hope of Nicodemus's story stands too: the Spirit who blows where he wills can bring a cautious, learned, respected man into the light.

### 🔦 The Life & Light Audit
**Pastor:** If a hearer obeyed this lesson exactly, they would stop leaning on their record and look to the Son lifted up, in plain prayer, tonight. One religious-but-lifeless relative or friend would be asked, gently, what John 3:3 means to them, and one person who thinks they are too far gone would receive John 3:16 in a handwritten note. The dark place lit would be one hidden deed, confessed to God by name, and where someone has been wronged, put right openly.

**Theologian:** The practices keep source discipline. None asks the hearer to make themselves born again, to generate faith by effort, or to produce light of their own. The Inward practices are all acts of looking and confessing, the posture of the bitten Israelite (Numbers 21:9) and of the one who 'cometh to the light' (John 3:21). The Outward practices carry a verse and point to the Son; the hearer is Moses lifting up what God provided, and the Baptist stepping aside.

**Teacher:** A Level 1 participant can do the Inward Life practice tonight: read two short passages and pray for two minutes. The Outward practices name the kind of person to reach (someone raised in church without life in it, someone who thinks they are too far gone, someone ashamed who expects condemnation) and leave a blank for the name, so a newcomer knows exactly what to do.

**Fix:** The Audit checked three risks and found each already handled in the unit, so no further change was needed. The Outward Light practices ask the hearer to put right their own wrong and to read John 3:17 with an ashamed person, never to expose someone else's sin. The Gather naming round reads only the second row of the Light Planner, never the private first row. And A Line to Say addresses both the respectable and the despairing, since John 3 speaks to both.

### ✅ Answer Key
**Q1.** He was 'of the Pharisees', his name was Nicodemus, and he was 'a ruler of the Jews' (John 3:1). He came to Jesus 'by night' (John 3:2).

**Q2.** He says, "we know that thou art a teacher come from God" (John 3:2). His evidence is the signs: "for no man can do these miracles that thou doest, except God be with him" (John 3:2).

**Q3.** "Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." (John 3:3) "Except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God." (John 3:5) 'Born again' is explained as 'born of water and of the Spirit', and 'see' becomes 'enter'.

**Q4.** He compares 'every one that is born of the Spirit' to the wind. The wind blows where it wills, you hear its sound, and you cannot tell where it comes from or where it goes (John 3:8).

**Q5.** Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, and in the same way 'must the Son of man be lifted up', so that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life (John 3:14–15).

**Q6.** They reported that the one John had borne witness to was baptizing, "and all men come to him" (John 3:26). John's first words are "A man can receive nothing, except it be given him from heaven" (John 3:27), and his last are "He must increase, but I must decrease." (John 3:30)

**Q7.** God must do the inward work: cleanse us, give a new heart, put his Spirit within us, and write his law there (Ezekiel 36:25–27; Jeremiah 31:33), and he does it through his word (1 Peter 1:23). Our part is to look and believe, as the bitten Israelites looked (Numbers 21:8–9): "Look unto me, and be ye saved, all the ends of the earth" (Isaiah 45:22). The passages together say that salvation is God's work from start to finish, received by faith.

**Q8.** The crowds in John 2:23–25 believed because of signs, and Jesus did not trust himself to them, because 'he knew what was in man' (John 2:25). Nicodemus reasons from the same signs (John 3:2). Jesus does not need more admirers; Nicodemus needs a new life, and without it he cannot even see the kingdom he is waiting for (John 3:3).

**Q9.** The people had spoken against God and were bitten and dying under his judgment; they confessed, "We have sinned" (Numbers 21:7). God provided a serpent of brass on a pole, and a dying person only had to look at it to live (Numbers 21:8–9). The lifted-up Son saves in the same way: God provides the remedy, it is lifted up where all can see it, and it is received by believing, not by effort (John 3:14–15).

**Q10.** The Son came to save, but he came into a world already under condemnation because of sin. Those who will not believe stay under the verdict that already stands, "because he hath not believed in the name of the only begotten Son of God" (John 3:18), and their refusal shows that they love darkness (John 3:19).

**Q11.** Model answer, not the only answer: View A fits the text best. Jesus expects Nicodemus to know this already (John 3:10), which points to an Old Testament promise such as Ezekiel 36:25–27; one preposition governs both water and Spirit (John 3:5); and verses 6 and 8 speak only of the Spirit. View B's texts are real (Acts 2:38; Titus 3:5), and baptism is the sign of this cleansing, but Christian baptism had not yet been given when Jesus spoke to Nicodemus. Let members who hold View B state it at its strongest.

**Q12.** Saul had to stop trusting his own righteousness, his heritage, and his zeal, and count them as loss (Philippians 3:7–9). His story answers 'How can these things be?' (John 3:9): not by self-improvement but by God acting from above, suddenly and freely, like the wind (John 3:8).

**Q13.** Answers will vary: church attendance, a family's faith, a past decision, good works, Bible knowledge. To 'look' is to turn from all of these and trust Christ alone as the one lifted up for us (John 3:14–15).

**Q14.** Because Scripture says the new birth is of God, not of the will of man (John 1:13; John 3:8). It humbles us: we contributed nothing. It gives hope: the Spirit can blow into any heart, however closed, as the breath came into the dry bones (Ezekiel 37:9). So we pray and speak the word, and we do not give up.

**Q15.** To believe in the Son is to trust him personally as the one God gave and lifted up, and to receive his testimony as true (John 3:16, 33). 'Whosoever' includes any person of any background, respectable or ruined. It leaves out no one who believes, but it leaves out every other ground of trust, and it does not include the one who refuses to believe (John 3:18).

**Q16.** Model answer: God measures his love by giving his only begotten Son, not by how lovable we are. On days we feel unlovable, the cross is the fixed proof: "He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all, how shall he not with him also freely give us all things?" (Romans 8:32)

**Q17.** Answers will vary: a coworker's promotion, another church's growth, a sibling's success, another member's gift. Loving Christ more than our place means rejoicing that he is honoured, whoever he uses, because everything we have was given from heaven (John 3:27, 29).

**Q18.** Model answers: telling one friend or family member that I am following Jesus; asking my question aloud in the group rather than keeping it private; asking the pastor about baptism; praying aloud in the group for the first time. Humbling ourselves means admitting to God first that we have been afraid of being seen (John 3:21).

**Q19.** No one contributes to his own birth. Jesus chose a picture that leaves no room for boasting: the new life comes wholly from God (John 1:13; John 3:6). That humbles us, because we bring nothing but our need, and it makes Christ glorious, because all the credit belongs to God who gives life through his Son (John 3:16).

**Q20.** Model answers: a churchgoing relative for whom faith is only habit; a religious friend who admires Jesus as a good teacher; a colleague who is moral and kind but has never thought they needed to be born again (John 3:3).

**Q21.** Model answers: the thief may have stolen the knowledge that they need anything, or the hope that anyone can change, or the joy that comes from the Bridegroom's voice. The thief comes "to steal, and to kill, and to destroy" (John 10:10).

**Q22.** Christ gives everlasting life: not perishing, but having the life of God now and forever (John 3:15–16). It is enough for both because the cure depends on the one lifted up, not on the patient: the respectable need it as much as the ruined, and the ruined can receive it as freely as the respectable.

**Q23.** Model answers: a religious parent I assume is fine; a friend in addiction I have given up on; a neighbour I think would never be interested. 'Whosoever' means we have no right to decide in advance who is beyond it (John 3:16).

**Q24.** Model answers: a habit, a resentment, a lie, a secret spending pattern, a private fear. We are afraid it would show us as we are. But the one who shines the light came to save, and "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness." (1 John 1:9)

**Q25.** Model answers: praying honestly about what I really feel; letting a trusted friend know my struggles; no longer hiding my faith at work; making things right with someone. When good comes out into the light, it is shown to be God's work, not ours (John 3:21).

**Q26.** Model answer: a friend ashamed of a past abortion, prison, divorce, or addiction, who expects the church to reject them. Show them John 3:17, listen first, and let them see that the Son came to save, not to condemn. Jesus was known as the friend who 'receiveth sinners' (Luke 15:2).

### 🗺️ Life & Light Leader's Map
**Worked Life Planner Row:** *Who:* my uncle Frank, who has been an usher at his church for thirty years and calls himself a good Christian, but who told me last Christmas that he has never really thought about Jesus much. *What the thief has stolen:* the knowledge that he needs anything; his religion has become a substitute for life. *What Christ gives instead:* everlasting life by looking to the Son lifted up (John 3:14–16). *What I will do this week:* call him on Saturday, ask about his church, and then ask what he makes of Jesus telling a religious leader 'Ye must be born again' (John 3:7), listening more than I talk. *Done?* A tick, and a note of what he said.

**Worked Light Planner Row:** *Where it is dark:* my own heart: I have been telling my small group that my marriage is fine, and it is not. *What is hidden there:* pride, and fear of being seen to fail. *What light Christ shines:* he came not to condemn but to save (John 3:17), and the one who does truth comes to the light (John 3:21). *How I carry it:* confess my pride to the Lord tonight, and on Thursday ask one trusted friend in the group to pray with me about my marriage. This first row stays private; in the naming round members read their second row.

**When Someone Has No One to Name:** Do not accept 'no one' too quickly, and do not shame the person either. John 3 gives two kinds of people to think of: the Nicodemus who seems fine (religious, respectable, comfortable) and the one who thinks they are too far gone. Walk through their week with them and ask, 'Who in your family, workplace, or church seems to have religion but no life? Who seems to think God could never want them?' Most people can name one of each. If they still cannot, suggest they ask the Lord for one name this week (Matthew 9:38).

**When Someone Points the Light at Others:** John 3:19–20 can be turned into a weapon: 'those people love darkness'. When a member uses it on a family member, a coworker, or the culture, bring them gently back to the first row of their Light Planner. Remind the group that in John 3 the Son was sent to save the world, not to condemn it (John 3:17), and that the person who comes to the light is the one who brings his own deeds into it (John 3:21). The Love Guard is printed in the lesson for this reason. If there is a real concern about another person's sin, point to the private, humble way of Matthew 18:15, after the beam has come out of their own eye (Matthew 7:5).

### 🐑 Shepherding Notes
This chapter touches several tender places. Handle them with care.

- **Assurance.** Members raised in church may leave this lesson afraid they have never been born again, especially if they cannot name a date. Do not stir up doubt for its own sake. Point them where Jesus pointed Nicodemus: to the Son lifted up. The question is not 'can I remember my birth?' but 'am I looking to Christ now?' The one who believes on him 'is not condemned' (John 3:18), and "him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out" (John 6:37).
- **Loved ones who do not believe.** John 3:36 speaks of the wrath of God abiding on the one who believes not. For members who have lost an unbelieving parent, spouse, or child, this can be crushing. Do not speculate about anyone's eternal state; we do not know what passed between a dying person and God. Grieve with them, and turn their grief into prayer for the living.
- **Shame.** John 3:19–20 speaks of deeds done in the dark. Some members carry secrets: abortion, abuse suffered or committed, addiction, betrayal. The lesson invites them to come to the light, but coming to the light happens first before God, not before the group. If someone discloses something serious to you afterwards, receive it quietly, pray with them, and, where others are at risk or the law requires it, get appropriate help.
- **Those who feel too far gone.** Some think they have done too much for God to love them. Linger on 'whosoever' and on 'not to condemn' (John 3:16–17).
- **Leaders and servers.** John 3:26–30 can sting anyone whose ministry has shrunk, who has been replaced, or who envies another's gifts. Speak of the Baptist's joy, not as a rebuke but as a freedom offered.
- **The Light Planner's first row.** It is private: never ask anyone to read it aloud, and say so at the start of the naming round. If someone chooses to share, receive it quietly, pray, and keep it in the group.

### 🎯 Teaching Angles
**For a Tier 1 Group:** Tell the story of the snakes in the wilderness first (Numbers 21:4–9), then Nicodemus's night visit. Bring a picture of a caterpillar and a butterfly, or a baby photo, to show that birth is something that happens to us. Ask: What did Jesus say Nicodemus needed? What did the people in the wilderness have to do to live? What does God so loving the world mean for me? Use the Tier 1 section, and end by saying John 3:16 together.

**For a Tier 2 Group:** Trace two lines through the chapter. The line of 'above' and 'earth': born from above (John 3:3), heavenly and earthly things (John 3:12), the one who came down from heaven (John 3:13), the one from above who is above all (John 3:31). The line of light and darkness: Nicodemus by night (John 3:2), the light come into the world (John 3:19–21), and Nicodemus's later appearances (John 7:50; John 19:39). Then show how the Baptist's testimony (John 3:27–36) ties the two lines together in the Son.

**Tough Question 1:** *Do I need a dramatic experience, or a date, to know I am born again?* No. Jesus compares the Spirit's work to the wind: you cannot see it, but you can hear its sound (John 3:8). Some people remember a day; many who grew up in believing homes do not. The evidence John gives is present faith in the Son and coming to the light (John 3:21; 1 John 5:1). Ask 'am I trusting Christ now?', not 'can I remember when I started?'

**Tough Question 2:** *What about people who never hear of Jesus? Are they condemned for not believing?* John 3:18 speaks of those who have heard and not believed. Scripture also teaches that all people are already under condemnation for sin against the light they have (Romans 1:20; Romans 2:14–15), which is why the Son was sent to save (John 3:17). The answer the chapter presses on us is not speculation but mission: "how shall they hear without a preacher?" (Romans 10:14). Faithful Christians differ on some details here; do not claim more than Scripture says.

**Tough Question 3:** *Is baptism necessary for salvation? Does John 3:5 mean the unbaptized cannot enter the kingdom?* Christians differ (see the Debate Corner). Baptism is commanded by Christ and is not optional for a believer who can receive it (Matthew 28:19). But the thief on the cross was promised paradise without it (Luke 23:43), and the water of John 3:5 is best read against Ezekiel 36:25–27, the cleansing the Spirit gives. We are saved by the Son lifted up, received by faith (John 3:14–15), and baptism is the sign and seal of that washing, not its source.
