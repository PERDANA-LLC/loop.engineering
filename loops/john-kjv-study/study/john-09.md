# John 9 · Once Blind, Now Seeing (KJV)
> Unit john-09 · John 9:1–41 · loop john-kjv-study v2.0 · DRAFT for human doctrine review

## 🧭 At a Glance
### 🧵 Golden Thread
Jesus gives sight to a man born blind and shows that those who confess their blindness receive light, while those who say We see remain in their sin.

### 🌗 Life & Light Line
John 9 answers a death that begins at birth and is finished by exclusion: a man who has never seen, who sits and begs, whom his neighbours cannot recognise, whose parents will not stand with him, and whom the religious court at last casts out with the verdict 'Thou wast altogether born in sins' (John 9:34). Christ gives instead a life that seeks the outcast: Jesus 'heard that they had cast him out; and when he had found him' (John 9:35), he gave him faith, a Lord to worship, and a place among his own sheep. The chapter exposes the darkness of people who are sure they see, who say 'we know' and will not look at a man whose eyes have been opened (John 9:24, 29), and whose sin therefore 'remaineth' (John 9:41); the light Christ gives instead is himself, 'the light of the world' (John 9:5), received by anyone who will say with the healed man, 'whereas I was blind, now I see' (John 9:25).

### 🎯 Objectives
- Follow the healed man's growing confession of Jesus (John 9:11, 17, 33, 38) alongside the Pharisees' growing blindness (John 9:16, 24, 29, 34, 40–41), and explain from John 9:39–41 why confessing blindness is the way to sight.
- Explain Jesus' answer to the disciples' question about suffering (John 9:2–5), stating what it denies and what it affirms, without turning it into either a charge against the sufferer or a reason to doubt God's goodness.
- **Doing:** Within the next day, tell one person in a few plain sentences what Jesus has done for you, as the healed man did (John 9:25), and seek out one person who has been pushed to the edge of a group, as Jesus sought the man who was cast out (John 9:35).

## 🏛️ Core Foundations
### ✝️ Christological Root
**Gospel Foundation:** John 9 rests on one claim of Jesus and one act that proves it. The claim, spoken before the healing: "As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world." (John 9:5) The act, which no one had ever done: "Since the world began was it not heard that any man opened the eyes of one that was born blind." (John 9:32) And the chapter turns on the moment when the light of the world goes looking for the man the world has thrown out: "Jesus heard that they had cast him out; and when he had found him, he said unto him, Dost thou believe on the Son of God?" (John 9:35) It ends with the man's answer, the goal of the whole Gospel in one line: "And he said, Lord, I believe. And he worshipped him." (John 9:38)

**The Bridge:** In John 8 Jesus said in the temple, 'I am the light of the world' (John 8:12), and was answered with stones. In John 9 he says it again on the road and shows what it means: he gives sight to a man who has never seen. The healing is a sign, a deed that points beyond itself: the eyes that open at Siloam picture the heart that God opens to see the glory of Christ (2 Corinthians 4:6). The doctrines grounded here are Christ as the light of the world and the one sent by the Father (John 9:4–5, 7); God's wise and good purpose in human suffering (John 9:3); the sovereign grace that sees, sends, and seeks the lost before they seek him (John 9:1, 35); the Son of God as the rightful object of faith and worship (John 9:35–38); and the terrible blindness of people who are sure that they see (John 9:39–41).

**Canonical Echoes:** Jesus (John 9:5) → Promise (Isaiah 35:5; Isaiah 42:7; Psalm 146:8) → Doctrine (2 Corinthians 4:4–6; Ephesians 5:8) → Life (Acts 26:18; 1 Peter 2:9)

**What Gets Lost:** Taught without Christ at the centre, John 9 falls apart into lessons that each go wrong. The healing becomes a wonder story, or a promise that faith will always cure disease. The disciples' question becomes a debate about the problem of evil with no one to answer it. The man's courage becomes a model of standing up to authority. The parents become a warning about peer pressure. And 'For judgment I am come' (John 9:39) becomes a club for beating people we think are blind. With Christ at the centre, every piece holds together: the one who sees the beggar is the light of the world; the pool is named Sent because the Sent One stands beside it; the one who finds the outcast is the Son of God, who receives his worship; and the one who judges the proud is the same one who opens the eyes of anyone who knows that he is blind.

### ⚔️ Spiritual Warfare
**The Lie:** You can see well enough already. Your knowledge, your tradition, and your certainty are sight: 'we know that this man is a sinner' (John 9:24); 'we know not from whence he is' (John 9:29). The lie has a second edge, used against the weak: suffering proves guilt, so the blind man and his family must have sinned (John 9:2, 34). And it has a third, used against the fearful: say nothing, because confessing Christ will cost you your place (John 9:22).

**The Truth:** Jesus names both the blindness and the cure: "And Jesus said, For judgment I am come into this world, that they which see not might see; and that they which see might be made blind." (John 9:39) "Jesus said unto them, If ye were blind, ye should have no sin: but now ye say, We see; therefore your sin remaineth." (John 9:41)

**The Battleground:** The mind's certainties, the family, and the congregation. The mind that says 'we know' and closes the case before looking at the evidence (John 9:24, 29). The family, where parents choose safety over their son (John 9:21–22). And the gathered community, which can use its power to cast out the very people Christ has touched (John 9:34). The tongue is in the fight too: reviling, labelling, and the pious formula 'Give God the praise' used to force a lie (John 9:24, 28).

**The Weapon:** A plain testimony of what Christ has done, held without shame: "He answered and said, Whether he be a sinner or no, I know not: one thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see." (John 9:25) The healed man did not win by cleverness but by refusing to deny what the Lord had done, and by reasoning from Scripture's own logic (John 9:30–33). Behind his witness stands the one who opened his eyes and then found him, for Christ came 'To open their eyes, and to turn them from darkness to light, and from the power of Satan unto God' (Acts 26:18).

### 🌱 Life Pathway
**The Theft:** In John 9 the thief steals a whole life in stages. Before birth he has taken the man's sight (John 9:1). Poverty follows, and the man is reduced to one who 'sat and begged' (John 9:8). Then he is turned into a question, discussed in front of him as though he were not there: 'who did sin, this man, or his parents' (John 9:2). When he is healed, the thief tries to steal the joy of it by suspicion, interrogation, and a demand that he call his healer a sinner (John 9:15, 24). He steals the man's family, whose parents leave him to face the court alone (John 9:21–23). And he steals his place among God's people: 'they cast him out' (John 9:34). He comes 'for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy' (John 10:10), and the religious machinery of the day served him well.

**The Source:** Christ, who says, "I must work the works of him that sent me, while it is day: the night cometh, when no man can work." (John 9:4) He came 'that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly' (John 10:10). His means in this chapter are his seeing eyes (John 9:1), his word of command, 'Go, wash in the pool of Siloam' (John 9:7), the humble clay and spittle he chose to use (John 9:6), and above all his own seeking of the outcast: 'when he had found him' (John 9:35). In the next breath he will call himself the good shepherd who 'calleth his own sheep by name' (John 10:3).

**The Channel:** The healed man never healed anyone. What he did was tell what had happened to him, again and again, to neighbours, to judges, and to hostile questioners: 'I went and washed, and I received sight' (John 9:11). That is how life passes through a believer: by testimony to what Christ has done, and by going where he went, to the people others have put out. The believer is a witness, not the Sent One; we are like the pool that carried the name of the one who gives sight. "For without me ye can do nothing." (John 15:5)

**The Abundance Guard:** Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, not prosperity, ease, or freedom from suffering. The man in John 9 received his sight and lost, the same day, his standing in the synagogue, the support of his parents, and his good name among the leaders (John 9:22, 34). His life became fuller, not easier: he gained a Lord to worship (John 9:38). Nor does John 9 promise that every blind eye will open in this life if we believe enough; Jesus chose this man, on this day, 'that the works of God should be made manifest in him' (John 9:3), and many faithful believers wait for the day when God will wipe away all tears (Revelation 21:4).

### 💡 Light Pathway
**The Darkness:** The darkness of John 9 is the darkness of certainty without sight. The disciples see a blind man and think first of blame (John 9:2). The neighbours see a changed man and argue about whether it is really him (John 9:8–9). The Pharisees see a sign and think first of the sabbath rules (John 9:16). They decide the verdict before hearing the evidence ('we know that this man is a sinner', John 9:24), call the witness's parents to break his story, and when the witness will not bend, they revile him and throw him out (John 9:28, 34). The parents know the truth and hide it for fear (John 9:22). And the deepest darkness is the one Jesus names at the end: to be blind and to say 'We see' (John 9:41).

**The Source:** Christ, who says, "As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world." (John 9:5) "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." (2 Corinthians 4:6)

**The Lamp:** In John 9 the lamp is a man's honest account of what Jesus did, 'one thing I know' (John 9:25), set against a court's refusal to look. It is Scripture's own logic applied honestly (John 9:31–33). And it is Christ's question, which brings every hearer to the point: 'Dost thou believe on the Son of God' (John 9:35). For us the lamp is the same: the Scriptures opened, our own blindness confessed before anyone else's is mentioned, the unfruitful works of darkness reproved (Ephesians 5:11), and good works that glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16), including the work of standing beside the one whom others have cast out.

**The Love Guard:** Light without love is glare. The healed man spoke boldly to his judges, and even with a sharp edge ('will ye also be his disciples', John 9:27), but he never reviled them, never called them names, and never cast anyone out. Jesus' hardest words in the chapter (John 9:39–41) were spoken to men who had just expelled a helpless man, and even then he spoke to open eyes, not to crush them: the door of sight stands open to anyone who will confess his blindness. The Pharisees' light was all glare: interrogation, contempt, and expulsion. Christ's light is grace and truth together (John 1:14): he finds the outcast first, and speaks to him face to face.

### 🏺 Setting the Scene
John 9 follows straight on from John 8. Jesus has just said in the temple at the feast of tabernacles, 'I am the light of the world' (John 8:12), and has left the temple as the crowd took up stones (John 8:59). As he 'passed by' (John 9:1), he sees a man who has been blind from birth. Beggars often sat where crowds passed, near the gates of the temple and on the roads into Jerusalem (compare Acts 3:2). In that world a man born blind had almost no way to earn a living; he depended on the gifts of passers-by. Many people also assumed that such a disability must be a punishment for sin, either the person's own or his parents' (see Exodus 20:5, which speaks of the iniquity of the fathers visited on the children). Some rabbis even discussed whether a child could sin before birth (verify the rabbinic sources), and the disciples' question reflects these ideas.

The pool of Siloam lay at the south end of the old city of David, where the water of the Gihon spring arrived through the tunnel that King Hezekiah cut (2 Kings 20:20; 2 Chronicles 32:30; the identification of the tunnel with the Siloam channel is mainstream, verify the details). A large stepped pool from the time of Jesus was uncovered there in 2004 (verify). The Old Testament calls these waters Shiloah (Isaiah 8:6), a name that sounds like the Hebrew word for 'sent'. Later Jewish tradition says that during the feast of tabernacles the priests drew water from Siloam each morning and poured it out at the altar (Mishnah *Sukkah* 4.9, verify). If so, the feast where Jesus cried 'let him come unto me, and drink' (John 7:37) had used water from this very pool.

The healing happened on a sabbath (John 9:14). The law forbade work on the sabbath (Exodus 20:10), and by Jesus' time teachers had defined many kinds of forbidden work, including kneading, which making clay resembles (Mishnah *Shabbat* 7.2, verify). By the time John wrote, and perhaps already during Jesus' ministry, the synagogue could formally exclude people, which is what the parents feared (John 9:22). Being 'put out of the synagogue' meant losing one's place in worship, in community, and often in family and trade. Listen for three questions the chapter keeps asking: How were his eyes opened (John 9:10, 15, 19, 26)? Where does Jesus come from (John 9:16, 29, 33)? And who is really blind (John 9:39–41)?

#### John 9:1–7 · The Light Opens Blind Eyes
**1** And as Jesus passed by, he saw a man which was blind from his birth.

**2** And his disciples asked him, saying, Master, who did sin, this man, or his parents, that he was born blind?

**3** Jesus answered, Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him.

**4** I must work the works of him that sent me, while it is day: the night cometh, when no man can work.

**5** As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world.

**6** When he had thus spoken, he spat on the ground, and made clay of the spittle, and he anointed the eyes of the blind man with the clay,

**7** And said unto him, Go, wash in the pool of Siloam, (which is by interpretation, Sent.) He went his way therefore, and washed, and came seeing.

#### John 9:8–12 · Is Not This He
**8** The neighbours therefore, and they which before had seen him that he was blind, said, Is not this he that sat and begged?

**9** Some said, This is he: others said, He is like him: but he said, I am he.

**10** Therefore said they unto him, How were thine eyes opened?

**11** He answered and said, A man that is called Jesus made clay, and anointed mine eyes, and said unto me, Go to the pool of Siloam, and wash: and I went and washed, and I received sight.

**12** Then said they unto him, Where is he? He said, I know not.

#### John 9:13–17 · He Is a Prophet
**13** They brought to the Pharisees him that aforetime was blind.

**14** And it was the sabbath day when Jesus made the clay, and opened his eyes.

**15** Then again the Pharisees also asked him how he had received his sight. He said unto them, He put clay upon mine eyes, and I washed, and do see.

**16** Therefore said some of the Pharisees, This man is not of God, because he keepeth not the sabbath day. Others said, How can a man that is a sinner do such miracles? And there was a division among them.

**17** They say unto the blind man again, What sayest thou of him, that he hath opened thine eyes? He said, He is a prophet.

#### John 9:18–23 · He Is of Age; Ask Him
**18** But the Jews did not believe concerning him, that he had been blind, and received his sight, until they called the parents of him that had received his sight.

**19** And they asked them, saying, Is this your son, who ye say was born blind? how then doth he now see?

**20** His parents answered them and said, We know that this is our son, and that he was born blind:

**21** But by what means he now seeth, we know not; or who hath opened his eyes, we know not: he is of age; ask him: he shall speak for himself.

**22** These words spake his parents, because they feared the Jews: for the Jews had agreed already, that if any man did confess that he was Christ, he should be put out of the synagogue.

**23** Therefore said his parents, He is of age; ask him.

#### John 9:24–34 · One Thing I Know
**24** Then again called they the man that was blind, and said unto him, Give God the praise: we know that this man is a sinner.

**25** He answered and said, Whether he be a sinner or no, I know not: one thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see.

**26** Then said they to him again, What did he to thee? how opened he thine eyes?

**27** He answered them, I have told you already, and ye did not hear: wherefore would ye hear it again? will ye also be his disciples?

**28** Then they reviled him, and said, Thou art his disciple; but we are Moses' disciples.

**29** We know that God spake unto Moses: as for this fellow, we know not from whence he is.

**30** The man answered and said unto them, Why herein is a marvellous thing, that ye know not from whence he is, and yet he hath opened mine eyes.

**31** Now we know that God heareth not sinners: but if any man be a worshipper of God, and doeth his will, him he heareth.

**32** Since the world began was it not heard that any man opened the eyes of one that was born blind.

**33** If this man were not of God, he could do nothing.

**34** They answered and said unto him, Thou wast altogether born in sins, and dost thou teach us? And they cast him out.

#### John 9:35–38 · Lord, I Believe
**35** Jesus heard that they had cast him out; and when he had found him, he said unto him, Dost thou believe on the Son of God?

**36** He answered and said, Who is he, Lord, that I might believe on him?

**37** And Jesus said unto him, Thou hast both seen him, and it is he that talketh with thee.

**38** And he said, Lord, I believe. And he worshipped him.

#### John 9:39–41 · We See
**39** And Jesus said, For judgment I am come into this world, that they which see not might see; and that they which see might be made blind.

**40** And some of the Pharisees which were with him heard these words, and said unto him, Are we blind also?

**View A:** When the healed man says 'God heareth not sinners' (John 9:31), he states a true principle that Scripture teaches plainly, and he applies it rightly. God does not answer those who cherish sin and refuse to turn from it: "If I regard iniquity in my heart, the Lord will not hear me:" (Psalm 66:18) "The LORD is far from the wicked: but he heareth the prayer of the righteous." (Proverbs 15:29) John records the man's argument because it is sound: a man through whom God did what had never been done must be one whom God hears, and so cannot be the sinner the leaders say he is (John 9:31–33).

**View B:** When the healed man says 'God heareth not sinners' (John 9:31), he is reasoning as a new believer with the Scripture he knows, and his words are true only in a limited sense. John records them faithfully as the man's argument, not as Jesus' teaching. The Gospels show God hearing the cry of a sinner who asks for mercy: "And the publican, standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner." (Luke 18:13) Jesus says that this man went home justified (Luke 18:14). So the principle holds for the unrepentant and the pretender, but not for the penitent sinner who calls on God.

### 👣 Case Study · Scripture in Action
Saul of Tarsus was a Pharisee, trained in the law, blameless in his own eyes, and sure that the followers of Jesus were blasphemers (Philippians 3:5–6). He was, in the words of John 9:41, a man who said 'We see'. On the road to Damascus the risen Jesus met him in a light brighter than the sun, and Saul got up from the ground blind: "And Saul arose from the earth; and when his eyes were opened, he saw no man: but they led him by the hand, and brought him into Damascus." (Acts 9:8) For three days he sat in darkness, fasting (Acts 9:9). Then the Lord sent a disciple named Ananias to him, and what happened next reads like John 9: "And immediately there fell from his eyes as it had been scales: and he received sight forthwith, and arose, and was baptized." (Acts 9:18) Like the man in John 9, Saul began at once to confess Jesus as 'the Son of God' (Acts 9:20), and like him, he paid for it: the leaders who had once sent him now plotted to kill him (Acts 9:23). Years later he described his whole mission in the language of John 9: Christ sent him 'To open their eyes, and to turn them from darkness to light' (Acts 26:18). And he never forgot that he himself had been blind: 'I obtained mercy, because I did it ignorantly in unbelief' (1 Timothy 1:13).

### 🏙️ Modern Example
Marcus had been drinking hard since he was sixteen. At forty-one, after his second arrest, a man from a church recovery group started meeting him for breakfast every Tuesday, and over a year Marcus came to trust Christ. He had been sober for eight months when his family gathered for his father's seventieth birthday. His brother-in-law, who taught philosophy at a community college, cornered him in the kitchen. Did Marcus really think a Jewish carpenter rose from the dead? Had he read any of the arguments against God? Wasn't this just one addiction swapped for another? Marcus felt his face go hot. He did not have answers to half of it. Then he remembered what his group had studied the week before. He said, *I can't answer all your questions. I'm not a philosopher. But I know this: a year ago I couldn't go a day without a drink, and my kids wouldn't talk to me. Today I've been sober eight months, and my daughter called me last night just to talk. I know who did that.* His brother-in-law rolled his eyes and walked away, and two of Marcus's old friends stopped inviting him out. It stung. But the next Sunday, a man in his group asked him to tell his story to a new member who had just come out of rehab. Marcus found that the one thing he knew was the one thing that man needed to hear.

### 📜 Voices from Church History
John Newton, once a slave-ship captain and later a minister in the Church of England, wrote the hymn now known as *Amazing Grace* for his congregation at Olney, and it was published in the *Olney Hymns* of 1779 (verify the date and the original title). ### ⛪ Doctrine in My Own Words
- **Theology Proper:** God rules even over a man's blindness from birth, and in his wise purpose he uses it so that his works are shown (John 9:3; Exodus 4:11).
- **Christology:** Jesus is the light of the world, sent by the Father to do his works, and he gives sight that no one else had ever given (John 9:4–5, 32).
- **Christology:** Jesus is the Son of God, who reveals himself to the one who seeks him and rightly receives his faith and worship (John 9:35–38).
- **Anthropology & Sin:** All people are born in sin and blind to God, and the worst blindness is to be blind and insist that we see (John 9:34, 39–41).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** Christ takes the first step and the last: he sees the blind man, opens his eyes, and finds him when he is cast out, bringing him to faith (John 9:1, 6–7, 35–38).
- **Ecclesiology:** Those whom men cast out for confessing Christ are found and gathered by Christ himself, the shepherd of his own flock (John 9:22, 34–35; John 10:16).
- **Eschatology:** Christ's coming brings judgment into the world now, dividing those who confess their blindness from those who say they see, while the day of his work lasts (John 9:4, 39).
- **Christian Walk:** A disciple bears witness to what Christ has done, plainly and without fear, even when it costs him his place (John 9:25, 27, 30–33).

### 👑 Christocentric & Humility · Level 4
Every problem in John 9 is answered in Jesus. A man is blind from birth, and no one has ever opened such eyes; Jesus, the light of the world, opens them (John 9:5, 32). The disciples want to know whose fault it is; Jesus answers that God has a work to do and that he has come to do it (John 9:3–4). The man cannot find his way to the one who healed him ('I know not', John 9:12); Jesus finds him (John 9:35). The neighbours are not sure who he is; he is sure, because he knows what Jesus did. The Pharisees say Jesus cannot be of God because of the sabbath; the Lord of the sabbath is doing the Father's work, which does not rest (John 5:17; 9:4). The parents are silent for fear; their son is given courage. The leaders say 'we know'; the man they despise knows the one thing that matters. They cast him out; Jesus receives him, reveals himself, and receives his worship. And to the men who say 'We see', the light still offers the only cure: to confess their blindness and receive sight from him.

Every truth in the chapter is found in him. Light is in him (John 9:5). The works of God are done by him (John 9:3–4). He is the Sent One whom the pool's name points to (John 9:7). He is 'of God', as even a new believer could see (John 9:33). He is the Son of God, the only rightful object of saving faith and worship (John 9:35–38). And he is the judge, whose coming divides the world (John 9:39). The chapter humbles every reader. It humbles the curious, because the disciples wanted an explanation and received a mission. It humbles the religious, because the best-taught people in the story were the blindest. It humbles the fearful, because the parents chose safety and missed their son's greatest day. And it makes Christ glorious, because the eternal light of the world stooped to spit in the dust, went looking for a beggar the world had thrown out, and let a man who had never seen anything come at last to see the Son of God.

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy
### 📌 Core Concept
As Jesus was walking along, he saw a man who had been blind since the day he was born. He had never seen the sky, or a tree, or his mother's face. He sat by the road and begged for money. Jesus' friends asked a question: *Whose fault is it that he was born blind? Did he do something wrong, or did his parents?* Jesus said it was not because of anyone's sin. It was so that people could see God at work. Then Jesus said, 'I am the light of the world'. He spat on the ground, made a little mud, put it on the man's eyes, and told him to go and wash in a pool called Siloam. The name Siloam means *Sent*. The man went, washed, and came back able to see! But instead of everyone being happy, people started arguing. The neighbours were not sure it was the same man. The religious leaders were angry, because Jesus had done this on the Sabbath, the day of rest. They asked the man over and over how it happened. They called his mum and dad, who were too scared to say much. The leaders said Jesus was a bad man. The man said: *I don't know about all that. But I know one thing: I was blind, and now I can see!* The leaders got so angry that they threw him out. Then Jesus came and found him, and told him who he was: the Son of God. The man said, 'Lord, I believe', and he worshipped Jesus. Jesus said that people who know they are blind can be given sight, but people who pretend they can see everything stay in the dark.

### 📖 Key Verses
"As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world." (John 9:5) Jesus is like the sun for our hearts: without him, we cannot see God at all.

"And said unto him, Go, wash in the pool of Siloam, (which is by interpretation, Sent.) He went his way therefore, and washed, and came seeing." (John 9:7) The man did what Jesus told him, even before he could see, and Jesus gave him sight.

"He answered and said, Whether he be a sinner or no, I know not: one thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see." (John 9:25) You don't need to know everything to tell people what Jesus has done for you.

"And he said, Lord, I believe. And he worshipped him." (John 9:38) When we see who Jesus really is, we trust him and worship him.

### 🪞 Analogy
Imagine a family who live in a house where all the curtains have been closed for years. They have got used to the dim light, and they think the house looks fine. Then someone throws the curtains open, and the sunshine pours in. One child laughs and says, *Wow, look at all the colours!* But one of the grown-ups squints and gets cross and says, *Shut them! It was perfectly fine before. I could see everything I needed to.* The sunshine was the same for both of them. The difference was that one was glad to see, and the other did not want to admit the room had been dark. Jesus is like the sunshine. People who say *I was in the dark, and now I can see* are glad when he comes. People who say *I can see perfectly well, thank you* stay in the dim room.

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium
### 📜 Walkthrough
#### John 9:1–3 · Who Did Sin?
**Says:** "And as Jesus passed by, he saw a man which was blind from his birth." (John 9:1) "And his disciples asked him, saying, Master, who did sin, this man, or his parents, that he was born blind?" (John 9:2) "Jesus answered, Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him." (John 9:3) *Master* here is the Greek *rhabbi*, teacher; *manifest* means shown openly, displayed.

**Means:** John 8 ended with Jesus leaving the temple as stones were raised against him. 'As Jesus passed by', *paragōn* (G3855, John 9:1), a verb the STEPBible lexicon lists at John 9:1 under 'to pass by', he 'saw a man'. The first move in this chapter is Jesus' own: the man does not call out, as blind Bartimaeus did (Mark 10:47); Jesus sees him. He was blind 'from his birth', *genetēs* (G1079, John 9:1), a word that the STEPBible lexicon lists only here in the New Testament. The detail matters for everything that follows. This is not someone who lost his sight and might regain it; it is a man who has never seen. The church counts this healing as the sixth of the seven signs John narrates in Jesus' ministry (John himself numbers only the first two, John 2:11 and 4:54), and it is the one in which we watch most closely what happens to a person after Jesus touches him.

The disciples see the same man and think of a theological problem. Their question assumes that suffering of this kind is a punishment for someone's particular sin, and it offers two options: his own, or his parents'. The second had a biblical-sounding basis, since the law speaks of 'visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children' (Exodus 20:5), though Ezekiel had already taught that 'The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father' (Ezekiel 18:20). The first raised the puzzle of how a man could sin before he was born, and some Jewish teachers did discuss that possibility (verify the sources). Jesus rejects both options. He does not say the man and his parents were sinless; the whole Bible teaches that 'all have sinned' (Romans 3:23). He says that this blindness was not a punishment for a particular sin of theirs. Jesus says the same elsewhere of people killed in a disaster: 'Suppose ye that these Galilaeans were sinners above all the Galilaeans, because they suffered such things' (Luke 13:2), and he answers his own question, 'I tell you, Nay' (Luke 13:3).

Then Jesus turns from the cause to the purpose: 'but that the works of God should be made manifest in him'. The verb is *phanerōthē* (G5319, John 9:3), 'might be made plain, displayed'. The same pattern appears at Lazarus's sickness: "When Jesus heard that, he said, This sickness is not unto death, but for the glory of God, that the Son of God might be glorified thereby." (John 11:4) Readers differ on how far to press the purpose clause (see the Debate Corner in Tier 3), but the KJV's reading is plain: God had a purpose in this man's life, and the purpose is about to be shown.

**Asks:** When we see someone suffering, our first instinct is often the disciples': to explain it, or to find someone to blame. Jesus' first instinct was to see the person, and his answer pointed to God's work, not to anyone's fault. Before you offer an explanation for someone's pain, ask what work of God you might be part of in their life.

#### John 9:4–5 · The Light of the World, While It Is Day
**Says:** "I must work the works of him that sent me, while it is day: the night cometh, when no man can work." (John 9:4) "As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world." (John 9:5)

**Means:** 'I must', *dei* (G1163, John 9:4), is the language of divine necessity in John: the Son of man 'must' be lifted up (John 3:14); Jesus 'must needs go through Samaria' (John 4:4); other sheep 'I must bring' (John 10:16). What he must do is 'the works of him that sent me'. Jesus is the Sent One of the Father, and the word 'sent' occurs 57 times in the KJV of John; the Gospel never lets us forget where Jesus came from and who sent him (John 5:36). The Textus Receptus behind the KJV reads 'I must'; many early manuscripts read 'We must', joining the disciples to his work (the Deep Dive in Tier 3 explains this calmly). Either way, the Sender is the Father and the work is his.

'While it is day: the night cometh, when no man can work'. The day is the time of Jesus' earthly ministry, now running short; the night is the coming of his hour, when he will be taken (John 11:9–10; 12:35). John will later note, as Judas goes out to betray him, 'and it was night' (John 13:30). Then, in words he spoke in the temple the day before, Jesus says: 'I am the light of the world'. In John 8:12 he claimed it; here he is about to show it. The light that shone in the darkness from the beginning (John 1:4–5) is about to shine into eyes that have never seen any light at all. 'As long as I am in the world' does not mean that he stops being the light when he goes to the Father; it means that the light is shining now, in his bodily presence, and the time to come to it is now (John 12:35–36).

**Asks:** Jesus worked with urgency, because the day was short. Our days are short too. What work of God is in front of you today that you have been putting off until a more convenient time?

#### John 9:6–7 · Clay, Siloam, and the Sent One
**Says:** "When he had thus spoken, he spat on the ground, and made clay of the spittle, and he anointed the eyes of the blind man with the clay," (John 9:6) "And said unto him, Go, wash in the pool of Siloam, (which is by interpretation, Sent.) He went his way therefore, and washed, and came seeing." (John 9:7) *Spittle* means saliva; *anointed* here means smeared, spread on.

**Means:** Jesus 'spat on the ground', *eptusen* (G4429, John 9:6), 'and made clay', *pēlon* (G4081, John 9:6), which the STEPBible lexicon defines here as wet clay or mud, listing John 9:6, 11, 14, and 15. He 'anointed', *epechrisen* (G2025, John 9:6), 'spread on', the man's eyes with it. Why this way, when he healed other people with a word? John does not say. Saliva was used as a remedy in the ancient world (verify the ancient medical sources), and Mark records Jesus using spittle for a deaf man and for a blind man (Mark 7:33; 8:23). Some early Christian writers, Irenaeus among them, saw in the clay an echo of the creation, when 'the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground' (Genesis 2:7), and read the healing as the Creator's own hand finishing a work in a man born without sight (Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.15, verify). That is a reading, not something John says, but it fits his first chapter, where the Word made all things (John 1:3). The clay also matters for the plot: making it on the sabbath is part of what the Pharisees will object to (John 9:14).

Then comes the command: 'Go, wash', *nipsai* (G3538, John 9:7), 'wash yourself', 'in the pool of Siloam'. The man must walk, still blind, with mud on his face, across the city to the pool at its southern end. He is given no promise in words, only a command, and he obeys. The story recalls the prophet Elisha, who sent Naaman the leper to wash in the Jordan, where Naaman was healed only after he obeyed (2 Kings 5:10, 14).

John adds his own explanation, one of his narrator's comments: Siloam, *Silōam* (G4611, John 9:7), is 'by interpretation, Sent', *apestalmenos* (G649, John 9:7). The name comes from the Hebrew *shiloach* (H7975, Isaiah 8:6), which the STEPBible lexicon glosses as 'sent'. John is pointing at something. In the previous verse Jesus called himself the one whom the Father 'sent' (John 9:4). The pool bears the name of the Sent One who stands beside it. Isaiah had warned that 'this people refuseth the waters of Shiloah that go softly' (Isaiah 8:6); in this chapter the leaders will refuse the one those waters point to. 'He went his way therefore, and washed, and came seeing', *blepōn* (G991, John 9:7), a verb the STEPBible lexicon lists at John 9:7 under the sense 'to see, have sight', the opposite of being blind. John tells the miracle itself in one short sentence. The rest of the chapter is about what the man does with his sight.

**Asks:** The man obeyed before he could see. Faith often begins that way: doing what Christ says before we understand how it will work. What has Christ plainly told you to do that you are waiting to understand before you obey?

#### John 9:8–12 · Is Not This He?
**Says:** "The neighbours therefore, and they which before had seen him that he was blind, said, Is not this he that sat and begged?" (John 9:8) "Some said, This is he: others said, He is like him: but he said, I am he." (John 9:9) "Therefore said they unto him, How were thine eyes opened?" (John 9:10) "He answered and said, A man that is called Jesus made clay, and anointed mine eyes, and said unto me, Go to the pool of Siloam, and wash: and I went and washed, and I received sight." (John 9:11) "Then said they unto him, Where is he? He said, I know not." (John 9:12)

**Means:** The neighbours knew him only as 'he that sat and begged', *prosaitōn* (G4319, John 9:8), a word the STEPBible lexicon defines as to keep asking, to beg alms, and lists only here. For years he had been a fixture of the street, known by his need. Now he is changed so much that they argue about whether it is him: 'This is he'; 'He is like him'. He settles it: 'I am he', *egō* (G1473, John 9:9) *eimi* (G1510, John 9:9). These are the same two Greek words Jesus used when he said 'Before Abraham was, I am' (John 8:58), but here they carry their everyday sense, *It's me*. The contrast is instructive: the same words can be an ordinary answer on a man's lips and a claim to the divine name on Jesus'.

'How were thine eyes opened?' This question, or one like it, is asked four times in the chapter (John 9:10, 15, 19, 26). The man's first answer is plain history: 'A man that is called Jesus' made clay, anointed his eyes, sent him to Siloam; 'I went and washed, and I received sight', *aneblepsa* (G308, John 9:11), which the STEPBible lexicon defines at John 9:11 as 'to recover sight'. This is the first stage of the man's growing sight of who Jesus is: to him Jesus is, for now, a man with a name. And he is honest about what he does not know: 'Where is he? He said, I know not'. He had never seen Jesus; he had only heard his voice.

**Asks:** Some of the people who knew us best may be the slowest to believe that Christ has changed us. The man did not argue about his identity; he told what happened. When someone doubts your change, the best answer is often the simplest: *I am he, and this is what Jesus did*.

#### John 9:13–17 · He Is a Prophet
**Says:** "They brought to the Pharisees him that aforetime was blind." (John 9:13) "And it was the sabbath day when Jesus made the clay, and opened his eyes." (John 9:14) "Then again the Pharisees also asked him how he had received his sight. He said unto them, He put clay upon mine eyes, and I washed, and do see." (John 9:15) "Therefore said some of the Pharisees, This man is not of God, because he keepeth not the sabbath day. Others said, How can a man that is a sinner do such miracles? And there was a division among them." (John 9:16) "They say unto the blind man again, What sayest thou of him, that he hath opened thine eyes? He said, He is a prophet." (John 9:17) *Aforetime* means formerly; *keepeth not* means does not observe.

**Means:** The neighbours take the case to the religious authorities. Only now does John tell us the detail that turns a wonder into a controversy: 'it was the sabbath day'. As in John 5, Jesus has healed on the sabbath, and here he has also made clay. Later rabbinic rulings list kneading among the kinds of work forbidden on the sabbath (Mishnah *Shabbat* 7.2, verify), and some discussed whether one might apply saliva to the eyes on the sabbath (verify the sources). The law itself forbade work on the seventh day (Exodus 20:10); what counted as work was the question. Jesus had already answered it: 'My Father worketh hitherto, and I work' (John 5:17), and it is lawful to make a man 'every whit whole on the sabbath day' (John 7:23).

The man's answer grows shorter each time he is asked: 'He put clay upon mine eyes, and I washed, and do see'. The Pharisees split. Some reason from their rule: 'This man is not of God, because he keepeth not the sabbath day'. Others reason from the sign: 'How can a man that is a sinner do such miracles?', where 'miracles' is *sēmeia* (G4592, John 9:16), 'signs', John's own word for Jesus' mighty works, deeds that point to who he is (John 20:30–31). 'And there was a division among them', *schisma* (G4978, John 9:16), the word from which 'schism' comes. It occurs 3 times in the Greek of John, and each time Jesus is its cause (John 7:43; 9:16; 10:19). Jesus always divides people.

Then, remarkably, the judges ask the witness for his opinion. He gives the second step of his confession: 'He is a prophet', *prophētēs* (G4396, John 9:17). In Israel's memory, prophets like Elijah and Elisha had done works of power, and Moses had promised a great Prophet to come (Deuteronomy 18:18). The Samaritan woman climbed the same ladder, calling Jesus a Jew, then 'a prophet', then asking whether he was the Christ (John 4:9, 19, 29).

**Asks:** The Pharisees had a rule, and they let the rule decide what they would see. It is possible to be so sure of our framework that we cannot see God at work in front of us. Where might a rule or a habit of yours be keeping you from seeing what Christ is doing?

#### John 9:18–23 · The Parents Afraid
**Says:** "But the Jews did not believe concerning him, that he had been blind, and received his sight, until they called the parents of him that had received his sight." (John 9:18) "And they asked them, saying, Is this your son, who ye say was born blind? how then doth he now see?" (John 9:19) "His parents answered them and said, We know that this is our son, and that he was born blind:" (John 9:20) "But by what means he now seeth, we know not; or who hath opened his eyes, we know not: he is of age; ask him: he shall speak for himself." (John 9:21) "These words spake his parents, because they feared the Jews: for the Jews had agreed already, that if any man did confess that he was Christ, he should be put out of the synagogue." (John 9:22) "Therefore said his parents, He is of age; ask him." (John 9:23) *Of age* means an adult, old enough to give legal testimony.

**Means:** In John 9:13–17 the questioners were 'the Pharisees'; now John calls them 'the Jews', meaning here, as often in John, the religious authorities in Jerusalem. Everyone in the story is Jewish, including the man, his parents, Jesus, and his disciples. The authorities now try to break the story at its root: perhaps he was never blind at all. So they summon his parents. The parents answer exactly two questions and no more. Yes, this is their son; yes, he was born blind. But how he sees and who opened his eyes, 'we know not'. They almost certainly knew what their son had told everyone. 'He is of age', *hēlikian* (G2244, John 9:23), 'he has age', old enough to answer for himself; in later Jewish practice a son reached legal majority at thirteen (verify for the first century). 'Ask him'.

John tells us why: 'because they feared the Jews'. The authorities had already 'agreed' that anyone who confessed Jesus to be the Christ would be *aposunagōgos* (G656, John 9:22), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'expelled from the congregation, excommunicated'. The word occurs 3 times in the Greek New Testament, all in John (John 9:22; 12:42; 16:2). To be put out of the synagogue meant losing one's place in worship, one's friends, and perhaps one's livelihood. Readers debate whether a formal ban of this kind existed as early as Jesus' ministry or reflects conditions later in the first century (see the Council's first question). Either way, the fear was real, and it silenced many: "Nevertheless among the chief rulers also many believed on him; but because of the Pharisees they did not confess him, lest they should be put out of the synagogue:" (John 12:42) The key verb is 'confess', *homologēsē* (G3670, John 9:22), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as to agree, confess, acknowledge, and which Paul uses for confessing 'the Lord Jesus' (Romans 10:9).

**Asks:** The parents had seen the greatest day of their son's life, and they could not say so. Fear of losing their place cost them their witness. 'The fear of man bringeth a snare' (Proverbs 29:25). Is there a place, a person, or a group whose approval you fear so much that you keep quiet about Christ?

#### John 9:24–25 · One Thing I Know
**Says:** "Then again called they the man that was blind, and said unto him, Give God the praise: we know that this man is a sinner." (John 9:24) "He answered and said, Whether he be a sinner or no, I know not: one thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see." (John 9:25)

**Means:** The second hearing opens with a solemn formula: 'Give God the praise', literally *give glory to God*, with *doxan* (G1391, John 9:24), 'glory'. It was the formula Joshua used to put Achan under oath to tell the truth and confess: 'give, I pray thee, glory to the LORD God of Israel, and make confession unto him' (Joshua 7:19). The authorities are telling the man to own up, to stop giving credit to Jesus, and to give it to God instead. They follow it with their verdict, reached before the evidence: 'we know that this man is a sinner', *hamartōlos* (G268, John 9:24). That word occurs 4 times in the Greek of John, every one of them in this chapter (John 9:16, 24, 25, 31), in the argument over whether Jesus is a sinner. The irony is deep. To give glory to God, in this chapter, is exactly what the man is doing by telling what Jesus did; the Son of God is 'glorified' in the works the Father gives him to do (John 11:4).

The man's reply is the most famous line in the chapter. He will not pretend to settle a theological question he cannot settle: 'Whether he be a sinner or no, I know not'. But he will not deny what he knows: 'one thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see'. The leaders say 'we know' (John 9:24, 29); the phrase 'we know' occurs 7 times in the KJV of John 9, sometimes as 'we know not'. The man sets his one piece of knowledge against their many. His knowledge is not a theory; it is an experience of Christ's power that no argument can take away.

**Asks:** You do not have to answer every question to be a faithful witness. What is the 'one thing' you know about what Christ has done for you? Have you ever said it aloud to someone who did not want to hear it?

#### John 9:26–29 · Will Ye Also Be His Disciples?
**Says:** "Then said they to him again, What did he to thee? how opened he thine eyes?" (John 9:26) "He answered them, I have told you already, and ye did not hear: wherefore would ye hear it again? will ye also be his disciples?" (John 9:27) "Then they reviled him, and said, Thou art his disciple; but we are Moses' disciples." (John 9:28) "We know that God spake unto Moses: as for this fellow, we know not from whence he is." (John 9:29) *Wherefore* means why; *reviled* means abused with insults; *fellow* is a word of contempt here.

**Means:** They ask him to tell it all again, perhaps hoping to catch a contradiction. The man has had enough: 'I have told you already, and ye did not hear'. And then, with a flash of humour and courage, he asks, 'will ye also be his disciples?' The word 'also' gives him away: he already counts himself one. The question expects the answer no, and he knows it; but he asks it anyway.

'Then they reviled him', *eloidorēsan* (G3058, John 9:28), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as to abuse, revile, and which Peter uses of Christ himself, who 'when he was reviled, reviled not again' (1 Peter 2:23). They draw the line: 'Thou art his disciple; but we are Moses' disciples'. They set Moses against Jesus, although Jesus had told them that Moses 'wrote of me' (John 5:46). 'We know that God spake unto Moses: as for this fellow', literally *this one*, spoken with contempt, 'we know not from whence he is'. That is a striking reversal. In John 7 the crowd in Jerusalem said the opposite: 'Howbeit we know this man whence he is' (John 7:27), and so, they reasoned, he could not be the Christ. Whatever they claim to know or not know about his origin, it becomes a reason not to believe. John's readers know the truth that both groups miss: he is from above, sent by the Father (John 8:23, 42).

**Asks:** The man's courage grew as the pressure grew. Opposition can make a new believer bolder, not weaker, when he holds fast to what Christ has done. Where has opposition to your faith made it stronger, and where has it made you quieter?

#### John 9:30–33 · A Marvellous Thing
**Says:** "The man answered and said unto them, Why herein is a marvellous thing, that ye know not from whence he is, and yet he hath opened mine eyes." (John 9:30) "Now we know that God heareth not sinners: but if any man be a worshipper of God, and doeth his will, him he heareth." (John 9:31) "Since the world began was it not heard that any man opened the eyes of one that was born blind." (John 9:32) "If this man were not of God, he could do nothing." (John 9:33) *Herein* means in this; *marvellous* means astonishing.

**Means:** The beggar now teaches the teachers. 'Why herein is a marvellous thing', *thaumaston* (G2298, John 9:30): the astonishing thing is not the miracle but their blindness to it. They are the experts on where God's messengers come from, and they cannot place a man who has done what no one has ever done. His argument has three steps. First, a principle the leaders themselves hold: 'Now we know', he says, using their own phrase, 'that God heareth not sinners: but if any man be a worshipper of God', *theosebēs* (G2318, John 9:31), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'God-fearing, godly' and lists only here in the New Testament, 'and doeth his will, him he heareth'. The Old Testament taught something like this (Psalm 66:18; Proverbs 15:29), though readers differ over how far to press it (see the View A / View B panel). Second, a fact of history: 'Since the world began', literally *from the age*, *aiōnos* (G165, John 9:32), no one has ever heard of the eyes of one born blind being opened. The Old Testament records no such healing; the prophets promised that in the day of salvation 'the eyes of the blind shall be opened' (Isaiah 35:5). Third, the conclusion: 'If this man were not of God, he could do nothing'. That is the third step in his confession: Jesus is 'of God', *para* (G3844, John 9:33) *theou* (G2316, John 9:33), 'from God', the phrase John uses of the Baptist 'sent from God' (John 1:6) and Jesus uses of himself (John 16:27).

**Asks:** A new believer with a Bible and honest eyes can see what experts sometimes miss. The man argued from Scripture and from what he had seen, and he followed the argument where it led. Are you willing to follow the evidence about Jesus wherever it leads, even when it leads against your own side?

#### John 9:34–35 · Cast Out, and Found
**Says:** "They answered and said unto him, Thou wast altogether born in sins, and dost thou teach us? And they cast him out." (John 9:34) "Jesus heard that they had cast him out; and when he had found him, he said unto him, Dost thou believe on the Son of God?" (John 9:35) *Wast* means were.

**Means:** Having no answer to his argument, the leaders attack the man. 'Thou wast altogether born in sins', *holos* (G3650, John 9:34), 'wholly', 'entirely'. They reach for the disciples' old assumption: his blindness from birth proves he was a sinner through and through, and such a man can teach them nothing. The irony is double. They had refused to believe that he was ever blind (John 9:18), and now they use his blindness against him. And what they say of him is true of every human being, themselves included: 'Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me' (Psalm 51:5). But they thought it true of him and not of themselves.

'And they cast him out', *exebalon* (G1544, John 9:34) with *exō* (G1854, John 9:34), 'out'. They put him out of the hearing and, it seems, out of the synagogue, as John 9:22 threatened (readers differ on whether this was the formal ban, verify). The same verb and the same adverb appear on Jesus' lips three chapters earlier: 'him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out' (John 6:37). The authorities cast out; Jesus never does.

'Jesus heard that they had cast him out; and when he had found him', *heurōn* (G2147, John 9:35). Jesus goes looking. In John 5, after healing the man at Bethesda, Jesus 'findeth him in the temple' (John 5:14); here too he seeks the one he healed. And he asks the question the whole Gospel asks: 'Dost thou believe on the Son of God?', with an emphatic *su* (G4771, John 9:35), 'you', 'Do *you* believe?' The Textus Receptus behind the KJV reads 'Son of God'; many early manuscripts read 'Son of man' (the Deep Dive explains this calmly). Both are titles Jesus bears in John, and the man's response of worship fits either.

**Asks:** The man lost his place among God's people for telling the truth about Jesus, and Jesus came and found him. If you have ever been pushed out for Christ's sake, or simply left out, he knows, and he comes looking. And if you are part of a group with the power to push people out, ask whether you are closing the door on someone Christ is seeking.

#### John 9:36–38 · Lord, I Believe
**Says:** "He answered and said, Who is he, Lord, that I might believe on him?" (John 9:36) "And Jesus said unto him, Thou hast both seen him, and it is he that talketh with thee." (John 9:37) "And he said, Lord, I believe. And he worshipped him." (John 9:38)

**Means:** The man is ready to believe but does not know whom to believe in: 'Who is he, Lord, that I might believe on him?' His first 'Lord', *kurie* (G2962, John 9:36), may mean no more than 'Sir'; the word can be simply polite (John 4:11). Remember that he had never seen Jesus. He had heard his voice and felt the clay on his eyes, and then gone off to Siloam and come back to find him gone. Now Jesus answers: 'Thou hast both seen him', *heōrakas* (G3708, John 9:37), 'you have seen him', 'and it is he that talketh with thee'. The man who could not see has now seen the Son of God with the eyes the Son gave him. The words echo what Jesus said to the woman at the well: 'I that speak unto thee am he' (John 4:26).

'And he said, Lord, I believe. And he worshipped him'. Now 'Lord' means Lord. This is the fourth and final step in his confession: a man, a prophet, from God, and now Lord, the object of faith and worship. The verb is *prosekunēsen* (G4352, John 9:38), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as to make obeisance, do reverence to, worship, used of God, Christ, and supernatural beings. In John the verb is used of the worship of God (John 4:20–24; 12:20), and here it is given to Jesus. Jesus receives it without rebuke, though Peter and the angel in Revelation both refused worship offered to them (Acts 10:25–26; Revelation 22:9). This is the moment John wrote his Gospel to produce: a person who, seeing what Jesus has done, believes that he is 'the Christ, the Son of God' and has life through his name (John 20:31).

**Asks:** The man's faith ended in worship. It is possible to believe true things about Jesus and never bow before him. Where does your faith stop short of worship, and what would it look like to worship him today?

#### John 9:39–41 · Those Who Say We See
**Says:** "And Jesus said, For judgment I am come into this world, that they which see not might see; and that they which see might be made blind." (John 9:39) "And some of the Pharisees which were with him heard these words, and said unto him, Are we blind also?" (John 9:40) "Jesus said unto them, If ye were blind, ye should have no sin: but now ye say, We see; therefore your sin remaineth." (John 9:41) *Remaineth* means stays, continues.

**Means:** Jesus turns the healing into a parable of his whole mission. 'For judgment', *krima* (G2917, John 9:39), which the STEPBible lexicon defines as the issue of a judicial process, a decision, 'I am come into this world'. This does not contradict John 3:17, 'God sent not his Son into the world to condemn the world'. The purpose of his coming is salvation, but its effect is a verdict, because people respond to the light in opposite ways: "And this is the condemnation, that light is come into the world, and men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil." (John 3:19) Those who know they cannot see receive sight, as the beggar did. Those who are sure that they see are 'made blind', confirmed in the blindness they have chosen, as Isaiah was told would happen when he preached (Isaiah 6:9–10), a text John will quote of this very unbelief (John 12:40).

Some Pharisees overhear and ask, 'Are we blind also?', in a form that expects the answer no. Jesus answers with the chapter's last and hardest sentence. 'If ye were blind', if they knew they were blind and their failure to see were mere lack of light, 'ye should have no sin', no guilt for this unbelief. 'But now ye say, We see'. They claim the light, the Scriptures, and the knowledge of God. 'Therefore your sin remaineth', *menei* (G3306, John 9:41), 'stays', 'abides'. It is John's word for abiding in Christ (John 15:4), used here of sin that abides on those who will not come to him, as 'the wrath of God abideth on him' who does not believe (John 3:36). The chapter that began with a question about whose sin caused a man's blindness ends with an answer about whose sin remains. The blind man's blindness was not his sin; the Pharisees' sight was theirs.

**Asks:** The only people Jesus cannot help are those who insist they do not need help. The most dangerous words in the chapter are 'We see'. Where are you most tempted to say them, and what would it look like to say instead, 'Lord, I am blind here'?

### 🧨 Cultural Landmines and Misconceptions
**Blindness as a picture, and blind people as persons.** John 9 uses physical blindness as a picture of spiritual blindness, and so does much of the Bible. A careful teacher must not let the picture become an insult. The man in John 9 was not spiritually blind because he was physically blind; he was the one person in the chapter who saw. Blind and visually impaired believers are not less able to see God's truth, and the words 'blind' and 'darkness' should never be used in a group as a jibe at anyone's disability. The spiritually blind people in this chapter had perfect eyesight.

**Karma and reincarnation.** Many people today, influenced by Hindu and Buddhist ideas or by popular spirituality, read suffering as the payback for wrongs done in a previous life. The disciples' question can sound like that. Jesus' answer rules it out: 'Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents' (John 9:3). Scripture teaches one life and then judgment (Hebrews 9:27), not a cycle of lives, and it teaches that God's purposes in suffering are wiser than any system of payback.

**'Everything happens for a reason' as a glib comfort.** John 9:3 does teach that God had a purpose in this man's blindness. But Jesus did not say it to the man as a cheap comfort, and he did not leave the man blind while talking about purposes; he healed him. People in pain can be hurt when believers rush to tell them what God is doing in their suffering. Jesus' first response was to see the man and to do the Father's works.

**Spit and mud as magic.** Some readers are put off by the spittle, and others treat it as a formula, as if the power were in the mud or the water. John shows that the power was in Jesus. He healed others with a word (John 4:50; 5:8). The clay and the pool were means he chose, not magic.

**'The Jews' in John 9.** In this chapter 'the Jews' (John 9:18, 22) means the Jerusalem authorities who questioned the man. The man, his parents, the neighbours, Jesus, and his disciples were all Jews. The chapter is not a verdict on the Jewish people, and should never be taught as one.

**All church discipline as Pharisaism.** A modern reader may conclude from John 9 that any exclusion from a church is wrong. Scripture does teach loving church discipline for unrepentant sin (Matthew 18:15–17; 1 Corinthians 5:1–5), aimed at restoration. What John 9 condemns is casting someone out for confessing Christ, for telling the truth, or for being inconvenient to the powerful. The question is always what someone is being put out for, and whether the aim is to restore or to be rid of them.

## 🔴 Tier 3 — Expert
### 🏗️ Theological Framework
John 9 is a narrative, not a treatise, but read through the Reformed conviction that Scripture interprets Scripture it bears on six areas of systematic theology.

*Providence and suffering.* 'But that the works of God should be made manifest in him' (John 9:3) places the man's congenital blindness inside God's purpose. Reformed theology teaches that God upholds and governs all creatures and all their actions and circumstances by his most wise and holy providence, including the painful ones, without being the author of sin (Westminster Confession, chapter 5, paraphrased, verify); the Heidelberg Catechism says in substance that health and sickness come to us not by chance but from God's fatherly hand (Question 27, paraphrased, verify). Its biblical roots are texts like Exodus 4:11, where the LORD claims to make the seeing and the blind, and Genesis 50:20. John 9 adds two guards. First, providence is not a key for reading particular sufferings as particular punishments; Jesus refuses exactly that inference (John 9:3). Second, providence is not fatalism: the purpose God had in the blindness was fulfilled in Jesus' healing, not in anyone's resignation to it.

*Christ the light: revelation and illumination.* 'I am the light of the world' (John 9:5) gathers up John's doctrine of revelation: God is known only in the Son (John 1:18; 14:9). John 9 shows the other side of revelation, illumination: the light may shine, and people may still not see, unless their eyes are opened. Paul draws the same line: the god of this world blinds the minds of unbelievers, and God shines in the heart to give the knowledge of his glory in Christ (2 Corinthians 4:4–6). Reformed theology teaches that fallen people cannot, by their own power, see the glory of Christ (1 Corinthians 2:14), and that the Spirit effectually enlightens the mind in regeneration (Canons of Dort, heads III/IV, articles 11–12, paraphrased, verify). The healing of a man 'born blind', whom 'Since the world began' no one had healed (John 9:32), is a sign of exactly that work.

*Grace that goes first.* The order of the chapter is instructive. Jesus sees the man before the man asks (John 9:1). He heals him before the man knows who he is (John 9:12, 36). He finds him before the man looks for him (John 9:35). And only then does the man believe and worship (John 9:38). A narrative sign does not by itself settle the order of salvation, and the Reformed doctrine that regeneration precedes and produces faith rests on other texts (John 1:12–13; 3:3–8; 6:44). But John 9 pictures it vividly: the opened eyes come first, and the believing sight follows.

*Culpability, knowledge, and judicial blindness.* 'If ye were blind, ye should have no sin: but now ye say, We see; therefore your sin remaineth' (John 9:41). Scripture teaches degrees of guilt according to light received (Luke 12:47–48; John 15:22), while holding that no one is wholly without light and so without excuse (Romans 1:20). The Pharisees' guilt is increased, not diminished, by their knowledge, because they use it to refuse the light that stands in front of them (John 3:19). And John 9:39 speaks of a judgment by which those who say they see are 'made blind', the judicial hardening announced in Isaiah 6:9–10 and applied by John to this unbelief (John 12:39–40). Reformed theology treats such hardening as God's just giving over of people to the blindness they have chosen (Romans 1:24, 28), not as God's creating unbelief in innocent hearts.

*The true flock and the true people of God.* The man is 'put out of the synagogue' (John 9:22, 34) and found by Jesus (John 9:35), and the next chapter describes the shepherd who 'leadeth them out' of the fold (John 10:3) and has other sheep to bring (John 10:16). Reformed ecclesiology distinguishes the true church, marked by the pure preaching of the gospel, from any institution that claims the name while excluding Christ's people for confessing him (Belgic Confession, article 29, paraphrased, verify). John 9 is not an argument against the visible church, which Christ himself gathers, but a warning that the visible community can cast out those whom Christ receives.

*The worship of the Son.* 'And he worshipped him' (John 9:38). Scripture reserves worship for God alone (Matthew 4:10), and yet the Father commands 'let all the angels of God worship him' (Hebrews 1:6), and wills that 'all men should honour the Son, even as they honour the Father' (John 5:23). Jesus receives the man's worship without correction. The Nicene faith, that the Son is true God of true God and of one substance with the Father (summarized, not quoted), is the church's account of why that worship is right.

### 📚 Scholarly Insights
- **Irenaeus**, *Against Heresies* (book 5, chapter 15, verify): reads the clay of John 9:6 as the Creator's own hand doing again what it did when it formed Adam from the dust, and argues from it that the God of creation and the Father of Jesus are one God (verify the particulars).
- **Augustine**, *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (Tractate 44, verify): reads the man born blind as a picture of the whole human race, blind from birth through sin; sees in the spittle mixed with earth the Word made flesh; notes that Siloam means Sent and so points to Christ; and compares the anointed but unwashed man to a catechumen not yet baptized (verify each).
- **John Chrysostom**, *Homilies on the Gospel of John* (Homilies 56–59, verify): praises the man's boldness and frankness before the rulers, sets it against the parents' cowardice, and argues that the sabbath controversy exposes the leaders' envy rather than their zeal for the law (verify).
- **John Calvin**, *Commentary on the Gospel According to John*: on John 9:3 teaches that Christ does not deny that afflictions in general come from sin, but forbids us to judge a particular sufferer, since God may have other ends in view, such as displaying his glory; on John 9:6 insists that the power was not in the clay; and on John 9:34 warns that the power of excommunication can be abused by ungodly rulers against the godly (verify each).
- **J. C. Ryle**, *Expository Thoughts on the Gospels* (John): draws from John 9:1–5 the lessons that sickness is not always the result of particular sin and that we must work while it is day; and from John 9:34–38 the comfort that Christ receives those whom men cast out for his sake (verify).
- **B. F. Westcott**, *The Gospel According to St. John* (1880): traces the man's growing faith through the chapter and discusses the reading 'Son of God' against 'Son of man' at John 9:35 (verify).
- **J. Louis Martyn**, *History and Theology in the Fourth Gospel* (1968): proposes that John 9 is written on two levels, the story of Jesus and a blind man, and the experience of John's own church late in the first century, when, Martyn argues, synagogues formally excluded Christian Jews through a benediction against heretics (verify). His thesis has been very influential and much criticized.
- **D. A. Carson**, *The Gospel According to John* (Pillar New Testament Commentary, 1991): argues that John 9:22 does not need the late benediction to be explained, since local exclusion from a synagogue was possible in Jesus' lifetime, and reads the man's progress as a model of growing faith (verify).
- **Jonathan Bernier**, *Aposynagōgos and the Historical Jesus in John* (2013): argues that the expulsions described in John are historically plausible in the time of Jesus (verify).
- **Raymond E. Brown**, *The Gospel According to John* (Anchor Bible, 1966): notes the use of John 9 in early Christian baptismal instruction, and discusses the omission of John 9:38–39a from a few early witnesses (verify).
- **Leon Morris**, *The Gospel According to John* (New International Commentary, 1971): stresses that the healing is a sign of Christ as the light, and that the Pharisees' sin lay in claiming sight while refusing it (verify).

### ⚔️ Debate Corner
**The question:** What does Jesus mean when he answers the disciples, "Jesus answered, Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him." (John 9:3)?

**Position 1 · The blindness was a punishment after all, for sin before birth or in a former life.** Some Jewish teachers of the period discussed whether a child could sin in the womb, pointing to Jacob and Esau struggling before birth (Genesis 25:22, verify the rabbinic use). Some early Christian thinkers influenced by the idea of the soul's pre-existence, and many in modern popular spirituality who believe in reincarnation or karma, read the disciples' question as reasonable and the man's blindness as the fruit of earlier wrongs. Holders of this view want to protect the justice of God: if God is just, they reason, no one suffers without cause.

**Position 2 · God ordained the blindness for his glory and the man's good.** Augustine, Calvin, and the Reformed tradition (verify each on this verse) read the verse as the KJV punctuates it: Jesus denies that the blindness was a punishment for particular sin, and affirms that God had a purpose in it, 'that the works of God should be made manifest in him'. God did not merely permit a random misfortune and then make the best of it; he governed this man's life from birth toward the day of his healing and faith. Holders of this view want to protect God's sovereignty and goodness together: nothing is outside his rule, and his purposes are good even when hidden.

**Position 3 · Jesus gives no reason for the blindness at all, only a response to it.** Some modern interpreters, including some who hold that God does not ordain evil (verify particular authors, for example among open theists), take the purpose clause in John 9:3 either as a command, *but let the works of God be displayed*, or as belonging with John 9:4: *Neither this man nor his parents sinned. But so that the works of God may be displayed in him, I must work the works of him that sent me.* On this view Jesus refuses both to blame the victim and to explain the evil; he simply acts against it. Holders of this view want to protect God from being the author of suffering, and to keep the sufferer from being told that God blinded him for a display.

**Most consistent with Reformed hermeneutics.** Scripture interpreting Scripture favours Position 2, held with the pastoral care that Position 3 rightly demands. (1) *Against Position 1*, Jesus' denial is flat: 'Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents'. Scripture knows nothing of sin in a former life; 'it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment' (Hebrews 9:27). (2) *The grammar and the parallel.* The KJV, with most translations, reads *hina* (G2443, John 9:3) as purpose attached to what precedes, and John uses the same shape at Lazarus's sickness: 'This sickness is not unto death, but for the glory of God' (John 11:4). The repunctuation of Position 3 is possible in Greek, which had no fixed punctuation, but it is not required, and the parallel of John 11:4 tells against it. (3) *The wider witness of Scripture.* The LORD claims to make 'the seeing, or the blind' (Exodus 4:11); what Joseph's brothers meant for evil 'God meant it unto good' (Genesis 50:20); and God works all things together for good to those who love him (Romans 8:28). God's purpose in suffering is not an embarrassment the Bible avoids but a comfort it offers. (4) *What the purpose was.* The 'works of God' displayed in the man were his healing, his faith, and his worship of the Son (John 9:7, 38). The purpose was not a cruel display but the man's own salvation and Christ's glory, which are never at odds.

Position 3 is right that Jesus' first response was action, not explanation, and that the verse must never be used to tell a sufferer glibly that God did this to him for a show. Position 1 is right that God is just. But the justice of God is not served by inventing sins to explain suffering; it is served by trusting that 'the Judge of all the earth' does right (Genesis 18:25) when we cannot see how.

**Pastoral Warning:** This debate reaches every church, because every church has members who were born with disabilities, have children with them, or live with chronic illness. A church can fail in three directions. It can quietly hold Position 1, and treat suffering as a sign of hidden sin or weak faith, so that the sick are shamed as well as ill. It can use Position 2 carelessly, and hand people in pain a sentence about God's glory before it has sat with them, prayed with them, or helped them, which is the disciples' error in a Reformed accent. Or it can drift into Position 3 and lose the comfort that nothing in a believer's life, not even this, is outside the Father's hand. Teach John 9:3 as Jesus used it: to stop the blame, to turn everyone's eyes to what God is doing, and to get to work. And when the suffering is not taken away in this life, point the sufferer to the one who opened the blind man's eyes, whose works in them are not yet finished (Philippians 1:6).

### 🔤 Original Language Deep Dive
Every Greek and Hebrew definition below comes from one source: the brief lexicon published by STEPBible.org (Tyndale House, Cambridge), whose Greek entries are drawn largely from G. Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament*. It is called 'the STEPBible lexicon' below.

**Blind, and blind from birth.** *Tuphlon* (G5185, John 9:1, A-ASM), from *tuphlos*, 'blind'. The STEPBible lexicon notes that in the Greek Old Testament it renders the Hebrew for 'blind', and lists metaphorical uses of spiritual blindness, among them John 9:39–41, Matthew 15:14, Romans 2:19, 2 Peter 1:9, and Revelation 3:17. The word 'blind' occurs 15 times in the KJV of John 9 and 18 times in the whole of John; the other three are John 5:3, where blind people lie at Bethesda, and John 10:21 and 11:37, which both look back to this healing. The chapter is saturated with the word, and it moves from the literal (John 9:1) to the figurative (John 9:39–41). *Genetēs* (G1079, John 9:1, N-GSF), 'birth', is listed by the lexicon only here in the New Testament, in the phrase 'from birth'.

**Born.** The verb *gennaō* runs through the chapter: *gennēthē* (G1080, John 9:2, V-APS-3S), *that he should be born*; *egennēthē* (G1080, John 9:20, V-API-3S), 'he was born'; *gegennēmenou* (G1080, John 9:32, V-RPP-GSM), a perfect passive participle, 'of one who had been born' blind; and *egennēthēs* (G1080, John 9:34, V-API-2S), 'thou wast born' in sins. It is the same verb Jesus used with Nicodemus: 'Except a man be born again' (John 3:3). The man's first birth left him blind; the chapter shows a man given sight by the one who gives the second birth.

**Made manifest.** *Phanerōthē* (G5319, John 9:3, V-APS-3S), an aorist passive subjunctive after *hina* (G2443, John 9:3), *that it might be made manifest*. John uses the verb for the revealing of Christ (John 1:31), of his glory in the first sign (John 2:11), and of the works that show whether a person's deeds are 'wrought in God' (John 3:21). The subjunctive after *hina* normally expresses purpose, which is how the KJV takes it; the Debate Corner discusses the alternative.

**I must work.** *Dei* (G1163, John 9:4, V-PAI-3S), 'it is necessary', and *ergazesthai* (G2038, John 9:4, V-PNN), 'to work', echoing 'My Father worketh hitherto, and I work' (John 5:17). *Nux* (G3571, John 9:4, N-NSF), 'night', and *hēmera* (G2250, John 9:4, N-NSF), 'day'. In John, night is often more than a time of day (John 3:2; 13:30).

**Clay and anointing.** *Pēlon* (G4081, John 9:6, N-ASM): the STEPBible lexicon gives two senses, clay as used by a potter (Romans 9:21, with Isaiah 29:16) and wet clay, mud, under which it lists John 9:6, 11, 14, and 15. The lexicon does not connect John 9 with Genesis 2:7; that connection is a theological reading (Irenaeus), not a lexical one. *Epechrisen* (G2025, John 9:6, V-AAI-3S), 'spread on, anointed', which the lexicon lists only in John 9:6 and 11 in the New Testament. Here it means simply to spread or smear something on, and no theology of anointing should be built on the English word 'anointed' in this verse.

**Sent.** *Apestalmenos* (G649, John 9:7, V-RPP-NSM), a perfect passive participle of *apostellō*, 'having been sent'. The STEPBible lexicon defines the verb as to send with a commission or on service, notes that in the Greek Old Testament it almost always renders the Hebrew *shalach* (H7971), 'to send', and lists its use of Christ being sent (Matthew 10:40) and of God sending his Son into the world (John 3:17). The verb occurs 28 times in the Greek of John, and the English 'sent' 57 times in the KJV of John, since John also uses the verb *pempō*, as in *pempsantos* (G3992, John 9:4, V-AAP-GSM), 'of him that sent me'. The name Siloam, *Silōam* (G4611, John 9:7, N-GSM-L), represents the Hebrew *shiloach* (H7975, Isaiah 8:6), which the lexicon explains as meaning 'sent', a fountain just south-east of Jerusalem. The name 'Siloam' occurs 3 times in the KJV (Luke 13:4; John 9:7, 11), and Nehemiah 3:15 has the form 'Siloah'. John's comment, *hermēneuetai* (G2059, John 9:7, V-PPI-3S), 'is interpreted', is one of several places where he translates a Semitic name for his readers (John 1:38, 41–42).

**Wash, and see.** *Nipsai* (G3538, John 9:7, V-AMM-2S), an aorist middle imperative, 'wash yourself'; the STEPBible lexicon lists John 9:7, 11, and 15 under the reflexive middle sense. It is the verb of the foot-washing in John 13:5–14, not the verb for a full bath or for baptism. *Blepōn* (G991, John 9:7, V-PAP-NSM), 'seeing', which the lexicon lists at John 9:7 under bodily sight as the opposite of blindness; and at John 9:39 the same verb is used of mental and spiritual perception: *blepontes* (G991, John 9:39, V-PAP-NPM), 'they which see', and *blepōsin* (G991, John 9:39, V-PAS-3P), 'might see'. The lexicon lists both a bodily and a metaphorical sense, and John plays on them. *Aneblepsa* (G308, John 9:11, V-AAI-1S), 'I received sight': the lexicon gives the senses 'to look up' and 'to recover sight', listing John 9:11 under the second. For a man born blind, 'receive sight' is the right English.

**Beggar.** *Prosaitōn* (G4319, John 9:8, V-PAP-NSM), 'begging', which the STEPBible lexicon defines as to continue asking, importune, beg alms, and lists only at John 9:8.

**Signs and division.** *Sēmeia* (G4592, John 9:16, N-APN), 'signs', which the lexicon defines as a sign, mark, or token, and under its fourth sense, miracles 'regarded as signs of a divine authority'. *Schisma* (G4978, John 9:16, N-NSN), 'a rent', and metaphorically 'a dissension, division'. John's three uses all describe the crowd or the leaders divided over Jesus (John 7:43; 9:16; 10:19).

**Confession and expulsion.** *Homologēsē* (G3670, John 9:22, V-AAS-3S), 'should confess'. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 9:22 with Romans 10:9 and 1 John 4:2 and 4:15 under the construction in which a person is confessed *as* something: to confess him as Christ, as Lord, as come in the flesh. *Aposunagōgos* (G656, John 9:22, A-NSM), 'expelled from the congregation, excommunicated', found only in John (John 9:22; 12:42; 16:2). *Sunetetheinto* (G4934, John 9:22, V-LMI-3P), a pluperfect middle, 'they had agreed together': the decision was taken before this hearing. *Exebalon* (G1544, John 9:34, V-2AAI-3P), 'they cast out', with *exō* (G1854, John 9:34, PREP), 'outside'. The lexicon defines the verb as to drive or cast out, to expel, and lists John 6:37, where it also stands with the word for 'out'; it also lists the verb for expulsion from the church in 3 John 1:10.

**Sinner and godly.** *Hamartōlos* (G268, John 9:24, A-NSM), 'sinful, a sinner'. It occurs 4 times in the Greek of John, all in this chapter (John 9:16, 24, 25, 31), and 46 times in the Greek New Testament. *Theosebēs* (G2318, John 9:31, A-NSM), 'God-fearing, godly', which the lexicon lists only here in the New Testament and notes renders the Hebrew for 'fearing God' in the Greek Old Testament (Exodus 18:21). The man's phrase *if any man be God-fearing* is the Old Testament's description of the righteous, applied to Jesus.

**Reviled.** *Eloidorēsan* (G3058, John 9:28, V-AAI-3P), 'they abused, reviled'. The lexicon lists its New Testament uses, including 1 Peter 2:23, where Christ, reviled, did not revile in return.

**Believe, and worship.** The chapter's climax is built on the verb *pisteuō* in three forms. *Pisteueis* (G4100, John 9:35, V-PAI-2S), 'dost thou believe', a present; *pisteusō* (G4100, John 9:36, V-AAS-1S), 'that I might believe', an aorist subjunctive of purpose; and *pisteuō* (G4100, John 9:38, V-PAI-1S), 'I believe'. The question, the readiness, and the confession. Then *prosekunēsen* (G4352, John 9:38, V-AAI-3S), 'he worshipped'. The STEPBible lexicon derives the verb from the verb 'to kiss', defines it as to make obeisance, do reverence to, worship, and lists its use of God, Christ, and supernatural beings. It occurs 10 times in the Greek of John: nine times in John 4:20–24 and John 12:20, of the worship of God, and once here, of the worship of Jesus. The title 'Son of God' occurs 10 times in the KJV of John, and John 20:31 names belief in 'the Christ, the Son of God' as the goal of the book.

**Judgment and remaining.** *Krima* (G2917, John 9:39, N-ASN), which the lexicon defines as the issue of a judicial process, a judgment or decision, distinct from *krisis*, the process of judging. Jesus' coming brings a verdict into the world. *Menei* (G3306, John 9:41, V-PAI-3S), 'remains', 'abides', the verb of John 15:4 and of John 3:36. Sin that remains is the dark counterpart of the believer who abides in Christ.

**Textual notes.** These should be explained calmly, without scorn for either side; none of them changes any doctrine. (1) *John 9:4.* The Textus Receptus behind the KJV reads 'I must work'; many early manuscripts, among them P66, P75, Codex Sinaiticus, and Codex Vaticanus, read *We must work the works of him that sent me*, and most modern editions follow them (verify the witnesses). Both readings keep the Father as the Sender. (2) *John 9:8.* The Textus Receptus reads that the neighbours had seen him 'that he was blind'; many early manuscripts read 'that he was a beggar' (verify). (3) *John 9:35.* The Textus Receptus reads 'Son of God'; P66, P75, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, and others read 'Son of man', and most modern editions print that (verify). Defenders of the Textus Receptus reading note its wide support in later manuscripts and versions and the fitness of the confession that follows; those who prefer 'Son of man' argue that a scribe would more easily change the unusual title to the familiar confession than the reverse. Both titles belong to Jesus in John (John 1:49, 51), and the man's worship shows that he understood the one he believed in to be more than a man. (4) *John 9:38–39a.* The words 'And he said, Lord, I believe. And he worshipped him. And Jesus said' are absent from a few early witnesses, including P75 and the first hand of Codex Sinaiticus (verify), and some scholars have suggested they were added from early baptismal liturgy (verify). They are present in the great majority of manuscripts, and the KJV prints them.

### 🚷 Preaching Mistakes to Avoid
- **Information without life:** a sermon that spends its time on the location of the pool, the sabbath regulations, the Martyn hypothesis, and the variant at John 9:35, and never brings the hearers to the place where Jesus asks, 'Dost thou believe on the Son of God?' (John 9:35).
- **Heat without light:** a sermon that thunders against 'the Pharisees' and the blind leaders out there, and never lets 'Are we blind also?' (John 9:40) fall on the preacher and the congregation.
- **Blaming the sufferer:** any application that suggests to the sick, the disabled, or their families that their suffering proves hidden sin or weak faith, when Jesus explicitly rejected that inference (John 9:3).
- **A healing promise the text does not make:** preaching John 9 as a guarantee that God will heal every believer's body now if they have enough faith; the man had not yet believed when he was healed, and many faithful believers wait for resurrection.
- **Allegory run wild:** assigning a hidden meaning to every detail (the spittle, the clay, the number of steps to Siloam) beyond what John himself signals. John interprets one detail for us, the name Sent (John 9:7); let that be the lead.
- **The man as the hero:** making the sermon about the healed man's courage, as a model of standing up to authority, instead of about the Christ who opened his eyes, gave him courage, and found him when he was cast out.
- **Using blindness as an insult:** calling opponents 'blind' in a way that mocks the disabled or contradicts the chapter, where the only man who saw had been born blind.

## 🟣 Tier 4 — Christocentric, Humility, Life & Light
### 👑 Every Answer in Jesus
John 9 demolishes every ground of self-glory. Religious knowledge is no ground: the men who said 'we know' were the ones who could not see (John 9:24, 29, 41). Loyalty to Moses is no ground: they set Moses against the one of whom Moses wrote (John 9:28; 5:46). Theological cleverness is no ground: the disciples' question about blame led nowhere, and Jesus' answer led to a healing (John 9:2–3). Family and belonging are no ground: the parents kept their place and lost their witness (John 9:22). Even the healed man's courage is no ground for boasting: he did not open his own eyes, he did not find his own way to Jesus, and he did not know whom to believe in until Jesus told him (John 9:12, 36). And the chapter leaves no ground for despair either: a man with nothing, blind from birth, a beggar, and finally an outcast, was the one who saw the Son of God and worshipped him.

And every good thing the chapter holds is found in him. Light is in him: 'I am the light of the world' (John 9:5). The works of God are his to do (John 9:3–4). He is the Sent One to whom the pool's name points (John 9:7). He is 'of God', as the beggar reasoned (John 9:33). He seeks and finds the cast out (John 9:35). He is the Son of God, worthy of faith and worship (John 9:35–38). And he is the judge, whose coming opens the eyes of the humble and confirms the blindness of the proud (John 9:39–41). This is what Paul means when he says of Christ, "In whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." (Colossians 2:3) The Pharisees thought the treasures of knowledge were theirs; the beggar found them in a person. The chapter humbles us by showing that we were born blind and could not have seen him unless he opened our eyes. And it makes Christ glorious by showing the light of the world stooping to the dust, making clay with his own spittle, and going out into the street to find a man whom the religious court had thrown away.

## 📚 Doctrine
### Theology Proper · The Sovereign and Good Purpose of God in Suffering
**Proposition:** God rules over every circumstance of human life, including disability and suffering from birth, and he works in them for purposes that are wise and good: the display of his works, the salvation of his people, and the glory of his Son. A particular suffering is not to be read as a particular punishment, and God's purposes are served by relieving suffering as well as by sustaining people through it.

**Textual Anchor:** "Jesus answered, Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him." (John 9:3), with *phanerōthē* (G5319, John 9:3, V-APS-3S) after *hina* (G2443, John 9:3); 'I must work the works of him that sent me' (John 9:4).

**Cross-References:** "And the LORD said unto him, Who hath made man's mouth? or who maketh the dumb, or deaf, or the seeing, or the blind? have not I the LORD?" (Exodus 4:11); "But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive." (Genesis 50:20); "And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose." (Romans 8:28)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Every suffering is the payback for a particular sin, in this life or a former one.* Holders of karma and reincarnation teach this, and so, in a different form, does the popular belief that the sick must have done something to deserve it; they want the world to be morally tidy. Jesus denies it of this man in so many words (John 9:3), and Scripture teaches one life and then judgment (Hebrews 9:27). (2) *God does not ordain or govern suffering; he only responds to it.* Open theists and others hold this, wanting to keep God free of any link to evil. But the LORD claims to make 'the seeing, or the blind' (Exodus 4:11), and Jesus speaks of a purpose in this blindness. (3) *Suffering is meaningless chance.* Secular materialism holds this, wanting to face the world without illusion. John 9 shows a life, blind from birth, that was not meaningless, because God was at work in it from the beginning.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Humility before a God whose purposes are larger than our explanations. Patience in suffering, because it is held in the Father's hand. And compassion toward sufferers, because we are forbidden to blame them and called instead to do the works of God among them.

### Christology · The Light of the World, the Sent One
**Proposition:** Jesus Christ is the light of the world, the only source of the saving knowledge of God, sent by the Father to do his works while it is day. He does what no one had ever done, opening the eyes of one born blind, and so shows himself to be the one of whom the prophets spoke, who would open blind eyes in the day of salvation.

**Textual Anchor:** "As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world." (John 9:5) 'I must work the works of him that sent me' (John 9:4), with *pempsantos* (G3992, John 9:4, V-AAP-GSM); Siloam, 'which is by interpretation, Sent' (John 9:7), with *apestalmenos* (G649, John 9:7, V-RPP-NSM); and the man's reasoning, 'Since the world began was it not heard that any man opened the eyes of one that was born blind' (John 9:32).

**Cross-References:** "I the LORD have called thee in righteousness, and will hold thine hand, and will keep thee, and give thee for a covenant of the people, for a light of the Gentiles;" (Isaiah 42:6); "I am come a light into the world, that whosoever believeth on me should not abide in darkness." (John 12:46)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus was one enlightened teacher among many, one light among the world's lights.* Religious pluralists hold this, wanting to honour every sincere seeker. But Jesus does not say he is a light; he says he is 'the light of the world' (John 9:5), and he does what 'Since the world began' no one had done (John 9:32). (2) *Jesus' healings were legends, or natural cures dressed up as miracles.* Some critical scholars since the Enlightenment hold this, wanting a faith that does not depend on miracles. John tells the story with the facts tested at every step: neighbours, parents, and two hearings all confirm that the man was born blind and now sees (John 9:8–9, 18–20). (3) *Jesus was only a prophet.* Muslims and some others hold this, wanting to honour Jesus without calling him divine; it is exactly the man's second confession (John 9:17), and the chapter moves beyond it to faith in the Son of God and worship (John 9:35–38).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Hope, because the one who opened the eyes of one born blind can open any eyes. Direction, because the light is a person to follow, not a technique to master. And urgency, because the day is short and the night comes (John 9:4).

### Christology · The Son of God, Believed and Worshipped
**Proposition:** Jesus is the Son of God, who reveals himself to those he seeks and calls them to believe on him. He rightly receives the faith and worship due to God alone, and he received them from the healed man without correction, as he receives them from all who truly see him.

**Textual Anchor:** "Jesus heard that they had cast him out; and when he had found him, he said unto him, Dost thou believe on the Son of God?" (John 9:35), with *pisteueis* (G4100, John 9:35, V-PAI-2S); 'Thou hast both seen him, and it is he that talketh with thee' (John 9:37); "And he said, Lord, I believe. And he worshipped him." (John 9:38), with *prosekunēsen* (G4352, John 9:38, V-AAI-3S).

**Cross-References:** "That all men should honour the Son, even as they honour the Father. He that honoureth not the Son honoureth not the Father which hath sent him." (John 5:23); "And again, when he bringeth in the firstbegotten into the world, he saith, And let all the angels of God worship him." (Hebrews 1:6); "And Thomas answered and said unto him, My Lord and my God." (John 20:28)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The man only bowed to Jesus as a mark of respect, not worship.* Jehovah's Witnesses, who hold that Christ is a created being, argue that the verb can mean simple obeisance, and it can (the lexicon allows the sense of doing reverence). But in John the verb is otherwise used only of the worship of God (John 4:20–24; 12:20), and Jesus, unlike Peter and the angel, did not refuse it (Acts 10:25–26; Revelation 22:9). (2) *'Son of God' means only a righteous man or Israel's king.* Some interpreters hold this, reading the title against its use for Israel's kings. In John it means the one who shares the Father's life and honour (John 5:18, 23, 26), and the book is written that we may believe that Jesus is 'the Christ, the Son of God' (John 20:31).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Worship that is not embarrassed: the right response to Jesus is to bow. Assurance for the seeker, because Jesus reveals himself to those who ask 'Who is he, Lord' (John 9:36). And confidence in witness, because the one we confess is worthy of all honour.

### Anthropology & Sin · Born Blind, and the Sin That Remains
**Proposition:** Every human being is born in sin and, apart from God's grace, is spiritually blind, unable to see the glory of God in Christ. This condition is not proven by any particular physical suffering. The deepest form of this blindness is the proud claim to see, which refuses the light that is offered, and so makes the unbeliever's guilt remain.

**Textual Anchor:** 'Thou wast altogether born in sins' (John 9:34), spoken in contempt, but true of every human being; "Jesus said unto them, If ye were blind, ye should have no sin: but now ye say, We see; therefore your sin remaineth." (John 9:41), with *menei* (G3306, John 9:41, V-PAI-3S); and the leaders' repeated 'we know' (John 9:24, 29).

**Cross-References:** "Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me." (Psalm 51:5); "In whom the god of this world hath blinded the minds of them which believe not, lest the light of the glorious gospel of Christ, who is the image of God, should shine unto them." (2 Corinthians 4:4); "Woe unto them that are wise in their own eyes, and prudent in their own sight!" (Isaiah 5:21)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *People are born morally neutral and able to see God clearly if they choose.* Pelagians in the fifth century and many modern people hold this, wanting to affirm human responsibility and freedom. But Scripture says we are shaped in iniquity from conception (Psalm 51:5) and that minds are blinded (2 Corinthians 4:4); the man born blind is the Gospel's picture of the human condition. (2) *Sincere religious people cannot be spiritually blind.* Many hold this, wanting to honour devotion wherever it is found. The Pharisees were sincere and learned, and Jesus says their sin remains, precisely because they claimed to see (John 9:41). (3) *Ignorance always excuses.* Some hold this, wanting a gentle view of the unbeliever. Jesus says the claim to sight removes the excuse (John 9:41), and Paul says that all have enough light to be without excuse (Romans 1:20).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Humility, because the blindness described is ours by nature. Gentleness toward the blind, since we were once there. And watchfulness, because the most dangerous words a believer can say are 'We see' about anything he will not let God correct.

### Soteriology & Grace · The Grace That Sees, Sends, and Seeks
**Proposition:** Salvation begins with Christ's initiative, not ours. He sees the blind before they call, opens eyes that cannot open themselves, and seeks out those whom others cast away, and he brings them to faith in himself. The faith that follows is real and personal, but it is the fruit of his prior work, not its cause.

**Textual Anchor:** 'And as Jesus passed by, he saw a man' (John 9:1); 'He went his way therefore, and washed, and came seeing' (John 9:7); 'when he had found him' (John 9:35), with *heurōn* (G2147, John 9:35); and the man's own confession of what he did not do: 'one thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see' (John 9:25).

**Cross-References:** "For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost." (Luke 19:10); "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." (2 Corinthians 4:6); "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God:" (Ephesians 2:8)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The man was healed because of his faith.* Some teachers of faith healing hold this, wanting to stir believers to expectant faith. But he did not know who Jesus was until after his healing (John 9:12, 36); his obedience at Siloam was real, but the initiative and the power were Christ's. (2) *People find their own way to God, and God responds.* Semi-Pelagians and many popular preachers hold this, wanting to stress human response. In John 9 the man never finds Jesus; Jesus finds him, twice (John 9:1, 35). (3) *Salvation is a gradual self-improvement.* Some hold this, reading the man's growing confession as his own achievement. His growth is real, but it is growth in seeing what Christ has already done; its beginning was an act of Christ alone.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Assurance, because the one who began the work is the one who keeps seeking. Gratitude, because our sight is his gift. And evangelistic hope, because no one is too blind for the one who opened the eyes of a man born blind.

### Ecclesiology · Cast Out by Men, Gathered by Christ
**Proposition:** The true people of God are those whom Christ gathers to himself, not simply those whom an institution admits. Religious communities can cast out those whom Christ receives, and when they do, Christ seeks them out and makes them part of his own flock. Christians must be ready to bear exclusion for confessing Christ, and churches must take care never to exclude anyone for that reason.

**Textual Anchor:** 'if any man did confess that he was Christ, he should be put out of the synagogue' (John 9:22), with *aposunagōgos* (G656, John 9:22, A-NSM); 'And they cast him out' (John 9:34), with *exebalon* (G1544, John 9:34, V-2AAI-3P); and Jesus' finding of him (John 9:35), followed at once by the shepherd who 'calleth his own sheep by name, and leadeth them out' (John 10:3).

**Cross-References:** "And other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring, and they shall hear my voice; and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd." (John 10:16); "Let us go forth therefore unto him without the camp, bearing his reproach." (Hebrews 13:13); "Blessed are ye, when men shall hate you, and when they shall separate you from their company, and shall reproach you, and cast out your name as evil, for the Son of man's sake." (Luke 6:22)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Membership in a religious body guarantees membership in God's people.* Some forms of institutionalism hold this, wanting to honour the visible church Christ founded. The synagogue in John 9 was the visible assembly of God's people, and it cast out a man whom Christ received. (2) *Since institutions can err, Christians need no church.* Many individualists hold this, wanting to avoid the abuses John 9 describes. But the one who found the man is the shepherd who gathers a flock, 'one fold, and one shepherd' (John 10:16); Christ gathers people into his church, he does not scatter them into isolation. (3) *Any exclusion from a church is Pharisaism.* Some hold this, wanting a church without walls. Scripture commands loving discipline for unrepentant sin (Matthew 18:15–17); what John 9 condemns is exclusion for confessing Christ.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Comfort for those who have been pushed out for their faith. Self-examination for church leaders, who hold the power the synagogue leaders abused. And a welcome for outsiders, since the church is the flock of the shepherd who seeks the cast out.

### Eschatology · The Day, the Night, and Judgment Come into the World
**Proposition:** Christ's coming brings God's final judgment into the present: those who confess their blindness and come to him receive sight, and those who claim to see and refuse him are confirmed in blindness. The day of his work is limited and the night is coming, so the response to the light cannot be postponed.

**Textual Anchor:** "And Jesus said, For judgment I am come into this world, that they which see not might see; and that they which see might be made blind." (John 9:39), with *krima* (G2917, John 9:39, N-ASN); 'the night cometh, when no man can work' (John 9:4), with *nux* (G3571, John 9:4, N-NSF).

**Cross-References:** "And this is the condemnation, that light is come into the world, and men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil." (John 3:19); "Then Jesus said unto them, Yet a little while is the light with you. Walk while ye have the light, lest darkness come upon you: for he that walketh in darkness knoweth not whither he goeth." (John 12:35); "He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life: and he that believeth not the Son shall not see life; but the wrath of God abideth on him." (John 3:36)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Judgment is only a future event, with no bearing on the present.* Some hold this, wanting to keep the gospel invitation free of threat. John teaches that the verdict is already being given as people respond to the light (John 3:18–19; 9:39), though a final judgment is still to come (John 5:28–29). (2) *Everyone will see in the end, whatever they do now.* Universalists hold this, wanting to honour the breadth of God's love. Jesus speaks of sin that remains (John 9:41) and of a night when no one can work (John 9:4). (3) *There is always time later.* Many hold this in practice, wanting to keep their options open. Jesus says the light is 'Yet a little while' (John 12:35).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Urgency in coming to Christ and in bringing others. Sobriety about the danger of hardening. And hope, because the same judgment that blinds the proud opens the eyes of the humble.

### Christian Walk · A Witness That Grows Under Pressure
**Proposition:** A disciple bears witness to what Christ has done, honestly distinguishing what he knows from what he does not, and he holds to that witness under pressure, even at the cost of his place. Such witness grows as it is tested, and it is given by Christ, who stands with his witnesses and finds them when they are cast out.

**Textual Anchor:** "He answered and said, Whether he be a sinner or no, I know not: one thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see." (John 9:25) 'will ye also be his disciples' (John 9:27); 'If this man were not of God, he could do nothing' (John 9:33); set against the parents' silence, 'because they feared the Jews' (John 9:22).

**Cross-References:** "For we cannot but speak the things which we have seen and heard." (Acts 4:20); "Whosoever therefore shall confess me before men, him will I confess also before my Father which is in heaven." (Matthew 10:32); "But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts: and be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear:" (1 Peter 3:15)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Only trained people can witness.* Many believers assume this, wanting to protect the gospel from being misrepresented. The healed man had no training; he had one thing he knew, and he told it. (2) *Faith is private and should be kept to oneself.* Much modern culture holds this, wanting to keep peace in a diverse society. The parents' silence is presented as fear, not wisdom (John 9:22), and Jesus calls his people to confess him before men (Matthew 10:32). (3) *Witness means winning arguments.* Some apologetic styles drift toward this, wanting to meet objections with reasons. The man did reason well (John 9:30–33), but his strength was honesty about what he did not know and certainty about what he did.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Courage for timid believers, since the simplest testimony is enough. Humility in witness, saying 'I know not' where we do not know. And endurance, because Christ finds those who are cast out for his name.

### Assumed Here
- **Pneumatology:** John 9 does not mention the Holy Spirit, though the opening of spiritual eyes that the healing pictures is his work. Scripture teaches it plainly in John 3:5–8 (the new birth by the Spirit), John 16:8–14 (the Spirit who convicts the world and glorifies Christ), 1 Corinthians 2:14 (the things of the Spirit spiritually discerned), and Ephesians 1:17–18 (the eyes of the understanding enlightened).

## ⚖️ The Council's Hard Questions
### ❓ Question 1 · Put Out of the Synagogue: History or Anachronism
**Moderator:** John says that the authorities 'had agreed already' to put out of the synagogue anyone who confessed Jesus as Christ (John 9:22), and says it again in John 12:42 and John 16:2. Some scholars argue that no such ban existed until decades after Jesus, and that John has read the experience of his own church back into the story. How should a group handle that?

**Historian:** The facts first, as far as they are known (verify each). Later Jewish sources speak of a benediction in the synagogue's daily prayer, the twelfth of the Eighteen Benedictions, directed against heretics, and some associate it with the rabbinic gathering at Yavneh near the end of the first century. J. Louis Martyn argued in 1968 that John 9:22 reflects that benediction, and that the chapter tells the story of John's own community on a second level. But the date, the wording, and the purpose of that benediction are all debated, and there is no clear evidence that it was designed to expel Christians. Meanwhile, local synagogues in Jesus' day certainly had the power to discipline members, and the Gospels and Acts show synagogue authorities acting against Jesus' followers (Luke 6:22; Acts 9:1–2; 2 Corinthians 11:24).

**Church Historian:** Martyn's reading was very influential for a generation, and it did some good by making readers notice how much the chapter meant to Christians who had lost their synagogue. More recent work has pushed back. Carson and others argued that a local agreement by the Jerusalem authorities is entirely plausible in Jesus' lifetime, and Jonathan Bernier's study of the word argues the same at length (verify). The church through most of its history read the verse as history, and the burden of proof lies on the claim that it is not.

**Philologist:** The word itself is modest. *Aposunagōgos* (G656, John 9:22) means 'put out of the synagogue', excluded from the congregation. It does not name a formal empire-wide decree, and John's verb *sunetetheinto* (G4934, John 9:22), 'they had agreed', describes an agreement among 'the Jews', which in this chapter means the Jerusalem authorities (John 9:18). That fits a local decision in Jerusalem better than a later benediction recited everywhere.

**Literary Scholar:** And the story reads as a story, not a code. John places the threat in the parents' mouths and explains their fear as a narrator's aside, which is how he handles other historical details he expects his readers to need (John 4:9; 7:13). It is true that the chapter also speaks powerfully to later believers who were put out; good history often does. But a text can apply to later readers without having been invented for them.

**Theologian:** The theological point does not depend on settling the date. Whether the ban was local in AD 30 or formal later, John shows that confessing Jesus as Christ could cost a person his place among God's people, and that Christ receives those who pay that price (John 9:35). Jesus had already warned his disciples that it would happen to them (John 16:2), which is itself a claim that he foresaw it.

**Resolution:** The group should know that scholars debate whether John 9:22 describes a local agreement in Jesus' lifetime or reflects a later, more formal exclusion, and that there are good reasons, textual and historical, to read it as the local decision John describes. Either way, the chapter's point is plain: confessing Christ can cost a person their community, and Christ finds those who pay it. "Nevertheless among the chief rulers also many believed on him; but because of the Pharisees they did not confess him, lest they should be put out of the synagogue:" (John 12:42)

### ❓ Question 2 · Clay, Siloam, and Baptism
**Moderator:** Why did Jesus use clay and send the man to wash, when he healed others with a word? And the early church used John 9 in teaching people preparing for baptism. Does John 9 teach baptism, and if so, how?

**Church Historian:** The baptismal reading is very old. Augustine compared the man, anointed but not yet washed, to a catechumen (Tractate 44, verify). Early Christian writers called baptism 'illumination'; Justin Martyr uses that name in his *First Apology* (chapter 61, verify), and the letter to the Hebrews speaks of those who were 'once enlightened' (Hebrews 6:4). Early lectionaries in some churches read John 9 during the weeks before Easter baptisms (verify). Irenaeus, earlier still, read the clay as the Creator's hand finishing his work (verify).

**Philologist:** The words John uses set limits on that reading. The man is told to *wash himself*, *nipsai* (G3538, John 9:7), the verb of washing hands, face, or feet, not the verb of baptism, and John says nothing about the water's power. The one detail John interprets for us is the name of the pool: 'Sent' (John 9:7). He points us to the one who sent the man, not to the water.

**Theologian:** Reformed theology would say that John 9 is not a text about baptism, but it can illustrate what baptism signifies. Sacraments are signs and seals of what Christ does; they are not the power itself (Westminster Confession, chapter 27, paraphrased, verify). In John 9 the means are humble, the command must be obeyed, and the power belongs entirely to Christ. The man was not healed by Siloam's water but by the Sent One, through his word, as he obeyed. That is the right relation of means to grace in every age.

**Pastor:** In the group, some will come from churches that teach that baptism itself regenerates, and some from churches that barely mention it. Do not turn the lesson into a baptism debate. Say plainly that the power was in Christ, not in the clay or the pool, and that the man's obedience before he could see is a model for every believer's response to Christ's word.

**Teacher:** I would ask the group to list what the man contributed to his healing: walking to the pool and washing. Then list what Jesus contributed: seeing him, choosing him, making the clay, giving the command, and giving the sight. Let them notice which list is longer, and which is the cause.

**Resolution:** John 9 does not teach that water gives sight or life. It shows Christ, the Sent One, using humble means and requiring obedience, while keeping all the power in himself; John's own comment, 'Sent', points past the pool to him. The church has long used the chapter to illustrate what baptism signifies, and that use is fitting as long as the sign is not confused with the one it signifies. "And said unto him, Go, wash in the pool of Siloam, (which is by interpretation, Sent.) He went his way therefore, and washed, and came seeing." (John 9:7)

### ❓ Question 3 · Who Is Really Blind? Judgment and Guilt in John 9:39–41
**Moderator:** In John 3:17 Jesus says God did not send his Son to condemn the world. In John 9:39 he says, 'For judgment I am come into this world', and that those who see may be 'made blind'. Does Jesus come to blind people? And what does he mean by 'If ye were blind, ye should have no sin' (John 9:41)?

**Literary Scholar:** Look at the chapter's shape. It begins with a blind man and a question about sin (John 9:1–2), and it ends with seeing men and a verdict about sin (John 9:40–41). The man moves from blindness to sight: a man, a prophet, from God, Lord. The leaders move the other way: divided, disbelieving, certain, contemptuous, and at last blind. John has built the whole chapter as a reversal, and verse 39 simply names it.

**Philologist:** The words help. 'Judgment' here is *krima* (G2917, John 9:39), the verdict or outcome, not the process of judging. Jesus' coming has an outcome, a separation. And 'remaineth' is *menei* (G3306, John 9:41), 'stays'. The sin was already there; Jesus' coming does not create it, but their refusal makes it stay.

**Theologian:** So the two texts do not conflict. The purpose of the Son's coming is to save (John 3:17), but light always divides: 'this is the condemnation, that light is come into the world, and men loved darkness rather than light' (John 3:19). Those who say 'We see' while refusing the light are left in the blindness they prefer, the judicial blindness Isaiah announced (Isaiah 6:9–10) and John will quote (John 12:40). As for John 9:41, Scripture teaches that guilt is measured by the light received (Luke 12:47–48; John 15:22). If they were truly without light, their unbelief would not carry this guilt; but they claim the light, the Scriptures, and the knowledge of God, and use it to reject the one the Scriptures promised. That is sin that remains.

**Pastor:** Two kinds of people in the group will hear this differently. Tender consciences will fear that they are the Pharisees. Tell them that the Pharisees' mark was not doubt but certainty: they said 'We see'. Anyone who is afraid of being blind and asks Christ for sight is already on the healed man's side of the chapter. And a few will be quick to put other people in the Pharisees' seat. Turn the question round, as the Pharisees themselves asked it: 'Are we blind also?' (John 9:40).

**Teacher:** I would draw two columns on a board, one for the man and one for the leaders, and have the group fill in each column verse by verse, what they say about Jesus and what they do. Then ask: at what point did each start moving the way they did? The answer for the man is a command obeyed; the answer for the leaders is a rule that could not be questioned.

**Resolution:** Jesus came to save, but his coming inevitably brings a verdict, because people respond to the light in opposite ways. Those who confess their blindness receive sight; those who claim to see while refusing him are left in their blindness, and their guilt is greater because of the light they claim. The way out of the Pharisees' seat is always open: to stop saying 'We see', and to ask him for sight. "Jesus said unto them, If ye were blind, ye should have no sin: but now ye say, We see; therefore your sin remaineth." (John 9:41)

### 🔦 The Life & Light Audit
**Pastor:** If a hearer obeyed this lesson exactly, then tonight they would read John 9:35–38 and thank the Lord that he found them first; they would write down, in three or four sentences, what Christ has done for them; and they would ask the Lord to show them one place where they have said 'We see'. Tomorrow they would seek out someone who has been left out or pushed out, and tell one person what Jesus has done. What would come alive is their own witness, long silent, and hope in someone who has been cast aside. The dark place lit would be their own certainty, first.

**Theologian:** The practices keep source discipline. The Life practices begin from Christ who sees and seeks (John 9:1, 35), and none asks the believer to heal or rescue anyone by their own power; the believer goes, listens, and tells what Christ has done. The Light practices turn first on the participant's own blindness (Q25; the Inward practices) and forbid the Pharisees' method of exposing others; that keeps the Love Guard. The Abundance Guard is kept by showing that the man's life became fuller and harder at once, and by refusing to promise physical healing to all who believe.

**Teacher:** A Level 1 participant can do the Inward Life practice tonight: read four verses and pray one sentence. The Outward practices name the kind of person to reach (someone left out of a group; someone everyone passes by; someone housebound or disabled; a new believer rejected by family; a believer facing hostility) and leave a blank for the name. The testimony exercise (Q20) is short enough for a new believer: what I was, what he did, what I am now.

**Fix:** The Audit found three risks, and the unit addresses them. First, a lesson about a blind man can wound members who live with disability or care for someone who does, either by blaming them or by promising a cure; so the Common Errors table answers both, the Cultural Landmines address blindness as a picture, the Debate Corner's Pastoral Warning forbids the glib use of John 9:3, and the Shepherding Notes name the tender places. Second, 'For judgment I am come' can be turned against others; so the Light questions begin with the participant's own 'We see' (Q25), the Leader's Map coaches the member who points the light outward, and the Council's third question turns 'Are we blind also?' on the group. Third, members who have been hurt by church discipline may hear the lesson as a charter against all discipline, and church leaders may hear it as being about someone else; so the Cultural Landmines and the Ecclesiology block distinguish loving discipline from casting out for Christ's sake. The naming round reads only the second row of the Light Planner, never the private first row.

### ✅ Answer Key
**Q1.** Jesus saw the man first, as he passed by; the man was blind from birth (John 9:1). The disciples asked who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind; their question assumed the blindness was a punishment either for the man's own sin or for his parents' (John 9:2).

**Q2.** Jesus said neither the man nor his parents had sinned so as to cause it, but that the works of God should be made manifest in him (John 9:3). He said he must work the works of him that sent him while it is day, because the night comes when no one can work, and that while he is in the world he is the light of the world (John 9:4–5).

**Q3.** Jesus spat on the ground, made clay of the spittle, anointed the man's eyes with the clay, and told him to go and wash in the pool of Siloam (John 9:6–7). The man went, washed, and came back seeing. John says Siloam means 'Sent' (John 9:7).

**Q4.** The neighbours asked whether this was the man who sat and begged; some said it was, others said he only looked like him (John 9:8–9). He said, 'I am he' (John 9:9), and described 'A man that is called Jesus' who made clay, anointed his eyes, and sent him to Siloam, where he washed and received sight (John 9:11). He did not know where Jesus was (John 9:12).

**Q5.** It was the sabbath day (John 9:14). Some Pharisees said Jesus was not of God because he did not keep the sabbath; others asked how a sinner could do such miracles, and there was a division among them (John 9:16). The man said Jesus was a prophet (John 9:17).

**Q6.** They said they knew he was their son and that he was born blind, but did not know how he now saw or who opened his eyes; he was of age, so they should ask him (John 9:20–21). They answered this way because they feared the Jews, who had agreed that anyone who confessed Jesus as Christ would be put out of the synagogue (John 9:22).

**Q7.** They told him to give God the praise and said they knew that Jesus was a sinner (John 9:24). He said he did not know whether Jesus was a sinner, but one thing he knew: that he was blind and now he sees (John 9:25). They cast him out (John 9:34).

**Q8.** Jesus heard that they had cast him out, found him, and asked whether he believed on the Son of God (John 9:35). The man asked who he was, so that he might believe; Jesus said he had seen him and was talking with him (John 9:36–37). The man said, 'Lord, I believe', and worshipped him (John 9:38).

**Q9.** The LORD is the one who makes the seeing and the blind (Exodus 4:11); he promised that in the day of salvation the eyes of the blind would be opened (Isaiah 35:5), and that his servant would be a light to the Gentiles to open blind eyes (Isaiah 42:6–7). Spiritual blindness is the work of the god of this world, and only God's light in the face of Christ removes it (2 Corinthians 4:4–6); Christ sends his people to open eyes (Acts 26:18); and a church that thinks it sees may be blind (Revelation 3:17–18). Together they show that Jesus' claim to be the light (John 9:5) and his unprecedented healing (John 9:32) mark him as the promised one, and that the judgment of John 9:39 falls on those who think they see.

**Q10.** Jesus denies that this blindness was a punishment for a particular sin of the man or his parents (John 9:3). He does not deny that all people are sinners (Romans 3:23) or that sin brought suffering into the world. He moves the question from cause to purpose: God's works would be shown in the man, and Jesus must do those works now (John 9:3–4).

**Q11.** The man's view grows: a man called Jesus, a prophet, one from God, and finally Lord and Son of God, whom he worships (John 9:11, 17, 33, 38). The leaders' view shrinks: divided, then convinced he is a sinner, then saying they do not know where he is from (John 9:16, 24, 29), until Jesus says they are blind (John 9:41). John shows that sight is growing in the one who admits his need, and blindness is growing in those who claim to know.

**Q12.** The light does not create blindness; it reveals and confirms it. When light comes, those who love darkness turn from it (John 3:19), and their refusal leaves them blinder than before. The Pharisees claimed to see, so their sin remained (John 9:40–41).

**Q13.** Suggested answer: the man's sentence is his argument, recorded truthfully by John, and it is true as a principle about the proud and unrepentant (Psalm 66:18; Proverbs 15:29). It does not mean God never hears a sinner who cries for mercy (Luke 18:13–14). Both views agree that his conclusion was right: Jesus was of God (John 9:33). Reading it as his argument keeps us from turning it into a rule that would shut the penitent out of prayer.

**Q14.** Answers will vary. Saul thought he saw clearly and was struck blind so that he could truly see (Acts 9:8–9, 18); John 9:39 describes his story exactly. It cost him his standing among the Pharisees and put his life in danger (Acts 9:23), and he gained Christ, counting all else loss (Philippians 3:7–8).

**Q15.** Answers will vary. Marcus's one thing was that Christ had set him free from drinking and begun to restore his family (John 9:25). A testimony cannot be argued away, because it is a fact of experience, but it should be told with meekness and not as a weapon (1 Peter 3:15). Encourage members to write their three or four sentences.

**Q16.** Answers will vary. The words are simple, honest, and true of every believer: we were lost and blind, and Christ found us and gave us sight (John 9:25, 35). To say them honestly is to confess that we did not open our own eyes, and so the glory goes to him (2 Corinthians 4:6).

**Q17.** God rules even over a man's blindness from birth and had a good purpose in it (John 9:3; Exodus 4:11). The verse does not tell us God's particular purpose in every suffering, and it does not tell us that suffering is a punishment. Romans 8:28 promises that God works all things for the good of those who love him, even when we cannot see how.

**Q18.** Answers will vary. The man had lost his community and his parents' support, and Jesus sought him out (John 9:34–35). Members may speak of times they were excluded or overlooked; point them to Jesus who seeks the lost (Luke 19:10) and receives all who come (John 6:37).

**Q19.** Answers will vary. Members may name family, work, school, or friendships (John 9:22; John 12:42–43). The man's worship shows what Jesus is worth, and it puts fear of losing a place in its right proportion (Proverbs 29:25; Matthew 10:32).

**Q20.** Answers will vary, but the testimony should be short and concrete: what I was, what Christ did, what I am now (John 9:25). Humbling ourselves means telling it so that Christ is the subject, not our own effort or change (1 Corinthians 4:7; John 15:5).

**Q21.** Model answer, not the only answer: we often find it hardest to learn from people who are younger, less educated, of a different background, or who have hurt us. The Pharisees' pride kept them blind (John 9:34, 41). It makes Christ more glorious that he chose to give the clearest sight to a beggar (1 Corinthians 1:27; Matthew 11:25).

**Q22.** Model answer, not the only answer: a coworker left out of the group; a relative who is the family's black sheep; a church member who stopped coming after a conflict; a disabled neighbour; a person who sits alone (John 9:1, 8, 34). Encourage members to write a specific name.

**Q23.** Model answer, not the only answer: the thief may have stolen their dignity, their belonging, their family's support, their good name, or their hope (John 10:10; John 9:2, 21–22, 34).

**Q24.** Model answer, not the only answer: Christ gives himself: he finds, reveals himself, and receives faith and worship (John 9:35–38). It matters that he went looking, because the man could not find his way back in, and no program could give him what Jesus gave: a Lord and a place in his flock (John 10:3, 16).

**Q25.** Model answer, not the only answer: a settled opinion about a person or group, a habit we will not examine, a way of reading Scripture we will not question, a sin we have renamed (John 9:41; Isaiah 5:21). The honest answer belongs in the private first row of the Light Planner.

**Q26.** Model answer, not the only answer: at work, at family gatherings, among old friends, or online, where confessing Christ might cost approval (John 9:22; John 12:43). Name the fear and bring it to the Lord (Proverbs 29:25).

**Q27.** Model answer, not the only answer: a friend who thinks Christianity is for weak people, a relative who has rejected faith, a coworker who has never heard the gospel plainly (2 Corinthians 4:4). Carry light by telling what Christ has done for you, honestly and without argument, and by praying that God will shine in their heart (2 Corinthians 4:6; John 9:25).

### 🗺️ Life & Light Leader's Map
**Worked Life Planner Row:** *Who:* 'Ruth, my neighbour, who was asked to leave her old church after her divorce and hasn't been to any church in three years'. *What the thief has stolen:* her belonging and her sense that God still wants her; she has been cast out, as the man was (John 9:34). *What Christ gives instead:* the Jesus who 'heard that they had cast him out' and went and found him (John 9:35). *What I will do:* on Saturday, take her a coffee and ask how she really is, and listen; not mention church unless she does; and if she does, tell her that Jesus went looking for a man everyone had thrown out, and that he is still like that. *Done?* Tick it when done, and write one line about how it went.

**Worked Light Planner Row:** *Where is it dark (second row):* 'the way our small group talks about people who have left the church'. *What is hidden there:* a self-satisfied certainty that we see and they are blind, and some gossip dressed as concern (John 9:24, 41). *What light Christ shines:* 'Are we blind also?' (John 9:40), and the Jesus who seeks the cast out (John 9:35). *How I carry it there:* at the next meeting, when the talk turns that way, say gently that I would rather pray for them by name, and suggest we each contact one person we've lost touch with.

**When Someone Has No One to Name:** Walk through their week with them: the person at work who eats lunch alone; the relative nobody invites; the neighbour who uses a wheelchair and rarely goes out; the young person who has drifted from church; the friend whose family cut them off. Remind them that Jesus 'saw a man' whom the crowd had walked past for years (John 9:1, 8), and that the disciples saw only a question until Jesus showed them a person. If they still cannot name anyone, ask them to pray tonight, 'Lord, show me one person I have been walking past', and to bring the name next week.

**When Someone Points the Light at Others:** When a member names another person's, another church's, or another group's blindness as their dark place, bring them gently back to the first row of their Light Planner and to John 9:40. Say something like: 'The Pharisees' question is the right one for all of us: are we blind also? Let's start there.' Remind them that the Pharisees' fault was exactly this, seeing everyone's blindness but their own, and that Jesus' light fell first on the proud, not on the beggar. If the member persists, speak with them privately after the session.

### 🐑 Shepherding Notes
- **Members with disabilities, or who care for someone with one.** John 9 can wound as easily as it heals. Some members, or their children, may have been born with a disability; some have been told, even in churches, that it must be sin or weak faith. Say clearly that Jesus rejected that idea (John 9:3). Do not use blindness as a figure of speech that mocks, and do not promise that every disability will be healed in this life. Let them know that Jesus saw the man first, as a person, and that he sees them.
- **Members living with chronic illness or unanswered prayer for healing.** They may hear the healing story as a rebuke: why not me? Acknowledge that the Bible does not promise every healing now, that Paul's prayer was answered with grace instead (2 Corinthians 12:9), and that the man's greatest gift was not his eyes but the Son of God he worshipped.
- **Members who have been put out of a church or a family.** Some have experienced exclusion, sometimes deserved, often not. John 9:34–35 may touch deep wounds. Do not rush to judge the situations they describe; let the verse speak: Jesus went and found him. Offer to talk privately.
- **Members whose faith has cost them their family's approval.** Converts from other faiths or from non-religious families may know the parents' silence and the man's loneliness from the inside. Honour what it has cost them, and make sure the group becomes the family they need (Mark 10:29–30).
- **Parents.** The parents in John 9 failed their son out of fear. Some parents in the group will feel that sting about their own children. Be gentle: the point is not to shame them but to invite courage now.
- **Church leaders in the group.** The leaders in John 9 held power and abused it. Anyone who holds authority in a church should hear this lesson as addressed to them first, not to someone else.
- **Members afraid of being spiritually blind.** Some tender consciences will fear that they are the Pharisees. Reassure them that the Pharisees' mark was the claim 'We see', and that anyone who asks Christ for sight is on the healed man's side.
- **The Light Planner's first row.** It is private. Never ask anyone to read it aloud or to share it. If someone chooses to speak about their own heart, receive it with gentleness and pray for them; do not probe for details.

### 🎯 Teaching Angles
**For a Tier 1 Group:** Bring blindfolds, a basin of water, and a towel. Ask two volunteers to wear the blindfolds while the story is read; ask them afterwards what it was like not to see, and what it was like when the blindfold came off. Then act out the chapter with simple parts: the man, the neighbours, the leaders, the parents, and Jesus. Pause at John 9:25 and let everyone say the man's line together: 'one thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see'. End by reading John 9:35–38 and asking: 'Who went looking for whom?'

**For a Tier 2 Group:** Trace three lines through the chapter. The line of sight: 'blind' occurs 15 times in the KJV of John 9, and the chapter moves from blind eyes (John 9:1) to blind hearts (John 9:39–41); ask who ends the chapter seeing, and who blind. The line of knowing: 'we know' occurs 7 times in the KJV of John 9; set the leaders' claims to know (John 9:24, 29) beside the man's 'one thing I know' (John 9:25), and ask what kind of knowing leads to sight. The line of titles: list what the man calls Jesus (John 9:11, 17, 33, 36, 38) and what the leaders call him (John 9:16, 24, 29). Then ask the group where they are on each line.

**Tough Question 1:** *Did God make that man blind just so Jesus could show off?* No. Jesus said the blindness was not a punishment, and that God's works would be shown in him (John 9:3). The works shown were the man's healing, his faith, and his worship of the Son of God (John 9:7, 38), which were for his own good as much as for God's glory; in Scripture those are never at odds. We are not told God's reasons in every suffering, but we are told that God is at work in it and that he is good (Romans 8:28). See the Debate Corner.

**Tough Question 2:** *If Jesus healed that man, why doesn't he heal me, or my child?* Jesus did not heal every blind person in Israel; he healed this man, as a sign of who he is (John 9:5, 32). Paul prayed three times for healing and received grace instead (2 Corinthians 12:8–9). God's love is not measured by whether he removes a suffering now. The full healing Jesus promises comes at the resurrection, when God 'shall wipe away all tears' (Revelation 21:4); until then, the same Jesus who found the man finds and keeps us.

**Tough Question 3:** *Isn't it arrogant to say that people who disagree with us are 'blind'?* It would be, if we said it the way the Pharisees did. The chapter warns most sharply against the people who are sure they see (John 9:41). Christians should say 'I was blind' before we ever say anyone else is (John 9:25), and should remember that we did not open our own eyes (2 Corinthians 4:6). The difference between a believer and an unbeliever is not that one is clever and the other foolish, but that one has been given sight by Christ.
