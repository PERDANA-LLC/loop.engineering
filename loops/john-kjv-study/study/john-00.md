# The Gospel of John · Book Overview (KJV)
> Unit john-00 · John 1–21 · loop john-kjv-study v2.0 · DRAFT for human doctrine review

## 🧭 At a Glance
### 🧵 Golden Thread
John wrote his Gospel so that its readers would believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and by believing have life through his name (John 20:31): every sign, saying, feast, and witness in the book is set down to bring us to that faith and to keep us in it.

### 🌗 Life & Light Frame
John speaks into a world that is dying in its sins, where even religion can search the Scriptures and still refuse to come to the One they speak of, and he shows us the Son who has life in himself and gives it, eternal life that begins now, to all who believe on him. John speaks into a world that loves darkness rather than light, blind while it says 'We see', and he shows us the Light of the world, full of grace and truth, who exposes sin in order to heal it and gives the light of life to all who follow him.

### 🎯 Course Outcomes
- Explain John's stated purpose from John 20:30–31 and show how any chapter of John serves it, through its signs, its sayings, its witnesses, or its hour.
- Trace the book's main threads (the seven signs, the 'I am' sayings, the feasts, the hour, the witnesses, and the key words believe, life, and light) and say what each reveals about who Jesus is.
- State from John's own words the deity and true humanity of Christ, the necessity of new birth, and the gift of eternal life through believing, and answer the most common errors about each.
- Read any chapter of John in the King James Version with growing confidence, separating what the text says from what it means and from what it asks of the reader.
- **Doing:** Over the 21 lessons, carry life to at least one named person and light into at least one named dark place each week, recording in the Life & Light Ledger what Christ did through you.

## 🏛️ Core Foundations
### ✝️ Christological Root
**Gospel Foundation:** The whole book rests on who Jesus is and what he did, and John tells us so twice, at the beginning and at the end. At the beginning: "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth." (John 1:14) At the end, having recorded the finished work of the cross, "It is finished" (John 19:30), and the risen Lord's appearing, John says why he wrote: "But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name." (John 20:31)

**The Bridge:** Everything John records is chosen to establish one doctrine and to produce one response. The doctrine is the person of Christ: the eternal Word who is God, made flesh without ceasing to be God, the promised Christ of Israel, and the only begotten Son who makes the Father known (John 1:1, 14, 18). The response is faith in him, and the gift that faith receives is eternal life, which John defines as knowing the only true God and Jesus Christ whom he has sent (John 17:3). Christology and soteriology are bound together in this book: who Jesus is decides what believing on him gives.

**Canonical Echoes:** Jesus (John 20:31) → Promise (Isaiah 9:6) → Doctrine (1 John 5:11–12) → Life (Galatians 2:20)

**What Gets Lost:** Taught without Christ at the center, John becomes a book of beautiful sayings about love and light, a mystical Gospel for the spiritually inclined, or a mine of proof texts for arguments. Its signs become magic tricks or moral lessons, its 'I am' sayings become slogans, and its call to believe becomes a call to try harder at being spiritual. John's own point is lost: that the man Jesus of Nazareth is the eternal Son of God, that life is found in him and nowhere else, and that the reader must decide what to do with him.

### ⚔️ Spiritual Warfare
**The Lie:** Jesus was a good teacher and a spiritual man, one light among many, and we can see well enough without him. The religious form of the lie is subtler: we already have God, the Scriptures, and our tradition, so we have no need to come to Jesus for life.

**The Truth:** Jesus himself names the liar and answers the lie. The devil "was a murderer from the beginning, and abode not in the truth" (John 8:44), and Jesus says, "I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me." (John 14:6)

**The Battleground:** The mind's verdict about Jesus. In chapter after chapter the crowds argue, 'He is a good man' or 'he deceiveth the people' (John 7:12), and the battle is fought in the judgment each hearer makes about who he is. Behind that lies the will, which loves darkness and will not come (John 3:19; 5:40).

**The Weapon:** The word and the witness. Jesus answers every accusation with the testimony of the Father, the works, and the Scriptures (John 5:36–39), and he sends the Spirit of truth to testify of him (John 15:26). The believer fights with the same weapons: Scripture believed, the Spirit's witness, and the plain testimony of what Christ has done, "whereas I was blind, now I see" (John 9:25). And the victory is already his: "be of good cheer; I have overcome the world" (John 16:33).

### 🌱 Life Pathway
**The Theft:** In John the thief has a name and a method. He comes "for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy" (John 10:10); he was a murderer and a liar from the beginning (John 8:44). In John's own scenes the theft looks like this: a man infirm for thirty-eight years, lying by a pool with no one to help him (John 5:5–7); a crowd fed with bread that perishes and wanting only more of it (John 6:26–27); religious leaders who search the Scriptures for life and will not come to the One they testify of (John 5:39–40); a grave four days old and a family sure that Jesus had come too late (John 11:21, 39); disciples scattered and hiding behind shut doors for fear (John 20:19). The deepest theft is death itself, and the slavery of sin that leads to it (John 8:34).

**The Source:** Christ alone. "For as the Father hath life in himself; so hath he given to the Son to have life in himself" (John 5:26), and he came "that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly" (John 10:10). His means in John are his word, which is spirit and life (John 6:63), his Spirit, who gives new birth (John 3:5–6), his own flesh given for the life of the world (John 6:51), and his resurrection (John 11:25).

**The Channel:** Life flows through believers, never from them. The one who drinks of Christ becomes a channel: "out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water" (John 7:38), which John explains as the Spirit (John 7:39). The branch bears fruit only as it abides in the vine (John 15:5), and the disciples are sent as the Son was sent (John 20:21). So in this course every Outward Life practice sends a believer to a named person with what Christ has given, never with what the believer has made.

**The Abundance Guard:** Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, not prosperity, ease, or freedom from suffering. The same Gospel that promises life more abundantly promises tribulation in the world (John 16:33), foretells Peter's death (John 21:18–19), and shows Jesus weeping at a grave (John 11:35) and dying on a cross (John 19:30). The fullness is knowing God in Christ (John 17:3), and it holds in poverty and grief.

### 💡 Light Pathway
**The Darkness:** John names it plainly: "men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil" (John 3:19). In his scenes it takes many shapes: a teacher of Israel who comes by night and cannot understand (John 3:2, 10); a woman who hides her history (John 4:16–18); accusers who expose another's sin to trap Jesus (John 8:3–6); Pharisees who say 'We see' and so remain blind (John 9:41); a disciple who goes out, "and it was night" (John 13:30); a governor who asks what truth is and walks away from it (John 18:38).

**The Source:** Christ, who says, "I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life." (John 8:12) He is the true Light "which lighteth every man that cometh into the world" (John 1:9), for "God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts" (2 Corinthians 4:6).

**The Lamp:** The word of Christ opened and believed (John 17:17); the truth told, as the Samaritan woman told it (John 4:29, 39); hidden sin confessed and forgiven (1 John 1:9); the unfruitful works of darkness reproved, beginning at home (Ephesians 5:11); and good works that glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16), done in the open, as Jesus said of the one who does truth: he "cometh to the light, that his deeds may be made manifest, that they are wrought in God" (John 3:21).

**The Love Guard:** Light without love is glare. The Light of John's Gospel is full of grace and truth (John 1:14): he exposes the woman at the well in order to give her living water, and he sends away the accusers of the woman taken in adultery before he tells her to sin no more (John 8:7–11). The light we carry is his, and it is carried in his manner: our own hearts first, then others, with tenderness and never with contempt or public shaming.

### 📘 How to Use This Workbook
**What this workbook is.** Twenty-one lessons, one for each chapter of John, each built for one 75-minute session of an adult small group. Every lesson has three parts, and they fit together like this:
- **The participant pages** (The Lesson) are for everyone. They open with prayer and a warm-up, print the whole chapter in the King James Version for marking, and then climb a ladder of questions: Observe (what does it say?), Interpret (what does it mean?), Apply (head, heart, and hands), Christocentric & Humility (how is Christ exalted and how am I humbled?), and Bring Life and Bring Light (who will I carry life to, and what dark place will I carry light into?). The pages are mostly questions with lines to write on. The answers are not printed there on purpose.
- **The Study** (Tiers 1 to 4, the Doctrine, and the Council's Hard Questions) is for the leader and for any participant who wants to go deeper. Tier 1 is simple enough for a child, Tier 2 walks through the chapter verse by verse, Tier 3 is for the teacher who wants the scholarship, and Tier 4 turns it all to Christ and practice.
- **The Leader's Guide** at the end of each lesson has a minute-by-minute session plan, an answer key with verse support, a glossary of the chapter's older words, and notes for the tender places the chapter touches.

**The individual track (before the group meets).** Pray Psalm 119:18 and read the chapter twice, once straight through and once with a pencil. Mark it with the marking key below. Answer the Observe questions from the text alone before looking anywhere else. When you meet a word the King James Version uses in an older sense (*let* for hinder, *prevent* for go before, *conversation* for way of life), look it up in Noah Webster's *American Dictionary of the English Language* (1828), which often gives the older senses the King James Version uses and frequently illustrates them from Scripture, or in the lesson's Glossary. For the Word Study, find the word's first appearance in the Bible (the Law of First Mention: the first place Scripture uses a word or theme often sets a pattern for the rest; a useful habit, not an unbreakable rule) and one more place in John where it occurs. Then work up the ladder as far as you can. Not finishing all five levels is normal; the group will help.

**The group track (when the group meets).** The session runs 75 minutes: opening prayer and warm-up, reading the chapter aloud, working through the ladder together, and the four Gather questions, the last of which is the naming round, where each member reads aloud the name on their Life Planner and the dark place on their Light Planner (never the private first row, which is about their own heart). Bring the workbook, a King James Bible, and a pencil.

**The marking key.** Circle repeated words · underline commands · box names of God · double-underline promises. In John, also watch for four things he wants you to notice: the signs, the 'I am' sayings, the feasts, and the word *hour*.

**The Life & Light Ledger.** At the end of each week, write one line on the Ledger below: the name you carried life to and what you did, the dark place you carried light into and what you did, and any answer to prayer. By the last lesson it will be a record of what Christ has done through the group, not a scorecard.

**How to read the King James Version with this workbook.** Every quotation in this workbook is copied from the King James Version and checked word for word. *Thee*, *thou*, and *thy* are singular (one person); *ye* and *you* are plural (more than one). John's Gospel often turns on that difference: when Jesus says "Ye must be born again" (John 3:7), he says it to Nicodemus but the pronoun reaches beyond him to everyone Nicodemus represents.

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy
### 📌 Core Concept
This book was written by one of Jesus' closest friends, whom Christians have long believed to be John the fisherman. The church remembers that when he was an old man, he wrote down some of the things Jesus did and said, and at the end of his book he told us why: so that we would believe that Jesus is the Christ, God's own Son, and so that by believing in him we would have life that never ends. John shows us Jesus doing seven amazing signs, like turning water into wine and raising his friend Lazarus from the dead. He shows us Jesus saying 'I am' about himself: I am the bread of life, I am the light of the world, I am the good shepherd. And he shows us Jesus dying on the cross for us and coming back to life. The whole book asks one question: who is Jesus, and will you trust him?

### 📖 Key Verses
"But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name." (John 20:31) John wrote his book so we would trust Jesus and live.

"For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." (John 3:16) God loved us so much that he gave us his Son.

"Then spake Jesus again unto them, saying, I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life." (John 8:12) If we follow Jesus, we don't have to be lost in the dark.

"Jesus saith unto him, I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me." (John 14:6) Jesus is the only way to God.

### 🪞 Analogy
Imagine a grandmother who makes a photo album about her father, who died before you were born. She can't fit in every picture, so she chooses the best ones. On the last page she writes: 'I made this so that you would know him and love him too.' John's Gospel is like that album. John couldn't write down everything Jesus did (John 21:25), so he chose the stories that show us best who Jesus is. And on one of the last pages he tells us why he made it: so that we would know Jesus and trust him and live.

### 🔎 Diagnostic Check
- If a friend asked me who Jesus is, could I tell them something true about him from the Bible?
- Do I think of Jesus as someone who is alive today, and not just someone from a long time ago?
- When I am scared or sad, do I ever talk to Jesus about it?
- Is there something I have done that I am hiding and hoping nobody finds out?
- Do I know someone who is lonely or sad who needs to hear that Jesus loves them?

This week, read one short part of John every day with a grown-up or by yourself: John 1:1–14 on the first day, then the wedding at Cana (John 2:1–11), then the good shepherd (John 10:1–15). Each time, finish this sentence out loud: 'Jesus is ...' and then thank him for what you found.

### 🌗 Life and Light, Simply
Jesus gives life: he makes dead things alive, like Lazarus, and he makes people who are far from God into God's own children. Jesus gives light: he helps us see what is true, he shows us what is wrong inside us, and he loves us enough to make it right.

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium
### 🏺 Setting the Scene
**Who wrote it.** The book does not put its author's name on its first page. It points to him instead. At its close we read: "This is the disciple which testifieth of these things, and wrote these things: and we know that his testimony is true." (John 21:24) That disciple is "the disciple whom Jesus loved" (John 21:20), who leaned on Jesus' bosom at the Last Supper (John 13:23), stood at the cross (John 19:26), ran to the empty tomb (John 20:2–4), and recognised the risen Lord on the shore (John 21:7). He was an eyewitness: "he that saw it bare record, and his record is true" (John 19:35). The church has received this disciple, from the second century on, as John the son of Zebedee, one of the Twelve and brother of James. That identification is **tradition**, strong and early but not stated in the text. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons late in the second century, wrote that John, the disciple of the Lord who leaned on his breast, published the Gospel while living at Ephesus (*Against Heresies*, book 3). Support from the text itself is indirect but real: the Gospel never names John the son of Zebedee, though he is prominent in the other three Gospels, and it calls the Baptist simply 'John', as if the author had no need to distinguish himself. Some scholars, ancient and modern, suggest another John, 'John the Elder', or an unnamed Jerusalem disciple; the Council's Hard Questions below weigh these views.

**When and where.** Tradition places the writing late in the first century, at Ephesus in the Roman province of Asia, after the other three Gospels were written. Many scholars date it in the AD 80s or 90s; some argue for a date before the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, pointing to the present tense of "Now there is at Jerusalem by the sheep market a pool" (John 5:2). Neither date is certain, and nothing in the book's message depends on the year.

**For whom.** John explains Jewish words and customs as he goes: "Messias, which is, being interpreted, the Christ" (John 1:41); "Rabboni; which is to say, Master" (John 20:16); the waterpots "after the manner of the purifying of the Jews" (John 2:6). So he writes for readers who include people outside Palestine and outside Judaism, as well as Jewish readers who know the Scriptures well, since he assumes they will catch his many allusions to Moses, the feasts, and the prophets.

**Why.** Luke tells Theophilus why he wrote (Luke 1:1–4); John tells every reader, and names the response he wants: "And many other signs truly did Jesus in the presence of his disciples, which are not written in this book:" (John 20:30) "But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name." (John 20:31) Two points follow for every lesson in this course. First, John *selected*: the book is not a complete biography, and he tells us so again at its end (John 21:25). Second, every selection serves one aim: faith in Jesus as the Christ and the Son of God, and life through that faith.

**How John differs from Matthew, Mark, and Luke.** Readers who know the other Gospels notice at once that John is different. He narrates much of Jesus' ministry in Judea and Jerusalem, not only in Galilee. He mentions several Passovers (John 2:13; 6:4; 11:55), which is why the church has long spoken of a ministry of about three years. He records long discourses and conversations (with Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman, the crowds at Capernaum, the disciples in the upper room) rather than short parables; in fact John records no parable of the kind the others have, though he has figures such as the shepherd and the vine. He leaves out much that they include (the birth in Bethlehem, the baptism narrated directly, the temptation, the transfiguration, the institution of the Lord's Supper) and includes much they leave out (Cana, Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman, the man born blind, Lazarus, the foot washing, the farewell discourses). The early church explained the difference not as contradiction but as complement: Clement of Alexandria, as reported by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History*, book 6), said that John, knowing the outward facts had been set out in the other Gospels, composed a 'spiritual Gospel'. The four Gospels are four witnesses to one Lord, and John often assumes his readers know the others (for example, he mentions that John the Baptist was not yet in prison, John 3:24, as if readers knew he later was).

### 📚 Chapter-by-Chapter Breakdown
#### John 1 · The Word Made Flesh
The Gospel opens before creation: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1) The Word made all things, and "In him was life; and the life was the light of men." (John 1:4) The Prologue (John 1:1–18) introduces nearly every theme of the book: life and light, darkness that does not overcome, witness, the world, believing, rejection by his own and new birth for those who receive him, and at its center, "the Word was made flesh" (John 1:14). The rest of the chapter is the first week of witness: John the Baptist denies that he is the Christ and points to Jesus, "Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world." (John 1:29) Disciples follow, one bringing another: Andrew finds Simon, Philip finds Nathanael, and the invitation is simple, "Come and see" (John 1:39, 46). The chapter piles up titles for Jesus: the Word, the Lamb of God, the Son of God, Rabbi, the Messias, the King of Israel, and the Son of man upon whom angels ascend and descend (John 1:51).

#### John 2 · The First Sign and the Cleansed Temple
At a wedding in Cana, Jesus turns six waterpots of purifying water into the best wine: "This beginning of miracles did Jesus in Cana of Galilee, and manifested forth his glory; and his disciples believed on him." (John 2:11) His reply to his mother, "mine hour is not yet come" (John 2:4), is the first mention of the hour that runs through the book to the cross. At Passover in Jerusalem he drives the traders from the temple and speaks of a greater temple: "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (John 2:19), "But he spake of the temple of his body." (John 2:21) The chapter ends with a warning about faith that rests on signs alone: Jesus "knew what was in man" (John 2:25). Wine for water and a body for a building: John begins to show that Jesus fulfils what Israel's worship pictured.

#### John 3 · Ye Must Be Born Again
Nicodemus, a Pharisee and ruler, comes by night, and Jesus tells him, "Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." (John 3:3) The new birth is the work of the Spirit, as sovereign and as real as the wind (John 3:8), and it comes to those who look to the Son lifted up, as Israel looked to the brass serpent (John 3:14–15). Then comes the Bible's best-known verse: "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." (John 3:16) The light has come, and the judgment is that "men loved darkness rather than light" (John 3:19). The chapter closes with the Baptist's last witness, "He must increase, but I must decrease." (John 3:30), and a summary that sets life and wrath side by side (John 3:36).

#### John 4 · Living Water and the Saviour of the World
At Jacob's well, Jesus, tired and thirsty, asks a Samaritan woman for a drink, crossing the lines of race, religion, sex, and reputation. He offers her living water: "the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life" (John 4:14). He exposes her history without contempt, teaches that the Father seeks those who worship "in spirit and in truth" (John 4:24), and reveals himself plainly as the Messiah (John 4:26). She leaves her waterpot and brings her town, and they confess him "the Christ, the Saviour of the world" (John 4:42). Back in Cana, Jesus heals a nobleman's son at Capernaum by a word spoken miles away, and John counts it: "This is again the second miracle that Jesus did" (John 4:54).

#### John 5 · The Son Who Gives Life
At an unnamed "feast of the Jews" (John 5:1), Jesus heals a man who has been lame for thirty-eight years at the pool of Bethesda, and he does it on the sabbath. When he is charged, Jesus answers that he works as his Father works, and the leaders understand him exactly: he was "making himself equal with God" (John 5:18). Jesus does not retract. He claims that the Son gives life to whom he will, that all judgment is committed to him, and that all should honour the Son as they honour the Father (John 5:21–23). "He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life, and shall not come into condemnation; but is passed from death unto life." (John 5:24) He then calls his witnesses: John the Baptist, his works, the Father, the Scriptures, and Moses, and he grieves over hearers who search the Scriptures and yet "will not come to me, that ye might have life" (John 5:40).

#### John 6 · The Bread of Life
Near the Passover, Jesus feeds five thousand from five barley loaves and two fishes, the only miracle of his ministry, apart from the resurrection, that all four Gospels record, and the crowd wants to make him king by force (John 6:15). That night he comes to his disciples walking on the sea: "It is I; be not afraid." (John 6:20) At Capernaum he explains the sign: he is the true bread from heaven, better than the manna. "I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger; and he that believeth on me shall never thirst." (John 6:35) He speaks of the Father giving, drawing, and keeping (John 6:37–44), and of eating his flesh and drinking his blood (John 6:53–58). Many disciples leave. Peter answers for the Twelve: "Lord, to whom shall we go? thou hast the words of eternal life." (John 6:68)

#### John 7 · Rivers of Living Water
At the feast of tabernacles, the autumn feast that remembered Israel's wilderness journey, Jesus' own brothers do not believe in him (John 7:5), and he goes up to Jerusalem quietly, on the Father's time and not theirs. In the temple, the crowd divides over him: a good man, a deceiver, a prophet, the Christ. On the last day of the feast he stands and cries, "If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink." (John 7:37) John explains that he spoke of the Spirit, not yet given because Jesus was not yet glorified (John 7:39). Officers sent to arrest him come back empty-handed, saying, "Never man spake like this man." (John 7:46) Nicodemus asks for a fair hearing (John 7:50–51). The chapter's last verse begins the account of the woman taken in adultery, which the King James Version prints in full (see the Council's Hard Questions).

#### John 8 · The Light of the World
A woman taken in adultery is brought to Jesus as a trap; he sends her accusers away one by one and says to her, "Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more." (John 8:11) Then he declares, "I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life." (John 8:12) The long debate that follows is the sharpest in the book. Jesus warns, "if ye believe not that I am he, ye shall die in your sins" (John 8:24), promises that "the truth shall make you free" (John 8:32), and tells those who boast of Abraham that they do the works of another father (John 8:44). It ends with the plainest claim to deity in the Gospels, "Before Abraham was, I am." (John 8:58), and the crowd takes up stones.

#### John 9 · Once Blind, Now Seeing
The disciples ask whose sin caused a man's blindness from birth; Jesus answers that it was so that the works of God should be made manifest in him (John 9:3). "As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world." (John 9:5) He makes clay, sends the man to wash in Siloam, and the man comes seeing. What follows is a trial in which the healed man grows in sight while his judges grow blinder: he calls Jesus a man, then a prophet, then one from God, and at last worships him as the Son of God (John 9:11, 17, 33, 38). His testimony is the simplest in the Bible: "one thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see" (John 9:25). He is cast out, and Jesus finds him. Jesus ends with a warning to those who say 'We see' (John 9:41).

#### John 10 · The Good Shepherd
Following straight on from John 9, where the leaders threw out a sheep, Jesus speaks of the true shepherd and the thieves. He is the door of the sheep, and he is the good shepherd: "I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep." (John 10:11) He lays down his life of himself and takes it again (John 10:18). At the feast of dedication in winter (John 10:22), he answers the demand to say plainly whether he is the Christ: "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me" (John 10:27), and "they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand" (John 10:28). Then, "I and my Father are one." (John 10:30), and again they take up stones. He withdraws beyond Jordan, and many believe there (John 10:42).

#### John 11 · The Resurrection and the Life
Lazarus of Bethany, whom Jesus loves, falls sick, and Jesus waits two days before going, for the glory of God (John 11:4–6). When he arrives, Lazarus has been in the grave four days. To Martha he says, "I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live" (John 11:25). At the grave, with Mary and the mourners, "Jesus wept." (John 11:35) Then he cries with a loud voice, "Lazarus, come forth." (John 11:43), and the dead man comes out. This last and greatest sign of the ministry moves the council to plot his death, and the high priest Caiaphas unwittingly prophesies that Jesus should die for the nation, and not for that nation only (John 11:50–52).

#### John 12 · The Hour Is Come
Six days before the Passover, Mary anoints Jesus' feet with costly ointment, for his burial (John 12:7). The next day he enters Jerusalem on a young ass, as Zechariah foretold, and the crowd cries Hosanna. When some Greeks ask to see him, Jesus says, "The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified." (John 12:23) The glory is a death: "Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit." (John 12:24) He is troubled, prays that the Father's name be glorified, and a voice answers from heaven. "And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me." (John 12:32) John closes the public ministry with a sober verdict: "though he had done so many miracles before them, yet they believed not on him" (John 12:37), as Isaiah had foretold, and with Jesus' last public appeal (John 12:44–50).

#### John 13 · Having Loved His Own
The second half of the book begins with the hour: "when Jesus knew that his hour was come that he should depart out of this world unto the Father, having loved his own which were in the world, he loved them unto the end." (John 13:1) Knowing all things, Jesus rises from supper and washes his disciples' feet, including the feet of the one who will betray him. Peter protests and is told that unless Jesus washes him he has no part with him (John 13:8). Jesus names the betrayer, and Judas goes out: "and it was night" (John 13:30). With Judas gone, Jesus speaks of his glory and gives the new commandment: "as I have loved you, that ye also love one another" (John 13:34). He foretells Peter's threefold denial before the cock crows (John 13:38).

#### John 14 · The Way, the Truth, and the Life
"Let not your heart be troubled: ye believe in God, believe also in me." (John 14:1) Jesus goes to prepare a place in his Father's house and will come again to receive his own. When Thomas asks the way, Jesus answers, "I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me." (John 14:6) When Philip asks to see the Father, Jesus says, "he that hath seen me hath seen the Father" (John 14:9). He promises "another Comforter" (John 14:16), the Spirit of truth, who will dwell in them, teach them, and bring his words to their remembrance (John 14:26). Love for him is shown in keeping his commandments (John 14:15, 21), and he leaves them his peace (John 14:27).

#### John 15 · Abide in Me
"I am the true vine, and my Father is the husbandman." (John 15:1) The branches bear fruit only by abiding in the vine: "for without me ye can do nothing" (John 15:5). Abiding in him means his words abiding in us, prayer, obedience, and continuing in his love, with the Father's pruning for more fruit (John 15:2–10). Jesus calls his disciples friends and defines the love he commands by his own: "Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends." (John 15:13) They did not choose him; he chose them to bear lasting fruit (John 15:16). The world will hate them as it hated him, but the Spirit of truth will testify of him, and so will they (John 15:26–27).

#### John 16 · Sorrow Turned into Joy
Jesus warns of persecution, even to death, so that the disciples will not be offended when it comes (John 16:1–4). Then he tells them something hard to believe: "It is expedient for you that I go away" (John 16:7), because then the Comforter will come. The Spirit will reprove the world of sin, of righteousness, and of judgment, and will guide the disciples into all truth, glorifying Christ (John 16:8–14). In a little while they will not see him, and then they will; their grief will be like a woman's labour, "your sorrow shall be turned into joy" (John 16:20). The chapter ends with honesty and victory: they will be scattered, "In the world ye shall have tribulation: but be of good cheer; I have overcome the world." (John 16:33)

#### John 17 · The High Priestly Prayer
Jesus lifts up his eyes to heaven: "Father, the hour is come; glorify thy Son, that thy Son also may glorify thee" (John 17:1). He defines the life he gives: "And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent." (John 17:3) He reports his finished work and asks for the glory he had with the Father before the world was (John 17:4–5). He prays for the disciples the Father gave him: that they be kept from the evil, sanctified, and sent; "Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth." (John 17:17) Then he prays for all who will believe through their word, "That they all may be one" (John 17:21), and that they may be with him to behold his glory (John 17:24).

#### John 18 · Whom Seek Ye?
In the garden, Jesus goes out to meet the band that comes to arrest him: "Whom seek ye?" (John 18:4) When he says "I am he" (John 18:5), they go backward and fall to the ground (John 18:6). He protects his disciples, rebukes Peter's sword, and goes willingly: "the cup which my Father hath given me, shall I not drink it?" (John 18:11) He is questioned before Annas, while in the courtyard Peter denies him three times. Before Pilate he speaks of his kingdom: "My kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36), and "Every one that is of the truth heareth my voice." (John 18:37) Pilate asks, "What is truth?" (John 18:38), finds no fault in him, and the crowd asks for Barabbas instead.

#### John 19 · It Is Finished
Pilate has Jesus scourged and brings him out crowned with thorns: "Behold the man!" (John 19:5) The chief priests cry, "We have no king but Caesar." (John 19:15), and Pilate delivers him to be crucified, under a title written in Hebrew, Greek, and Latin (John 19:19–20). John records the dividing of his garments as Psalm 22 foretold (John 19:24), Jesus giving his mother into the beloved disciple's care (John 19:26–27), and his cry of thirst (John 19:28). Then, "When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost." (John 19:30) His legs are not broken and his side is pierced, and John insists on his own eyewitness testimony (John 19:35–37). Joseph of Arimathaea and Nicodemus bury him in a new tomb.

#### John 20 · That Ye Might Believe
On the first day of the week, Mary Magdalene finds the stone taken away; Peter and the beloved disciple run to the tomb and see the grave clothes lying in order, and the beloved disciple believes (John 20:8). Jesus meets Mary in the garden and calls her by name (John 20:16). That evening he stands among the fearful disciples, shows them his hands and side, sends them as the Father sent him, and breathes on them: "Receive ye the Holy Ghost" (John 20:22). A week later Thomas, who had doubted, confesses, "My Lord and my God." (John 20:28) Jesus blesses those "that have not seen, and yet have believed" (John 20:29), and John states the purpose of his book (John 20:30–31).

#### John 21 · Lovest Thou Me?
By the sea of Tiberias, seven disciples fish all night and catch nothing. At dawn a stranger on the shore tells them to cast the net on the right side, and the catch is so great they cannot draw it in (John 21:6). The beloved disciple says, "It is the Lord" (John 21:7), and Jesus feeds them breakfast by a fire of coals. Then, three times, he asks Peter, who had denied him three times: "Simon, son of Jonas, lovest thou me?" (John 21:16) Each time he gives him a charge: feed my lambs, feed my sheep. He foretells Peter's death and says, "Follow me." (John 21:19) When Peter asks about the beloved disciple, Jesus answers, "what is that to thee? follow thou me" (John 21:22). The book closes with the witness of that disciple, and with the many other things Jesus did that the world itself could not contain in books (John 21:25).

### 🧨 Cultural Landmines and Misconceptions
**'The Jews' in John.** A modern reader can hear John's frequent phrase 'the Jews' as a charge against the Jewish people as a whole, and that misreading has fed terrible sins in church history. In John the phrase has several senses. Often it means the Judean religious authorities who opposed Jesus: the man's parents, themselves Jews, "feared the Jews" (John 9:22). Sometimes it means Judeans as opposed to Galileans, and sometimes it is neutral or positive: "many of the Jews... believed on him" (John 11:45), and Jesus himself says, "salvation is of the Jews" (John 4:22). Jesus, his mother, the beloved disciple, and all the first believers were Jews. Teach these chapters without a trace of contempt, and remember that the unbelief John exposes is the unbelief of every human heart, including ours.

**'The world'.** In John the world can mean the created order (John 1:10), humanity as the object of God's love (John 3:16), or humanity organised against God (John 15:18). Read each use in its context; 'God so loved the world' does not mean God approves of the world's ways, and 'love not the world' elsewhere does not contradict God's love for it.

**'Miracles' and 'signs'.** The King James Version often translates John's word for sign as *miracles* (John 2:11, 23; 12:37). John's word points beyond the wonder to its meaning: each sign is a window onto who Jesus is. That is why John reports some signs with a discourse attached (the bread in chapter 6, the light in chapter 9, the resurrection in chapter 11).

**'Verily, verily'.** This double 'amen' at the start of a saying is unique to John among the Gospels. Jesus uses it to introduce a solemn truth on his own authority. The prophets said 'Thus saith the LORD'; Jesus says 'Verily, verily, I say unto you'.

**The hours of the day.** John counts some hours in ways that are debated (the 'tenth hour' of John 1:39, the 'sixth hour' of John 4:6 and John 19:14). Some think he used the Jewish reckoning from sunrise, others a Roman reckoning from midnight for some passages. Do not build doctrine on a clock time, and do not treat a difficulty in reckoning as a contradiction in the Gospels.

**The feasts.** John's feasts are not background decoration. Jesus is the Passover Lamb (John 1:29; 19:36), the living water and the light at Tabernacles (John 7:37; 8:12), and the one the Father sanctified at Dedication (John 10:22, 36). Knowing what each feast remembered opens the chapter.

## 🔴 Tier 3 — Expert
### 🏗️ Theological Framework
John's Gospel has shaped Christian doctrine more than almost any other book of the Bible, and it is best read through the Reformed conviction that Scripture interprets Scripture: John's claims are to be set beside the rest of the canon, not played against it, because "the scripture cannot be broken" (John 10:35).

*Revelation.* John's first word about Christ is that he is the Word, God's self-expression, and his last word in the Prologue is that the Son "hath declared him" (John 1:18). The whole Gospel is revelation in a person: to see the Son is to see the Father (John 14:9). This grounds the Reformed doctrine that God is truly known, not exhaustively but truly, in Christ and through his word, and that the apostolic witness, written down, is how later believers come to that knowledge (John 17:20; John 20:31).

*The Trinity.* John supplied much of the language of the church's Trinitarian confession. The Word was *with* God and *was* God (John 1:1): distinct in person, one in being. The Son says "I and my Father are one" (John 10:30) and yet prays to the Father and is sent by him. The Spirit is 'another Comforter' (John 14:16), a person who teaches, testifies, and reproves, sent by the Father in the Son's name (John 14:26) and by the Son from the Father (John 15:26). The Nicene Creed's confession that the Son shares the one being of the Father, and its statement that the Spirit proceeds from the Father, draw directly on John; the Western addition that the Spirit proceeds from the Son as well (the *filioque*), which divides the Eastern and Western churches, is argued from texts like John 15:26 and John 16:7, where the Son sends the Spirit.

*Christology.* John is the Gospel of the incarnation: "the Word was made flesh" (John 1:14). He insists as strongly on the Son's true humanity (weary at the well, John 4:6; weeping at the grave, John 11:35; thirsting on the cross, John 19:28) as on his deity (the 'I am' of John 8:58, and Thomas's confession, "My Lord and my God" (John 20:28)). The Council of Chalcedon (AD 451) summarized the biblical teaching as one person in two natures, neither mixed together nor divided, and John is the single largest biblical source of that confession.

*Salvation.* John holds together what many readers try to pull apart. On one side stands God's sovereign initiative: "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him" (John 6:44); "ye believe not, because ye are not of my sheep" (John 10:26). On the other stands the free and urgent offer: "whosoever believeth in him should not perish" (John 3:16); "If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink" (John 7:37); and real guilt for refusal: "ye will not come to me, that ye might have life" (John 5:40). The Reformed confessions (the Canons of Dort, the Westminster Confession, chapter 10 on effectual calling) receive both as true, holding that God's drawing is the cause of our coming and that our refusal is truly our own. Other traditions, notably the Arminian and Wesleyan, read the drawing as a grace given to all that can be resisted; they are stated fairly in the lesson on John 6.

*Eschatology.* John teaches that eternal life begins now, "hath everlasting life, and... is passed from death unto life" (John 5:24), and that the resurrection and judgment are still to come "at the last day" (John 6:40), when all that are in the graves shall hear his voice (John 5:28–29). Both belong together: the life given now is the life of the age to come, received in advance and completed at the resurrection.

*Covenant and fulfilment.* The feasts, the temple, the manna, the water from the rock, the brass serpent, and the Passover lamb all find their meaning in Jesus. John's pattern is not the replacement of Israel's Scriptures but their fulfilment: "the law was given by Moses, but grace and truth came by Jesus Christ" (John 1:17), and Moses himself "wrote of me" (John 5:46).

### 📚 Scholarly Insights
- **Irenaeus of Lyons**, *Against Heresies* (book 3): names John, the disciple who leaned on the Lord's breast, as the author, writing at Ephesus, and uses the Gospel against the Gnostic teachers of his day, arguing that the Word who made all things is the same Word who became flesh. His testimony matters because he knew Polycarp, who was said to have known John (verify the details of the Polycarp connection in book 3 and in his letter to Florinus as preserved by Eusebius).
- **Eusebius of Caesarea**, *Ecclesiastical History* (books 3 and 6): preserves Clement of Alexandria's description of John as a 'spiritual Gospel' written last, and preserves Papias's reference to 'John the Elder' alongside the apostles, the fragment from which later scholars have argued for a second John (verify the book and chapter numbers).
- **Augustine**, *Tractates on the Gospel of John*: a long series of sermons preached through the whole Gospel. Augustine reads John as the Gospel that rises highest to contemplate the divinity of the Word, and in his *Harmony of the Gospels* he connects John with the eagle among the four living creatures of Revelation 4:7, a symbol the church has long used for this Gospel (verify the location of the eagle comparison).
- **John Calvin**, *Commentary on the Gospel According to John*: in his preface ('The Argument') he says the other Gospels set out Christ's body, as it were, while John sets out his soul, and that he is accustomed to call John the key that opens the door to the others (verify the exact wording). Calvin reads the book as teaching above all the power and fruit of Christ's coming.
- **J. C. Ryle**, *Expository Thoughts on the Gospels* (John, three volumes): a verse-by-verse devotional exposition for families and ordinary readers, pressing the practical lessons of each passage for heart and life. Useful for the Asks of each walkthrough.
- **B. F. Westcott**, *The Gospel According to St. John* (1880): in his introduction argues from the Gospel's own evidence in narrowing circles that the author was a Jew, a Jew of Palestine, an eyewitness, an apostle, and finally John the son of Zebedee. The argument is still the classic form of the case for the traditional authorship.
- **Leon Morris**, *The Gospel According to John* (New International Commentary on the New Testament) and **D. A. Carson**, *The Gospel According to John* (Pillar New Testament Commentary, 1991): both defend the traditional authorship and the historical reliability of John. Carson argues that the book's primary purpose is evangelistic, reading John 20:31 as answering the question 'who is the Christ?' with 'Jesus' (verify; he develops this in a 1987 article in the *Journal of Biblical Literature*).
- **Raymond E. Brown**, *The Gospel According to John* (Anchor Bible, two volumes): a Roman Catholic scholar who proposed that the Gospel grew in stages within a community gathered around the beloved disciple; he is the most influential modern advocate of a developmental account of the book's composition (verify details of his later revisions).
- **Richard Bauckham**, *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* (2006): argues that the Gospels rest on named eyewitness testimony and that the beloved disciple was the real author of the Fourth Gospel and an eyewitness, but identifies him with John the Elder rather than the son of Zebedee.

### ⚔️ Debate Corner
**The question:** For whom did John write, and what was his purpose? Was the Gospel written chiefly to bring unbelievers to faith, or chiefly to strengthen believers in the faith they already had?

**A textual note first.** John writes "that ye might believe" (John 20:31). In the Textus Receptus behind the King James Version, the verb is an aorist subjunctive (see the Deep Dive below). Some important early manuscripts read a present subjunctive, which some take to mean 'that ye may go on believing'. The King James text is taught here as printed; the point is that the purpose of the book is debated partly because of this small difference, though the grammar alone cannot settle it.

**Position 1 · Written chiefly to bring unbelievers to faith.** D. A. Carson and others argue that John is an evangelistic book, aimed especially at Jews and Gentile converts to Judaism in the synagogues of the Greek-speaking world. They point to John's explanations of Jewish terms for outsiders, his constant appeals to Scripture and to witnesses, the summons to believe in nearly every chapter, and the book's purpose statement read as answering the question 'Who is the Christ?' The strength of this view is that it takes John's repeated invitations at face value.

**Position 2 · Written chiefly to strengthen believers.** Many scholars (Raymond Brown among them, verify) hold that John wrote for Christian communities facing expulsion from the synagogue (John 9:22; 16:2), false teaching, and discouragement, to confirm them in a faith they already held. They point to the long farewell discourses addressed to disciples alone (John 13–17), to the prayer for 'them also which shall believe' (John 17:20), and to the pastoral concern of the closely related First Epistle, written "unto you that believe on the name of the Son of God; that ye may know that ye have eternal life" (1 John 5:13).

**Position 3 · Written to beget faith and to sustain it.** A third view, held by many Reformed and evangelical interpreters, says the choice is a false one. The same word that brings a person to faith keeps that person believing.

**Most consistent with Reformed hermeneutics.** Scripture interprets Scripture, and the rest of the New Testament treats the preached and written word as the means by which God both begets faith and nourishes it: "faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God" (Romans 10:17), and believers are "born again... by the word of God" (1 Peter 1:23) and are then to "desire the sincere milk of the word, that ye may grow thereby" (1 Peter 2:2). John's own narrative shows the same pattern: the disciples believe at Cana (John 2:11), believe again at the empty tomb (John 20:8), and are still being called to believe in the upper room (John 14:1). And John uses the same purpose phrase, 'that ye might believe', of the eyewitness testimony at the cross (John 19:35), which addresses every reader. Position 3 is therefore the reading this study holds: the Gospel has a real evangelistic edge (it calls every reader to believe and live), and it is equally a book for the church's feeding and assurance. That is why the course can both be taught to seasoned believers and be offered to a seeker.

**Pastoral Warning:** A church that reads John only as an evangelistic tract will skim its deepest chapters and leave believers undernourished; a church that reads it only as a book for insiders will forget that its every page calls the unbelieving reader to come to Christ, and it will stop inviting. Both mistakes are costly. Do not let the debate become a reason to hold back the invitation from the seeker in the room, or the assurance from the struggling believer.

### 🔤 Original Language Deep Dive
Every definition below comes from one source: the brief Greek and Hebrew lexicon published by STEPBible.org (Tyndale House, Cambridge), whose Greek entries are drawn largely from G. Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament*. It is called 'the STEPBible lexicon' below.

**Believe (a verb, never a noun).** John's purpose clause uses the verb twice: *pisteusēte* (G4100, John 20:31, V-AAS-2P), an aorist subjunctive, 'that ye might believe', and *pisteuontes* (G4100, John 20:31, V-PAP-NPM), a present participle, 'believing'. The Greek verb occurs 100 times in the Textus Receptus of John, against 11 times in Matthew. In the King James Version, words beginning 'believ' occur 101 times in John, 11 times in Matthew, 17 times in Mark, and 11 times in Luke. By contrast, the English noun 'faith' never occurs in the King James text of John. John speaks of faith as an activity, a coming and receiving and trusting, not as a possession. The STEPBible lexicon defines the verb as 'to have faith (in), to believe', and notes that the New Testament also uses it with prepositions and that the significance of the various constructions has been much discussed. John's favourite construction is believing *into* or *on* a person (John 2:11; 3:16), and the lexicon's own caution is worth keeping: do not make each preposition carry more weight than its context gives it. On the variant reading, see the Debate Corner.

**Sign.** John's word for the works he selects is *sēmeia* (G4592, John 20:30, N-APN), 'signs'. The STEPBible lexicon notes that in the Greek Old Testament it chiefly renders the Hebrew word for a sign, and defines it as a sign, mark, or token, including 'miracles and wonders... regarded as signs of a divine authority'. The Greek word occurs 17 times in the Textus Receptus of John; the King James Version often renders it 'miracles', a word that occurs 9 times in the King James text of John. A sign in John is a miracle with a message, a deed that means something about the doer.

**Life.** The goal of believing is *zōēn* (G2222, John 20:31, N-ASF), 'life'. The STEPBible lexicon distinguishes natural life from 'the life of the kingdom of God, the present life of grace and the life of glory which is to follow', and cites John 6:51 and 53 under that second sense. That definition fits John exactly: eternal life is present now (John 5:24) and completed at the resurrection (John 6:40). The English word 'life' occurs 44 times in the King James text of John.

**I am.** In John 8:58 Jesus sets two verbs against each other: before Abraham *genesthai* (G1096, John 8:58, V-2ADN), 'came to be', an aorist infinitive of a verb of becoming, and then *egō* (G1473, John 8:58, P-1NS) *eimi* (G1510, John 8:58, V-PAI-1S), 'I am', a present with the emphatic pronoun. Abraham came into existence; Jesus simply is. The hearers understood it as a claim to the divine name, for they took up stones (John 8:59). Behind it stands God's name in Exodus 3:14, 'I AM THAT I AM', built on the Hebrew verb *hayah* (H1961), 'to be', and the repeated divine 'I am he' of Isaiah (Isaiah 43:10). The Greek Old Testament renders both passages with this same pair of words, which is part of why readers hear the echo (a mainstream observation, though the extent of John's dependence on the Greek Old Testament is debated).

**Witness.** John the Baptist "came for a witness, to bear witness of the Light" (John 1:7): the noun *marturian* (G3141, John 1:7, N-ASF) and the verb *marturēsē* (G3140, John 1:7, V-AAS-3S). The STEPBible lexicon defines the verb 'to be a witness, bear witness, testify', and cites John for several of its constructions. The King James Version renders the family both 'witness' (21 times in John) and 'record' (10 times in John, as in "his record is true" (John 19:35)). The verb occurs 33 times and the noun 13 times in the Textus Receptus of John. John builds his Gospel like a courtroom: witnesses are called, testimony is weighed, and the reader is the jury.

**Abide.** *Meinate* (G3306, John 15:4, V-AAM-2P), 'abide', is the aorist imperative of a verb the STEPBible lexicon defines as 'to stay, abide, remain', listing its use of the Spirit remaining on Jesus (John 1:32–33), of Christ in believers and believers in Christ (John 6:56; 15:4). The same Greek verb stands behind the King James 'abode' (John 1:39), 'continue' (John 8:31), and 'tarry' (John 21:22), and occurs 43 times in the Textus Receptus of John, while words beginning 'abide' occur 16 times in the King James text. The English reader therefore sees less of the thread than the Greek reader does: the first disciples ask where Jesus abides (John 1:38–39), and in the upper room he tells them to abide in him.

**Glory.** "We beheld his glory" (John 1:14): *doxan* (G1391, John 1:14, N-ASF). The STEPBible lexicon notes that in the Greek Old Testament this word very frequently renders the Hebrew *kavod* (H3519), the glory of God's presence, and that in the New Testament it always means good opinion, honour, glory. It cites John 5:44 and John 12:43 for the glory people seek from one another, which is exactly the contrast John draws: the leaders "loved the praise of men more than the praise of God" (John 12:43), while the Son sought only his Father's glory. In the King James text of John, 'glory' occurs 12 times and words beginning 'glorif' 21 times; the hour of the cross is the hour when the Son is glorified (John 12:23).

**Verily, verily.** The double *amēn* (G281, John 8:58, INJ-HEB) is a Hebrew word carried into Greek; the STEPBible lexicon notes its Hebrew origin in a verb meaning to be firm. In the Gospels, only John records Jesus doubling it; the phrase 'verily, verily' occurs 25 times in the King James text of John.

### 🚷 Preaching Mistakes to Avoid
- **Information without life:** teaching the structure of John, its feasts, its seven signs, and its Greek words as a course in religious literature, without ever pressing the question John asks: do you believe, and have you life in his name?
- **Heat without light:** using John's hard words to 'the Jews' or to the Pharisees as ammunition against other people, other churches, or the Jewish people, with no mirror held up to ourselves; the blindness of John 9:41 is a warning to the religious, which means us.
- **Flattening or pitting the Gospels:** either forcing John's material into the pattern of the other three, or setting them against one another as if they told different stories. Let each witness speak, and let them stand together.
- **Allegory without restraint:** finding hidden meanings in every number (the six waterpots, the thirty-eight years, the hundred and fifty and three fishes) and building doctrine on them. Say what John says; mark a suggestion as a suggestion.
- **Believing without abiding:** preaching faith as a single decision with no call to continue in his word, or preaching abiding as a technique for spiritual success. John joins them: those who believe are those who come, eat, abide, and follow.
- **Mysticism without the flesh:** presenting John as a 'spiritual Gospel' about inner light, forgetting that its central claim is that the Word became flesh, died in the flesh, and rose in the body with wounds that could be touched (John 20:27).

## 🟣 Tier 4 — Christocentric, Humility, Life & Light
### 👑 Every Answer in Jesus
John's Gospel leaves no room for self-glory. Every human being in it is exposed as needing Jesus. The religious teacher cannot enter the kingdom without a new birth he cannot give himself (John 3:3). The woman at the well cannot quench her own thirst (John 4:13). The man at Bethesda has no one to put him in the pool (John 5:7). The crowd wants bread but not the Bread (John 6:26). His own brothers do not believe (John 7:5). The leaders who say 'We see' are blind (John 9:41). Martha and Mary cannot bring back their brother (John 11:21, 32). The disciples, even the best of them, scatter and deny (John 16:32; 18:17, 25, 27). Pilate cannot recognize the truth standing before him (John 18:38). No one in the book gives himself life or light; no one earns the right to see God.

And every good thing the book holds is found in Jesus. The life that was in the beginning is in him (John 1:4). The glory of God is seen in him (John 1:14). The Father is seen in him (John 14:9). The bread, the water, the light, the door, the shepherd, the resurrection, the way, the truth, the vine: every 'I am' names something we need and cannot supply, and says that he is it. This is what Paul means when he says of Christ, "In whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." (Colossians 2:3) The Baptist's words are the posture of the whole book, and of this course: "He must increase, but I must decrease." (John 3:30) We bring our emptiness, and he is glorified in filling it.

## 📚 Doctrine
### Theology Proper · The One True God Who Sends His Son and Is Known in Him
**Proposition:** There is one living and true God, who is Spirit, holy, and loving, and who is savingly known only as the Father who sends his Son into the world; to know him in this way is eternal life.

**Textual Anchor:** "God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth." (John 4:24) "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son" (John 3:16). "And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent." (John 17:3) The Father is always at work: "My Father worketh hitherto, and I work." (John 5:17)

**Cross-References:** "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD:" (Deuteronomy 6:4); "In this was manifested the love of God toward us, because that God sent his only begotten Son into the world, that we might live through him." (1 John 4:9); "neither knoweth any man the Father, save the Son, and he to whomsoever the Son will reveal him" (Matthew 11:27)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *God can be truly known without Christ.* Its holders say: 'Every sincere religion is a path to the same God, and Jesus is one great teacher among many.' John answers that no one has seen God at any time except as the Son declares him (John 1:18), and that no one comes to the Father except by him (John 14:6). Sincerity is not the issue; the Father has chosen where he is to be known. (2) *God is distant and inactive.* Deism, ancient and modern, holds that God made the world and leaves it to run. John's God so loves the world that he gives his Son, and the Father works until now (John 5:17). (3) *The God of love cannot be a God of wrath.* Some hold that love rules out judgment. John sets both in one paragraph: God so loved the world, and for the one who does not believe the Son, "the wrath of God abideth on him" (John 3:36). His love is the love of a holy God.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Worship directed to the Father through the Son, in spirit and in truth. Confidence in prayer, because the Father himself loves those who love his Son (John 16:27). And an evangelism that is both humble and bold: humble because we did not find God by ourselves, bold because he can be known.

### Christology · The Word Who Was God Became Flesh
**Proposition:** Jesus of Nazareth is the eternal Word, who was with God and was God, through whom all things were made, and who became true man without ceasing to be true God: one person, fully divine and fully human, the only begotten Son who reveals the Father.

**Textual Anchor:** "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1) "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). "Before Abraham was, I am." (John 8:58) "And Thomas answered and said unto him, My Lord and my God." (John 20:28) And he was "wearied with his journey" (John 4:6) and "Jesus wept." (John 11:35)

**Cross-References:** "Who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God:" (Philippians 2:6); "For in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily." (Colossians 2:9); "and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God" (Isaiah 9:6)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The Word is the first and highest creature.* Arius in the fourth century, and the Watchtower Society today (whose translation renders John 1:1 'a god'), hold that the Son is a created being, divine in rank but not in nature. John answers that "All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made." (John 1:3): the Word is on the Creator's side of the line. Thomas calls him Lord and God, and Jesus accepts it (John 20:28–29). (2) *Jesus only seemed to be human.* Docetic and Gnostic teachers in the early church held that the divine Christ could not truly suffer, so his body was an appearance. John answers that the Word became flesh, wept, thirsted, and bled (John 19:34), and the First Epistle tests every spirit by whether it confesses that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh (1 John 4:2). (3) *The Father and the Son are one person in two modes.* Modalism holds that God is one person who appears now as Father, now as Son. John answers that the Word was *with* God (John 1:1) and that the Son prays to the Father for the glory he had *with* him before the world was (John 17:5).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Worship of Jesus as God, without embarrassment. Comfort, because our Saviour knows weariness and grief from the inside. And confidence that when we see Jesus in the Gospel, we see what God is like (John 14:9).

### Christology · The Lamb Lifted Up: His Finished Work and Bodily Resurrection
**Proposition:** Jesus laid down his life of his own will, as the Lamb of God and the good shepherd, dying in the place of sinners to take away the sin of the world; he finished the work the Father gave him on the cross, and he rose bodily from the dead on the third day.

**Textual Anchor:** "Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world." (John 1:29) "I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep." (John 10:11) "I lay it down of myself" (John 10:18). "It is finished" (John 19:30). And to Thomas: "Reach hither thy finger, and behold my hands" (John 20:27).

**Cross-References:** "But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed." (Isaiah 53:5); "Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree" (1 Peter 2:24); "Who was delivered for our offences, and was raised again for our justification." (Romans 4:25)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The cross is only an example of love.* Holders of the moral influence view, associated with Peter Abelard in the Middle Ages (verify how far Abelard himself went) and common in modern liberal theology, say Jesus' death saves by moving us to love God, not by bearing anything in our place. John does show the cross as love (John 15:13), but he also calls Jesus the Lamb who takes away sin (John 1:29) and records the high priest's unwitting prophecy that one man should die for the people (John 11:50–52): the death is *for* others, in their place. (2) *The work must be completed or repeated.* Some systems add human merit to Christ's work, or re-present his sacrifice as though it needed renewing. Jesus' own word from the cross is a perfect tense of completion (see the lesson on John 19): the work is finished. (3) *The resurrection was spiritual, not bodily.* Some say only Jesus' cause or influence lived on. John records an empty tomb with grave clothes lying in order (John 20:6–7), wounds that could be touched (John 20:27), and a breakfast cooked on the shore (John 21:9–13).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Assurance, because the payment is finished and the Lamb has taken the sin away. Love for Christ, because he laid down his life of his own will. And hope, because the same body that died was raised.

### Pneumatology · The Spirit Who Gives New Birth and Bears Witness to Christ
**Proposition:** The Holy Spirit is a divine person, another Comforter sent by the Father and the Son, who gives new birth sovereignly, dwells in believers, teaches and reminds them of Christ's words, reproves the world, and always glorifies Christ.

**Textual Anchor:** "Except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God." (John 3:5) "The wind bloweth where it listeth" (John 3:8). "But the Comforter, which is the Holy Ghost, whom the Father will send in my name, he shall teach you all things, and bring all things to your remembrance, whatsoever I have said unto you." (John 14:26) "He shall glorify me" (John 16:14).

**Cross-References:** "A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you" (Ezekiel 36:26); "Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost;" (Titus 3:5); "and that no man can say that Jesus is the Lord, but by the Holy Ghost" (1 Corinthians 12:3)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The Spirit is an impersonal force.* The Watchtower Society and others describe the Spirit as God's active power, not a person. John's Spirit teaches, testifies, hears, speaks, guides, and reproves (John 14:26; 15:26; 16:8, 13), and he is 'another Comforter', one like Jesus (John 14:16). (2) *The Spirit brings revelation beyond or apart from Christ.* Some movements claim new words of the Spirit that add to or correct Christ. Jesus says the Spirit "shall not speak of himself" (John 16:13) and will take what is Christ's and show it (John 16:13–14). A spirit who draws attention away from Christ is not this Spirit. (3) *New birth is produced by a decision or a rite.* Some treat regeneration as the result of a human choice, others as conveyed by baptism of itself. Jesus compares the Spirit to the wind, which blows where it wills (John 3:8), and John says the children of God are born "not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God" (John 1:13).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Humility, because new life is received, not achieved. Dependence on the Spirit in reading Scripture, praying for him to bring Christ's words to mind. And discernment: every teaching is tested by whether it glorifies Christ.

### Anthropology & Sin · Dead, Blind, and Enslaved, Loving the Darkness
**Proposition:** Every human being, apart from Christ, is spiritually dead, blind, and enslaved to sin, not merely ignorant but unwilling, loving darkness rather than light, and unable to come to Christ unless the Father draws him; yet each remains fully responsible for refusing him.

**Textual Anchor:** "And this is the condemnation, that light is come into the world, and men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil." (John 3:19) "Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin." (John 8:34) "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him" (John 6:44). "And ye will not come to me, that ye might have life." (John 5:40)

**Cross-References:** "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it?" (Jeremiah 17:9); "There is none that understandeth, there is none that seeketh after God." (Romans 3:11); "And you hath he quickened, who were dead in trespasses and sins;" (Ephesians 2:1)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *People are able to turn to God by their own natural power.* Pelagius in the fifth century, and many since, have argued that because God commands faith, everyone must be naturally able to believe. John records both the command, "If any man thirst, let him come unto me" (John 7:37), and the inability, "no man can come unto me, except it were given unto him of my Father" (John 6:65). A command shows our duty, not our strength. (2) *Sin is ignorance, cured by teaching.* Ancient Gnostics and modern optimists alike see humanity's problem as a lack of knowledge. Nicodemus was a teacher of Israel and still needed to be born again (John 3:10); the root is that men love darkness "because their deeds were evil" (John 3:19). (3) *Religion cures sin.* The most religious people in John, those who searched the Scriptures, were those who would not come (John 5:39–40) and who said 'We see' (John 9:41).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Humility, because we were no better than the blind Pharisees. Compassion for unbelievers, who are enslaved and need rescue, not contempt. And prayer in evangelism, because only the Father can draw.

### Soteriology & Grace · Eternal Life Given Freely to All Who Believe
**Proposition:** Eternal life is the free gift of God in Christ, received by believing on him, not earned by works; everyone who believes has it now and will be raised at the last day, and everyone the Father gives to the Son comes to him, is kept by him, and will never perish.

**Textual Anchor:** "But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name:" (John 1:12) "This is the work of God, that ye believe on him whom he hath sent." (John 6:29) "All that the Father giveth me shall come to me; and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out." (John 6:37) "And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand." (John 10:28)

**Cross-References:** "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God:" (Ephesians 2:8); "and whom he called, them he also justified: and whom he justified, them he also glorified" (Romans 8:30); "These things have I written unto you that believe on the name of the Son of God; that ye may know that ye have eternal life" (1 John 5:13)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Salvation is earned by works.* When the crowd asks what works they must do, Jesus answers that the work God requires is to believe on the one he sent (John 6:28–29). Life is given, not earned (John 10:28). (2) *God's drawing is a grace given to everyone, which each person may accept or resist.* This is the Arminian and Wesleyan reading, and its holders appeal to John 12:32, "I... will draw all men unto me" (John 12:32), and to the 'whosoever' of John 3:16. The Reformed reading answers from the context of John 6: the one the Father draws is the one Jesus raises up at the last day (John 6:44), and all whom the Father gives shall come (John 6:37); the 'all men' of John 12:32 is spoken when Greeks have come to see Jesus (John 12:20–21), and is read as all kinds of people, Gentile as well as Jew. Both sides agree that the offer is to whosoever will. (3) *A true believer can be lost.* Some traditions hold that a believer may finally fall away. Jesus says his sheep "shall never perish" (John 10:28), and that of all the Father gave him he should lose nothing (John 6:39). The warnings of John 15:6 are real; the Reformed reading takes them to describe branches that never bore true fruit, not sheep that Christ lets go, and the lesson on John 15 weighs the other readings fairly.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Assurance resting on Christ's promise, not on the strength of our grip. Freedom from the treadmill of earning. And confident evangelism: the invitation is to whosoever will, and the Father will draw his own.

### Ecclesiology · One Flock, Gathered and Sent
**Proposition:** Christ gathers into one flock, under one shepherd, all whom he calls from Israel and the nations; he keeps them in the truth, binds them together in love, and sends them into the world as the Father sent him, to bear witness and to declare forgiveness in his name.

**Textual Anchor:** "and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd" (John 10:16); Jesus died "that also he should gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad" (John 11:52); "That they all may be one" (John 17:21); "as my Father hath sent me, even so send I you" (John 20:21); "By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another." (John 13:35)

**Cross-References:** "There is one body, and one Spirit, even as ye are called in one hope of your calling;" (Ephesians 4:4); "that ye should shew forth the praises of him who hath called you out of darkness into his marvellous light" (1 Peter 2:9); "and ye shall be witnesses unto me both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea, and in Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth" (Acts 1:8)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Faith is private, and the church is optional.* Many today say, 'I follow Jesus, but I don't need the church.' Jesus speaks of one flock, gives a commandment that can only be kept with others (John 13:34), and prays for the visible unity of all believers (John 17:21). (2) *Unity means agreement at any cost, or uniformity under one institution.* Some pursue unity by setting truth aside; others equate it with one visible organization. Jesus prays for unity in the same breath as "Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth." (John 17:17): the unity he asks for is in the Father and the Son and in the truth. (3) *John 20:23 gives a priestly power to absolve.* Roman Catholic teaching reads "Whose soever sins ye remit, they are remitted unto them" (John 20:23) as the institution of the sacrament of penance through ordained priests. The Reformed churches read it as the authority given to the church to declare, in the preaching of the gospel and in discipline, the forgiveness that God grants to all who believe and the guilt that remains on those who refuse (the 'keys'); the lesson on John 20 sets out both views.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Commitment to a local flock. Love for believers of other backgrounds and churches. And a sent life: every member of the group is sent somewhere this week.

### Eschatology · Eternal Life Now, Resurrection at the Last Day
**Proposition:** The life of the age to come begins now for all who believe, and it will be completed when Christ returns, raises the dead, judges all people, and receives his own to be with him where he is.

**Textual Anchor:** "He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life" (John 5:24), and "the hour is coming, in the which all that are in the graves shall hear his voice" (John 5:28). "I will come again, and receive you unto myself; that where I am, there ye may be also." (John 14:3) "I will raise him up at the last day" (John 6:40).

**Cross-References:** "And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt." (Daniel 12:2); "For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first:" (1 Thessalonians 4:16); "but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Everything John promises is already fulfilled.* Some twentieth-century scholars (C. H. Dodd, and in another way Rudolf Bultmann, verify) stressed or reduced John's eschatology to present experience, treating the future sayings as secondary. John holds both: the hour 'now is' and the hour 'is coming' (John 5:25, 28), and the resurrection at the last day is repeated four times in one discourse (John 6:39, 40, 44, 54). (2) *Speculation built on misread words.* John himself corrects a rumor that the beloved disciple would not die: "yet Jesus said not unto him, He shall not die" (John 21:23). Prophecy is to be read as it is spoken, not stretched into dates and schemes. (3) *There is no final judgment.* Some hold that all will be saved in the end. Jesus speaks of a resurrection of life and a resurrection of damnation (John 5:29).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Present joy, because eternal life has already begun. Courage at the graveside, because the dead will hear his voice. And sobriety, because every person will rise to meet him.

### Christian Walk · Abiding, Loving, and Following
**Proposition:** The Christian life is lived by abiding in Christ, drawing all life and fruitfulness from him, and it shows itself in keeping his commandments, loving one another as he has loved us, and following him wherever he leads, without comparing our path to anyone else's.

**Textual Anchor:** "I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing." (John 15:5) "If ye love me, keep my commandments." (John 14:15) "A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you, that ye also love one another." (John 13:34) "what is that to thee? follow thou me" (John 21:22).

**Cross-References:** "I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me" (Galatians 2:20); "He that saith he abideth in him ought himself also so to walk, even as he walked." (1 John 2:6); "As ye have therefore received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk ye in him:" (Colossians 2:6)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The Christian life is self-improvement with God's help.* Much popular religion treats holiness as a project we manage with God as coach. Jesus says the branch bears no fruit of itself (John 15:4–5). (2) *Love needs no commandments.* Some say that if we love, rules do not matter. Jesus binds them together: love keeps his commandments (John 14:15, 21). (3) *My discipleship is measured against others'.* Peter looked at the beloved disciple and asked, 'What shall this man do?' Jesus answered that it was not Peter's concern (John 21:21–22).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Daily dependence on Christ's word and prayer. Obedience that flows from love, not fear. And freedom from comparison: each disciple follows the same Lord on his own path.

### Assumed Here
Every category of doctrine is taught in John, so none is merely assumed. Two things the book assumes rather than narrates are worth noting:
- **Ecclesiology (the sacraments):** John does not narrate the institution of the Lord's Supper or the command to baptize all nations, though he mentions baptism by Jesus' disciples (John 4:1–2) and speaks of eating his flesh and drinking his blood (John 6:53–56); he assumes readers know the church's practice, taught plainly in Matthew 26:26–28, Matthew 28:19, and 1 Corinthians 11:23–26.
- **Theology Proper (creation):** John asserts that all things were made by the Word (John 1:3), and assumes the account of creation in Genesis 1, whose opening words his own first words echo.

## ⚖️ The Council's Hard Questions
### ❓ Question 1 · Who Wrote This Gospel, and Does It Matter
**Moderator:** The book never names its author. The church says John the son of Zebedee; many modern scholars say otherwise. Who wrote it, and would it change anything if tradition were wrong?

**Literary Scholar:** Start with what the text says. The author is "the disciple whom Jesus loved" (John 21:20), who was at the supper, the cross, the tomb, and the lake, and who "wrote these things" (John 21:24). He never names himself. And a striking silence: John the son of Zebedee, prominent in the other Gospels, is never named here, and the Baptist is called simply 'John', as though no other John needed to be distinguished. That fits an author who is John himself and keeps his name out of the story.

**Historian:** The external evidence is early and consistent. Irenaeus, writing around AD 180, names John, the disciple who leaned on the Lord's breast, and places him at Ephesus (*Against Heresies*, book 3). Irenaeus had heard Polycarp as a youth, and Polycarp was said to have known John (verify the details). But Eusebius also preserves Papias, who mentions a 'John the Elder' along with the apostles, and some scholars take that as evidence of a second John who wrote the Gospel.

**Church Historian:** And the modern views follow those lines. Westcott argued in narrowing circles that the author was a Palestinian Jew, an eyewitness, an apostle, and John. Raymond Brown proposed a Gospel shaped in stages within a community gathered around the beloved disciple. Richard Bauckham argues that the beloved disciple was the author and an eyewitness, but identifies him with John the Elder rather than the son of Zebedee. The disagreement is real, and serious scholars stand on each side.

**Theologian:** Here is what matters. The authority of the book does not rest on a name the book does not give; it rests on its place in the canon the Spirit gave to the church, and on the claim the book makes about itself: that its witness is an eyewitness and his testimony true (John 19:35; 21:24). What would change everything is if there were no eyewitness at all. John will not allow that: "That which we have seen and heard declare we unto you" (1 John 1:3).

**Resolution:** The Gospel was written by the beloved disciple, an eyewitness of Jesus' ministry, death, and resurrection (John 19:35; John 21:24). The church's tradition that he is John the son of Zebedee is early, consistent, and fits the internal evidence well, and this study follows it while marking it as tradition; faithful scholars who identify him otherwise still affirm an eyewitness author. What the reader must hold is what the text itself affirms: "we know that his testimony is true" (John 21:24).

### ❓ Question 2 · Is John's Jesus the Same as the Jesus of Matthew, Mark, and Luke
**Moderator:** Readers notice that Jesus sounds different in John: long discourses instead of parables, open claims to deity instead of a guarded 'Son of man'. Some say John's Jesus is a later theological creation. How do we answer?

**Historian:** Begin with the setting. John reports mainly the Judean and Jerusalem ministry, at feasts, in debate with trained teachers, and in private with the disciples on the last night. Different audiences draw out different kinds of speech. And the other Gospels hint at the ministry John records: Jesus' lament, "how often would I have gathered thy children together" (Matthew 23:37), assumes repeated visits to Jerusalem that Matthew does not narrate.

**Philologist:** And the style question is honest. John's narrative and Jesus' speeches in John share a vocabulary: light, life, truth, abide, world. Some explain this as John's own idiom shaping his report, since Jesus probably spoke mostly Aramaic and every Gospel gives his words in Greek; others see John's idiom as learned from Jesus. Either way, faithful reporting of what someone said does not require the same words as another reporter, and the evangelists often give the sense in their own style (a mainstream view, held with varying emphasis).

**Theologian:** The claims themselves are not John's invention. In Matthew, Jesus says that no one knows the Father except the Son and those to whom the Son reveals him (Matthew 11:27), a saying that could sit in John. In Mark, when the high priest asks if he is the Christ, the Son of the Blessed, "Jesus said, I am" (Mark 14:62). In all the Gospels he forgives sins, accepts worship, and claims to judge the world. John makes explicit what the others show.

**Pastor:** For our group, I would rather they meet four witnesses than one blurred picture. Some believers have been unsettled by the claim that the Gospels disagree. They need to see that John writes as an eyewitness who assumes his readers know the other accounts and adds what they left out.

**Resolution:** John's Jesus is the same Jesus as the Synoptics', seen by a different witness, speaking in different settings, and reported with a different emphasis. The high claims of John have clear parallels in the other Gospels (Matthew 11:27; Mark 14:62), and John's own testimony is that he writes what he saw (John 19:35). The four Gospels are four witnesses to one Lord.

### ❓ Question 3 · How Should We Teach the Passages Many Modern Editions Question
**Moderator:** The King James Version prints the angel troubling the water (John 5:3–4) and the woman taken in adultery (John 7:53–8:11), and it reads "the only begotten Son" (John 1:18). Many modern editions bracket or change these. How will this course handle them?

**Philologist:** Let us state the facts calmly. The passage about the woman taken in adultery is absent from many of the oldest Greek manuscripts that survive, and in some later manuscripts it appears in other places; but it is present in a great many manuscripts, in the Latin tradition, and in the Greek text the King James translators used. The last part of John 5:3 and all of John 5:4 are likewise missing from some early manuscripts. At John 1:18, several early manuscripts read 'only begotten God' rather than 'only begotten Son'; both readings present the Son as the one who uniquely makes the Father known, and the first would, if anything, make the verse a stronger statement of his deity.

**Church Historian:** The story of the woman was known and cherished in the church from early times; Augustine discusses it and suggests that some may have removed it for fear it would seem to excuse adultery (verify). The King James translators worked in 1611 from the printed Greek editions of their day, which stand in the tradition now called the Textus Receptus. Christians today differ about how to weigh these witnesses, and on both sides there are people who love the Scriptures and believe them to be God's word.

**Teacher:** The course's rule is simple and settled by the owner's decision: teach the text the King James Version prints, and explain the question at Tier 3, fairly and without scorn. In the lessons on John 5, 7, and 8, the participant pages print these verses as the King James Version prints them, and the Tier 3 sections explain why other editions differ. If a participant raises it from another translation, we honour the question and move on to the text.

**Pastor:** Some members will be unsettled by learning that manuscripts differ at all. They need to hear two things: the differences are few in number compared with the whole, and no doctrine of the faith rests on a disputed passage alone. The mercy of Christ to sinners in John 8, for example, is taught throughout the Gospel (John 3:17; John 4).

**Resolution:** Teach the text as the King James Version prints it (John 1:18; John 5:3–4; John 7:53–8:11). At Tier 3, explain calmly that many modern editions differ and why, without contempt for either side. Remind the group that the word of the Lord is sure, and that every doctrine of the faith is taught in passages that are not in question (John 10:35).

### 🔦 The Life & Light Audit
**Pastor:** If a participant obeyed this course exactly, each week one named person would receive something Christ gives in that chapter: an invitation to come and see, a visit to a grieving friend with the promise of the resurrection, a word of peace to a troubled colleague. And each week one dark place would be lit, starting always with the participant's own heart: a hidden sin confessed, a motive examined, a truth spoken in love.

**Theologian:** Source discipline holds, as long as the practices stay what they are here: carrying what Christ gives, never producing life or light by effort. The Course Outcomes and the Ledger name what Christ did through the believer, not what the believer achieved (John 15:5). The one risk is that the Ledger becomes a scorecard. The closing line under it guards against that, and the How to Use section says so again.

**Teacher:** A new believer can do this. The first Life practice is a verse read and one sentence said to the Lord; the first Light practice is one private honest prayer. The Outward practices name a kind of person (a grieving neighbour, a colleague who does not believe) and leave a blank for a name, so no one is left wondering what to do.

**Fix:** The Audit asked for two changes, and both are in the unit. First, the doing outcome in the Course Outcomes now ties the weekly practice to the Life & Light Ledger, so each lesson ends with a named person and a named place instead of a vague intention. Second, the How to Use section tells members that the naming round reads only the second row of the Light Planner, never the private first row about their own heart, so light is carried in love and no one is exposed.

## 🗝️ Leader's Guide
### ❓ Teacher FAQ
- **What if a participant uses another version of the Bible?** Welcome them and their Bible. The workbook quotes the King James Version throughout, and its questions and Glossary are built on its wording, so ask them to follow the King James text printed in the Read & Mark section during the session and to read their own version alongside it at home. Differences can be illuminating; when they touch a textual question (John 5:3–4; John 7:53–8:11; John 1:18), point them to the Tier 3 section of that lesson and keep the group on the text (John 20:31).
- **What if someone voices a doctrinal misunderstanding in the group?** Thank them for saying what they think; it takes courage. Ask the group, 'What does the verse actually say?' and let the text answer, as Jesus sent his hearers to the Scriptures (John 5:39). If it needs more than a minute, offer to talk afterward. Never shame the person, and never let the error stand unanswered if it touches the gospel itself.
- **What if people do not finish all five levels?** That is normal and expected. Observe and Interpret are the foundation; if the group finishes those well and does the Bring Life and Bring Light planners, the lesson has done its work. Encourage members to finish the rest at home. Jesus himself said, "I have yet many things to say unto you, but ye cannot bear them now" (John 16:12).
- **What if someone hears 'abundant life' as money, health, or success?** Read John 10:10 with John 16:33 and John 21:18–19. The same Jesus who promises abundant life promises tribulation and foretells Peter's death. Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, knowing God (John 17:3), which holds in poverty and suffering.
- **What if someone wants to use the light on other people's sins?** Read John 8:7–11 together. Jesus sent the accusers away before he dealt with the woman, and he dealt with her in mercy and truth. The Light Planner's first row is always the participant's own heart, and the naming round never reads it aloud. Light without love is glare.
- **What if someone has no one to name on the Life Planner?** Ask gentle questions: who did you see this week who seemed tired, lonely, or far from God? A cashier, a neighbour, a relative who has stopped calling? Remind them of Philip, who simply found Nathanael and said 'Come and see' (John 1:45–46). If they truly cannot think of anyone, ask them to pray this week that the Lord will show them one person, and to write the name when he does.
- **Is John anti-Jewish?** No, though it has been misused that way. 'The Jews' in John often means the Judean authorities who opposed Jesus, and sometimes simply Judeans; Jesus and all his first followers were Jews, and Jesus said "salvation is of the Jews" (John 4:22). The unbelief John exposes is the unbelief of the human heart. Teach these passages with sorrow for the church's sins against the Jewish people and with love.
- **Why is John so different from Matthew, Mark, and Luke?** John writes as a fourth witness who knows the others, supplies much of the Judean ministry and the last night's teaching, and chooses signs and discourses to show who Jesus is (John 20:30–31). The differences are those of four witnesses to one Lord, not of rival accounts. See Council Question 2.
- **Why does the workbook print John 5:4 and the woman taken in adultery, when my Bible puts them in brackets?** Because the course teaches the King James text as printed, and explains the textual question calmly at Tier 3 of those lessons. No doctrine rests on these passages alone (John 10:35). See Council Question 3.

### 🛠️ Advanced Teacher Hacks
- **Silent processing:** after asking an Interpret or Apply question, wait seven to ten seconds in silence before anyone answers. The quiet members often give the best answers when they are given time. Jesus let silence do its work, too (John 8:6–8).
- **Group engagement:** before discussing a question, have each member share their Big Idea for the chapter in one sentence; compare them, and let the group see how the text produced them. Draw out the quiet by name, gently, and redirect the talkative by thanking them and asking, 'Who else noticed something?'
- **The cross-reference hack:** when a verse in John quotes or echoes the Old Testament (John 1:29; John 3:14; John 6:31; John 19:36), have one member read the Old Testament passage aloud before discussing John. John expects his readers to hear those echoes.
- **The application trick:** ask the Apply questions in the first person, 'What will *I* do?' rather than 'What should people do?' Then have members write their answer before anyone speaks. James 1:22 is the principle: doers, not hearers only.
- **The doctrinal anchor:** at the end of each lesson, ask the group to state one doctrine the chapter taught, in one sentence, with its verse. Over 21 weeks the group builds a small systematic theology from John, anchored in the text (2 Timothy 1:13).
- **Humbling and exalting:** in every lesson, ask two questions: 'How does this chapter humble me?' and 'How does it make Christ glorious?' The Baptist's words are the key: "He must increase, but I must decrease." (John 3:30)
- **Bring life:** before the naming round, give one minute of silence for members to write a name on the Life Planner. Ask them to choose someone they will actually see this week, and to plan what they will say. The line to say is part of the lesson; rehearse it aloud (John 1:46).
- **Bring light:** model the Light Planner's first row yourself, briefly and appropriately, before asking anyone else to share. A leader who confesses their own need of the light (1 John 1:8–9) gives the group permission to be honest, and keeps the light from turning into accusation.

### 🐑 Shepherding Notes
John's Gospel touches tender places, and a leader should expect them across the course.

**Grief and death.** Lazarus (John 11) and the cross (John 19) will bring up losses in the group. Some will have prayed for someone who was not healed, as Martha said, "Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died" (John 11:21). Do not rush past the tears; Jesus wept before he raised Lazarus. Offer the hope of the resurrection without promising that every grief will be undone now.

**Doubt.** Thomas (John 20) and the disciples who walked away (John 6:66) give permission to name doubts honestly. Treat a doubting member as Jesus treated Thomas: meet the doubt with the evidence of the wounds, and invite belief without scorn.

**Failure and shame.** Peter's denial and restoration (John 18; John 21) will reach members who carry a failure they think disqualifies them. Let them hear that the Lord came to Peter first with breakfast, then with questions, then with a charge.

**Family unbelief.** Jesus' own brothers did not believe in him (John 7:5). Members with unbelieving spouses, parents, or children will find comfort here, and should be encouraged to keep praying and keep loving.

**The Light Planner's first row.** Every week the first row of the Light Planner asks each member to name a dark place in their own heart. This is private. Never ask anyone to read it aloud, and never press. If a member confides something serious (abuse, addiction, thoughts of self-harm), take it seriously, keep appropriate confidence, and help them find further pastoral care and, where needed, professional help and the protection the law requires.

**Religious pride.** The blind Pharisees of John 9 are most dangerous in a Bible study, because Bible study can make us feel that we see. Watch for it in yourself first.

### 🎯 Teaching Angles
**For a Tier 1 Group:** Keep the whole course on one question: who is Jesus, and will you trust him? Each week, read the chapter's memory verse together, use the Tier 1 Core Concept and Analogy, and let the Observe questions do the work. Use John's own storytelling: the characters (Nicodemus, the woman at the well, the man born blind, Martha, Thomas, Peter) each meet Jesus and each responds. Ask, 'Who are you most like in this chapter?'

**For a Tier 2 Group:** Follow the threads across the course. Keep a chart on the wall of the seven signs, the 'I am' sayings, the feasts, and the hour, and add to it each week. Use the Walkthrough to show how each chapter builds on the last, and the Doctrine section to state what it teaches. Assign a member each week to report on one Tier 3 insight, and use the Council's Hard Questions for the last ten minutes when the group is ready for them.

**Tough Question 1:** *If John says 'whosoever believeth', how can he also say that no one can come unless the Father draws him?* Both are taught side by side in John 6: the Father draws (John 6:44), and whoever comes is never cast out (John 6:37). Scripture holds both God's sovereign grace and our real responsibility, and so should we. The drawing is not an obstacle to anyone who wants to come; it is the reason anyone wants to. Take the full question up in Lesson 6.

**Tough Question 2:** *Why does John leave out so much that the other Gospels include, like the birth of Jesus and the Lord's Supper?* Because he tells us he selected (John 20:30; John 21:25), writing after the others and assuming their accounts. His Prologue gives the eternal origin behind the birth in Bethlehem (John 1:1, 14), and his chapter on the bread of life (John 6) and the upper room (John 13–17) give the meaning behind the Supper.

**Tough Question 3:** *Does 'eternal life' mean living forever after we die, or something now?* Both, and John insists on both. Eternal life is knowing God and Jesus Christ (John 17:3), which begins the moment one believes (John 5:24), and it will be completed at the resurrection at the last day (John 6:40). It is the life of the age to come, begun now.

### 🌀 Going Deeper
For the weeks after the course:
- **Life:** read the First Epistle of John, which begins with the Word of life and ends with "He that hath the Son hath life" (1 John 5:12); and Romans 6:1–11, on being raised to walk in newness of life.
- **Light:** read 1 John 1:5–2:2, on walking in the light, and Psalm 119:105–112, on the word as a lamp; and Ephesians 5:8–14, on walking as children of light.
- **Sound doctrine:** read Colossians 1:15–20 and Hebrews 1:1–4 alongside John 1:1–18, to see how the apostles speak of the Son; and memorize 2 Timothy 1:13, holding fast the form of sound words.
- **The Old Testament John echoes:** Exodus 12 (the Passover lamb), Numbers 21:4–9 (the brass serpent), Isaiah 53 (the suffering Servant), Ezekiel 34 (the shepherds of Israel), and Psalm 23.
- **John's other writing:** read Revelation 1:9–18, where the same Lord who walked by the sea of Galilee appears in glory and says, "I am he that liveth, and was dead; and, behold, I am alive for evermore" (Revelation 1:18).
