# John 12 · The Hour Is Come (KJV)
> Unit john-12 · John 12:1–50 · loop john-kjv-study v2.0 · DRAFT for human doctrine review

## 🧭 At a Glance
### 🧵 Golden Thread
The hour of glory arrives: the King enters Jerusalem, the corn of wheat must die to bear fruit, and the lifted-up Son draws all men while unbelief fulfils Isaiah.

### 🌗 Life & Light Line
John 12 answers the death that hides inside a life kept for oneself: 'it abideth alone' (John 12:24), the grain that will not fall into the ground, the man who 'loveth his life' and so 'shall lose it' (John 12:25), the thief who carries the bag and calculates the price of worship (John 12:5–6), and rulers so afraid of losing their place that they plan to kill a man Jesus has raised (John 12:10). Christ gives instead his own death as a seed, a grain that falls and dies and 'bringeth forth much fruit' (John 12:24), a lifting up on the cross that draws all men to him (John 12:32), and a commandment from the Father that 'is life everlasting' (John 12:50). The chapter exposes a darkness that falls on people who have seen the light and turned from it: a crowd that hears the Father's voice and calls it thunder (John 12:29), a people who saw 'so many miracles' and 'believed not on him' (John 12:37), eyes blinded and hearts hardened as Isaiah said (John 12:40), and rulers who believed and would not say so, because 'they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God' (John 12:43). The light Christ gives instead is himself: 'I am come a light into the world, that whosoever believeth on me should not abide in darkness' (John 12:46), a light that is with us 'a little while' and must be walked in while it shines (John 12:35–36).

### 🎯 Objectives
- Explain from John 12:23–33 why Jesus calls his death the hour of his glory: what the corn of wheat shows (John 12:24), what the Father's answer shows (John 12:28), and what his being 'lifted up from the earth' does (John 12:32–33).
- Describe the two responses to Jesus at the end of his public ministry, the unbelief that fulfils Isaiah (John 12:37–41) and the faith that will not confess him (John 12:42–43), and say what Jesus' last public words offer to both (John 12:44–50).
- **Doing:** Name one specific way to lose your life for Christ's sake in the coming week (a comfort, a plan, a reputation, an hour, or a sum of money you would otherwise keep for yourself), choose one day to do it, and do it as service to Christ, praying 'Father, glorify thy name' (John 12:28).

## 🏛️ Core Foundations
### ✝️ Christological Root
**Gospel Foundation:** John 12 rests on what Jesus said when the hour he had waited for since Cana finally came. Told that Greeks wanted to see him, he answered, "And Jesus answered them, saying, The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified." (John 12:23) And he explained what that glory would be: "Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit." (John 12:24) Then, with his soul troubled, he refused to be saved from the hour and prayed only that the Father's name be glorified (John 12:27–28), and he told the crowd what his death would do: "And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me." (John 12:32) John adds the key in one line: "This he said, signifying what death he should die." (John 12:33)

**The Bridge:** The hour of John 2:4, 7:30, and 8:20, the hour that was 'not yet', is now come (John 12:23). From here on John's Gospel moves toward the cross, and Jesus calls the cross his glory. That is the bridge to the chapter's doctrines: Christ's death as his glorification and the Father's (John 12:23, 28); his kingship, entered in lowliness on an ass's colt, as Zechariah foretold (John 12:13–15); his death as the means of life, a single grain whose death brings much fruit (John 12:24) and whose lifting up draws all men, Jews and Greeks, to himself (John 12:20–21, 32); his victory over the prince of this world (John 12:31); God's judicial hardening of those who refuse the light (John 12:37–40); Christ's deity, the one whose glory Isaiah saw (John 12:41); and the unity of the Son with the Father who sent him, so that to believe on him and to see him is to believe on and see the Father (John 12:44–45).

**Canonical Echoes:** Jesus (John 12:24, 32) → Promise (Isaiah 52:13; Zechariah 9:9; Isaiah 6:1) → Doctrine (Philippians 2:8–9; Hebrews 2:14; Galatians 6:14) → Life (Mark 8:35; 2 Corinthians 5:14–15; Romans 12:1)

**What Gets Lost:** Taught without Christ at the centre, John 12 becomes three separate lessons: a lesson on generosity from Mary, a Palm Sunday pageant, and a proverb about seeds and sacrifice that could be printed on a gardening calendar. 'He that hateth his life' becomes a call to heroic self-denial, as if we could bear fruit by dying well. With Christ at the centre, the chapter holds together. Mary's ointment is poured on the one who is going to be buried (John 12:7). The King rides a colt because he is going to a cross, not a throne of the world's kind (John 12:15). The grain that dies is first Jesus himself, and only because he died and bore fruit can his servants follow him (John 12:24, 26). And the unbelief that Isaiah foresaw is unbelief in the one whose glory Isaiah saw (John 12:41). Without Christ the chapter asks us to die; with him, it shows us the one who died so that we may live, and then calls us to follow him where he has gone.

### ⚔️ Spiritual Warfare
**The Lie:** Keep your life, and you will have it. The lie speaks in several voices in this chapter. To Judas it says, *worship is waste; the sensible thing is to put a price on it*, and it covers theft with talk of the poor (John 12:5–6). To the chief priests it says, *protect what you have, even if it means killing the evidence*, so they plot against Lazarus (John 12:10). To the believing rulers it says, *you can believe in private and keep your seat in public*, because the praise of men is worth more than the praise of God (John 12:42–43). And it even presses on Jesus himself, in the troubled hour: *save yourself from this* (John 12:27).

**The Truth:** "He that loveth his life shall lose it; and he that hateth his life in this world shall keep it unto life eternal." (John 12:25) "Forasmuch then as the children are partakers of flesh and blood, he also himself likewise took part of the same; that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil;" (Hebrews 2:14)

**The Battleground:** The purse, the place, and the praise of men. The purse is the first ground: the bag Judas carried and the three hundred pence he said were wasted (John 12:5–6). The place is the second: the rulers' fear of losing position, which runs straight on from John 11:48 into the plot against Lazarus (John 12:10–11, 19). And the praise of men is the third: the quiet, respectable battlefield of the believing rulers, where no one is asked to deny Christ, only to say nothing about him (John 12:42–43).

**The Weapon:** The prayer Jesus prayed in the troubled hour: 'Father, glorify thy name' (John 12:28). He did not fight the lie by steeling himself; he gave the hour to the Father's glory, and the Father answered from heaven. And the victory is his, not ours: 'now shall the prince of this world be cast out' (John 12:31). The believer's weapon is to take that same prayer on his lips when the lie says *save yourself*, to trust the one who was lifted up and cast out the enemy, and to follow him where he goes (John 12:26).

### 🌱 Life Pathway
**The Theft:** In John 12 the thief is named. Judas 'was a thief, and had the bag, and bare what was put therein' (John 12:6): he stole from the common purse, and he tried to steal the worth of Mary's worship by putting a price on it (John 12:5). The same theft works more quietly in every heart that keeps its life for itself. A grain of wheat kept in the barn 'abideth alone' (John 12:24): it is safe and it is barren. The man who 'loveth his life shall lose it' (John 12:25). The chief priests, guarding their place, try to take Lazarus's life a second time (John 12:10), and the believing rulers let the praise of men steal their confession of Christ (John 12:42–43). The thief steals worship, fruitfulness, witness, and in the end the very life we try to keep.

**The Source:** Christ, who says, "Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit." (John 12:24) His means in this chapter are his own death, the grain that falls into the ground (John 12:24, 33); his being lifted up, by which he draws all men to himself (John 12:32); his victory over the prince of this world (John 12:31); his word, which is the Father's commandment and 'is life everlasting' (John 12:49–50); and his call to serve and follow, with the promise 'where I am, there shall also my servant be' (John 12:26).

**The Channel:** Life passes to others in this chapter through people who bring others to Jesus and who spend themselves on him. Mary pours out what she has, and 'the house was filled with the odour of the ointment' (John 12:3): one act of devotion fills a whole house. Lazarus, simply by being alive at the table, becomes a reason that 'many of the Jews went away, and believed on Jesus' (John 12:11). The people who saw him raise Lazarus 'bare record' (John 12:17). And when Greeks say 'Sir, we would see Jesus' (John 12:21), Philip and Andrew take them to him. None of these gives life; each points to the one who does. We are channels, never the source: "for without me ye can do nothing." (John 15:5)

**The Abundance Guard:** Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, not prosperity, ease, or freedom from suffering. In John 12 the way to life is a grain falling into the ground and dying (John 12:24), and the promise to those who serve Jesus is not comfort but his company: 'where I am, there shall also my servant be' (John 12:26), and he is going to a cross. The one who 'hateth his life in this world shall keep it unto life eternal' (John 12:25). Life in Christ may cost us money, reputation, safety, and even life in this world, as it nearly cost Lazarus (John 12:10). It never costs us Christ.

### 💡 Light Pathway
**The Darkness:** John 12 names its darkness in Jesus' own words: 'he that walketh in darkness knoweth not whither he goeth' (John 12:35). The crowd hears the Father speak from heaven and says it thundered (John 12:29). People who have seen 'so many miracles' do not believe (John 12:37), and John says that Isaiah foresaw it: 'He hath blinded their eyes, and hardened their heart' (John 12:40). And there is a respectable darkness too: rulers who 'believed on him' but kept it hidden, because 'they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God' (John 12:42–43). This is the darkness of people who have had the light among them and have turned away from it, or who keep what light they have under cover for fear of what it will cost.

**The Source:** Christ, who says 'I am come a light into the world' (John 12:46) and is 'the light of the world' (John 8:12): "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." (2 Corinthians 4:6)

**The Lamp:** In John 12 the lamp is Christ's own call, spoken twice: 'Walk while ye have the light' (John 12:35), and 'believe in the light, that ye may be the children of light' (John 12:36). The light is a person, and it is with them 'a little while' (John 12:35), so the lamp is lit by believing him now and confessing him openly, as the believing rulers would not (John 12:42). For us the lamp is the same: Scripture opened (John 12:38–41 shows the prophets read in the light of Christ), the truth told about Christ, hidden faith confessed aloud (Romans 10:9), a reproof of 'the unfruitful works of darkness' (Ephesians 5:11), and good works that glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16).

**The Love Guard:** Light without love is glare. Jesus' last public words in this Gospel are a cry, not a curse: 'I came not to judge the world, but to save the world' (John 12:47). He warns of judgment at the last day (John 12:48), but he offers light first, to 'whosoever believeth' (John 12:46). And the Scripture about blinded eyes is given to explain unbelief, not to authorise us to decide who is beyond hope. Whoever carries the light of John 12 must carry it the way Jesus did: pleading with people to walk in it while it shines, and grieving, not gloating, over those who turn away (Luke 19:41–42).

### 🏺 Setting the Scene
John 12 opens 'six days before the passover' (John 12:1), the third Passover John names (John 2:13; 6:4; 11:55) and the last of Jesus' life. At the end of John 11 the council had decided to put Jesus to death and had ordered anyone who knew where he was to report it (John 11:53, 57). Jesus now comes back to Bethany, less than two miles from Jerusalem (John 11:18), to eat with the family he loves. Matthew and Mark tell of an anointing at Bethany in the house of Simon the leper, where a woman pours ointment on Jesus' head (Matthew 26:6–13; Mark 14:3–9); John names the woman as Mary and says she anointed his feet. Many interpreters take these as the same event, told from different angles (see Cultural Landmines). The ointment was costly: Judas values it at three hundred pence, and a penny (a Roman denarius) was a day's wage for a labourer (Matthew 20:2), so the ointment was worth close to a year's wages.

Passover brought huge crowds of pilgrims to Jerusalem, many of them from Galilee, where Jesus had followers. The crowd that meets Jesus 'on the next day' (John 12:12) waves palm branches, which were carried at the feast of tabernacles (Leviticus 23:40) and had become a sign of national rejoicing and victory in Jewish history; in the Maccabean period palm branches greeted a Jewish leader entering Jerusalem in triumph (1 Maccabees 13:51, a book outside the Protestant canon; verify the details). Their shout comes from Psalm 118, part of the Hallel psalms sung at the great feasts (verify the liturgical use): 'Save now, I beseech thee, O LORD' (Psalm 118:25), which is where the word *Hosanna* comes from, and 'Blessed be he that cometh in the name of the LORD' (Psalm 118:26). To shout this while waving palms was to greet a king and a deliverer.

Among the pilgrims were 'certain Greeks' (John 12:20). They were not Greek-speaking Jews but Gentiles who worshipped the God of Israel and came up to the feast, like the Ethiopian official in Acts 8:27 (verify that the term here means Gentiles; most interpreters take it so). At the temple, Gentiles could enter the outer court but were forbidden, on pain of death, to go further (verify the inscription and its wording). Their request to see Jesus is the signal that his hour has come. Listen as you read for three things: what Jesus says his death will do, how different people respond to him, and what his last public words offer to anyone who will hear.

#### John 12:1–11 · The Supper and the Ointment
**1** Then Jesus six days before the passover came to Bethany, where Lazarus was which had been dead, whom he raised from the dead.

**2** There they made him a supper; and Martha served: but Lazarus was one of them that sat at the table with him.

**3** Then took Mary a pound of ointment of spikenard, very costly, and anointed the feet of Jesus, and wiped his feet with her hair: and the house was filled with the odour of the ointment.

**4** Then saith one of his disciples, Judas Iscariot, Simon's son, which should betray him,

**5** Why was not this ointment sold for three hundred pence, and given to the poor?

**6** This he said, not that he cared for the poor; but because he was a thief, and had the bag, and bare what was put therein.

**7** Then said Jesus, Let her alone: against the day of my burying hath she kept this.

**8** For the poor always ye have with you; but me ye have not always.

**9** Much people of the Jews therefore knew that he was there: and they came not for Jesus' sake only, but that they might see Lazarus also, whom he had raised from the dead.

**10** But the chief priests consulted that they might put Lazarus also to death;

**11** Because that by reason of him many of the Jews went away, and believed on Jesus.

#### John 12:12–19 · The King Comes to Jerusalem
**12** On the next day much people that were come to the feast, when they heard that Jesus was coming to Jerusalem,

**13** Took branches of palm trees, and went forth to meet him, and cried, Hosanna: Blessed is the King of Israel that cometh in the name of the Lord.

**14** And Jesus, when he had found a young ass, sat thereon; as it is written,

**15** Fear not, daughter of Sion: behold, thy King cometh, sitting on an ass's colt.

**16** These things understood not his disciples at the first: but when Jesus was glorified, then remembered they that these things were written of him, and that they had done these things unto him.

**17** The people therefore that was with him when he called Lazarus out of his grave, and raised him from the dead, bare record.

**18** For this cause the people also met him, for that they heard that he had done this miracle.

**19** The Pharisees therefore said among themselves, Perceive ye how ye prevail nothing? behold, the world is gone after him.

#### John 12:20–26 · The Greeks and the Hour
**20** And there were certain Greeks among them that came up to worship at the feast:

**21** The same came therefore to Philip, which was of Bethsaida of Galilee, and desired him, saying, Sir, we would see Jesus.

**22** Philip cometh and telleth Andrew: and again Andrew and Philip tell Jesus.

**23** And Jesus answered them, saying, The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified.

**24** Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit.

**25** He that loveth his life shall lose it; and he that hateth his life in this world shall keep it unto life eternal.

**26** If any man serve me, let him follow me; and where I am, there shall also my servant be: if any man serve me, him will my Father honour.

#### John 12:27–36 · The Troubled Soul and the Voice from Heaven
**27** Now is my soul troubled; and what shall I say? Father, save me from this hour: but for this cause came I unto this hour.

**28** Father, glorify thy name. Then came there a voice from heaven, saying, I have both glorified it, and will glorify it again.

**29** The people therefore, that stood by, and heard it, said that it thundered: others said, An angel spake to him.

**30** Jesus answered and said, This voice came not because of me, but for your sakes.

**31** Now is the judgment of this world: now shall the prince of this world be cast out.

**32** And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me.

**33** This he said, signifying what death he should die.

**34** The people answered him, We have heard out of the law that Christ abideth for ever: and how sayest thou, The Son of man must be lifted up? who is this Son of man?

**35** Then Jesus said unto them, Yet a little while is the light with you. Walk while ye have the light, lest darkness come upon you: for he that walketh in darkness knoweth not whither he goeth.

**36** While ye have light, believe in the light, that ye may be the children of light. These things spake Jesus, and departed, and did hide himself from them.

#### John 12:37–43 · Unbelief Foretold
**37** But though he had done so many miracles before them, yet they believed not on him:

**38** That the saying of Esaias the prophet might be fulfilled, which he spake, Lord, who hath believed our report? and to whom hath the arm of the Lord been revealed?

**39** Therefore they could not believe, because that Esaias said again,

**40** He hath blinded their eyes, and hardened their heart; that they should not see with their eyes, nor understand with their heart, and be converted, and I should heal them.

**41** These things said Esaias, when he saw his glory, and spake of him.

**42** Nevertheless among the chief rulers also many believed on him; but because of the Pharisees they did not confess him, lest they should be put out of the synagogue:

**43** For they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God.

#### John 12:44–50 · The Last Public Cry
**44** Jesus cried and said, He that believeth on me, believeth not on me, but on him that sent me.

**45** And he that seeth me seeth him that sent me.

**46** I am come a light into the world, that whosoever believeth on me should not abide in darkness.

**47** And if any man hear my words, and believe not, I judge him not: for I came not to judge the world, but to save the world.

**48** He that rejecteth me, and receiveth not my words, hath one that judgeth him: the word that I have spoken, the same shall judge him in the last day.

**49** For I have not spoken of myself; but the Father which sent me, he gave me a commandment, what I should say, and what I should speak.

**50** And I know that his commandment is life everlasting: whatsoever I speak therefore, even as the Father said unto me, so I speak.

**View A:** When Jesus says he 'will draw all men' (John 12:32), 'all men' means all kinds of people without distinction: not only Jews but Gentiles, like the Greeks who have just asked to see him (John 12:20–21). The drawing is the powerful work by which Christ actually brings people to himself, the same verb Jesus used when he said "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him: and I will raise him up at the last day." (John 6:44), where those who are drawn are raised up at the last day. On this view the cross will gather people from every nation to Christ, and every one it draws comes. Compare John 11:52, where his death will 'gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad'.

**View B:** 'All men' means every person without exception. When Christ is lifted up on the cross he exerts a drawing on the whole human race, enabling everyone to come to him, though not everyone yields to it. Supporters point to the universal language of John 1:9 and John 3:16–17, and to Jesus' own words in the same chapter, 'I came not to judge the world, but to save the world' (John 12:47). On this view the drawing of John 12:32 is a real, gracious pull on every human heart, which can be resisted, and John 6:44 describes the same drawing from the side of those who respond.

### 👣 Case Study · Scripture in Action
Stephen understood John 12:24–26 with his life. He was one of the seven chosen to serve the church's widows (Acts 6:1–5), and he became a bold witness to Jesus in Jerusalem, in the same city and before the same council that had condemned his Lord (Acts 6:12–15). When they stoned him, he followed his Master to the end, praying as Jesus had prayed: "And they stoned Stephen, calling upon God, and saying, Lord Jesus, receive my spirit." (Acts 7:59) "And he kneeled down, and cried with a loud voice, Lord, lay not this sin to their charge. And when he had said this, he fell asleep." (Acts 7:60) He did not love his life; he lost it, and he kept it unto life eternal. And the grain that fell into the ground bore fruit. The persecution that followed scattered the church, and "Therefore they that were scattered abroad went every where preaching the word." (Acts 8:4) Standing by at Stephen's death, keeping the coats of those who killed him, was a young man named Saul (Acts 7:58; 22:20), who would become the apostle Paul and carry the gospel to the Greeks. Stephen never saw the harvest. But he is a picture of what Jesus promised: 'where I am, there shall also my servant be' (John 12:26). He saw 'the Son of man standing on the right hand of God' (Acts 7:56) as he died. The fruit of Stephen's death was real, but it came from Christ's death before it: Stephen could lose his life because Christ had already died and risen, and his prayer 'Lord Jesus, receive my spirit' rested on a Saviour who had gone through death ahead of him.

### ⛪ Doctrine in My Own Words
- **Theology Proper:** The chief end of all that the Father does, and of all that the Son does, is the glory of God's name, and the Father glorifies his name in the Son's death (John 12:28).
- **Christology:** Jesus' death on the cross is not a defeat but the hour of his glory, the lifting up by which he is exalted and draws all men to himself (John 12:23, 32–33).
- **Christology:** Jesus is the King of Israel, who comes as Zechariah foretold, lowly and riding on an ass's colt, to reign by way of the cross (John 12:13–15).
- **Christology:** Jesus is the LORD whose glory Isaiah saw, and to believe on him and to see him is to believe on and see the Father who sent him (John 12:41, 44–45).
- **Anthropology & Sin:** The human heart can see many signs and still refuse to believe, can love money more than Christ, and can love the praise of men more than the praise of God (John 12:6, 37, 43).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** Life comes through Christ's death: he is the grain of wheat that dies and bears much fruit, and those who hate their life in this world keep it unto life eternal (John 12:24–25).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** God justly hardens those who persist in refusing the light, so that the unbelief of many fulfils Scripture, yet the light is still offered to whosoever believes (John 12:37–40, 46).
- **Ecclesiology:** Christ's death gathers Jews and Gentiles into one people who serve him, follow him, and are honoured by the Father (John 12:20–26, 32).
- **Eschatology:** The world was judged and its prince cast out at the cross, and Christ's word will judge every rejecter at the last day (John 12:31, 48).
- **Christian Walk:** A disciple serves Christ by following him, hates his life in this world rather than love it, and confesses Christ openly rather than seek the praise of men (John 12:25–26, 42–43).

### 👑 Christocentric & Humility · Level 4
Every problem in John 12 is answered in Jesus. A family that loves him has only a pound of ointment to give; he receives it as preparation for his burial, and says the gift will not be wasted (John 12:7). A thief puts a price on worship; Jesus defends the worshipper and will give himself, which no one can price (John 12:5–8). The chief priests want to kill the evidence of his power (John 12:10); he is going to die himself, and rise, so that death has no hold on any of his people. The crowd wants a king of the familiar kind; he comes on an ass's colt, as the lowly King of Zechariah 9:9 (John 12:15). The disciples do not understand; he will be glorified, and then they will remember (John 12:16). Greeks, outsiders to Israel, want to see him; he will be lifted up and draw all men to himself (John 12:21, 32). His own soul is troubled; he gives the hour to the Father's glory (John 12:27–28). The world stands under judgment and its prince holds it; he casts the prince out (John 12:31). People walk in darkness; he is the light (John 12:35–36, 46). They cannot believe; he is the one whose glory Isaiah saw, and the arm of the Lord revealed (John 12:38, 41). And the whole world deserves judgment; he came 'to save the world' (John 12:47).

Every truth in the chapter is found in him. Kingship is his (John 12:13). Glory is his, and it shines brightest at the cross (John 12:23, 28). Fruitfulness is his, the harvest of the grain that dies (John 12:24). Victory over Satan is his (John 12:31). The drawing power that brings sinners to God is his (John 12:32). Light is his (John 12:46). And life everlasting is in the word the Father gave him to speak (John 12:50). The chapter humbles us because it shows that we are the crowd that wants a different kind of king, the disciples who understand only later, the rulers who hide their faith, and sometimes Judas with his hand in the bag. It humbles us most by showing that the way to life is not up but down: the grain must fall into the ground (John 12:24). And it makes Christ glorious, because the one whose glory Isaiah saw on a throne 'high and lifted up' (Isaiah 6:1) was lifted up on a cross for people who did not believe in him.

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy
### 📌 Core Concept
Six days before a big feast called the Passover, Jesus went to have supper with his friends Martha, Mary, and Lazarus, the man he had brought back from the dead. Mary loved Jesus so much that she poured a whole jar of very expensive perfume on his feet and wiped them with her hair. The whole house smelled of it. Judas, one of the disciples, grumbled that the perfume should have been sold for money, but John tells us Judas was really a thief who stole from the money bag. Jesus said, *Leave her alone.* The next day Jesus rode into Jerusalem on a young donkey, and huge crowds waved palm branches and shouted, *Hosanna!* They thought he had come to be a king like other kings. But Jesus had come to be a different kind of king. When some visitors from far away asked to see him, Jesus said that his time had come, and he talked about a seed. A seed that stays in a jar stays all alone. But a seed that is planted in the ground, and seems to die, grows into a plant with lots and lots of new seeds. Jesus was talking about himself. He was going to die on a cross, and because he died, many, many people would come to God. Then God the Father spoke from heaven, and Jesus told the people, *I am the light. Believe in me while you can, so you won't be lost in the dark.*

### 📖 Key Verses
"Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit." (John 12:24) Jesus is like a seed that was planted, so that many people could have new life.

"If any man serve me, let him follow me; and where I am, there shall also my servant be: if any man serve me, him will my Father honour." (John 12:26) If we follow Jesus, we will always be where he is, and God the Father will be pleased with us.

"And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me." (John 12:32) When Jesus was lifted up on the cross, he was starting to bring people from every country to himself.

"I am come a light into the world, that whosoever believeth on me should not abide in darkness." (John 12:46) Jesus is the light, and everyone who trusts him does not have to stay in the dark.

### 🪞 Analogy
Imagine you have a sunflower seed. You could keep it in a jar on your shelf. It would be safe there, and it would stay exactly the same for years: one small seed, all alone. Or you could plant it. You would dig a hole, put the seed in the dark soil, and cover it up. For a while it would look as if the seed were gone. But under the ground, the seed breaks open, and a green shoot comes up, and by the end of the summer there is a sunflower taller than you, with hundreds of new seeds in its big yellow face. One seed, planted, becomes hundreds. Jesus said he was like that seed. When he died on the cross and was buried, it looked as if everything was over. But he rose again, and from his death came new life for more people than anyone can count, maybe even you. And Jesus says that when we give our lives to him, instead of keeping them in a jar for ourselves, something good grows from them too.

### 📜 Walkthrough
#### John 12:1–3 · A Supper at Bethany and a Pound of Spikenard
**Says:** "Then Jesus six days before the passover came to Bethany, where Lazarus was which had been dead, whom he raised from the dead." (John 12:1) "There they made him a supper; and Martha served: but Lazarus was one of them that sat at the table with him." (John 12:2) "Then took Mary a pound of ointment of spikenard, very costly, and anointed the feet of Jesus, and wiped his feet with her hair: and the house was filled with the odour of the ointment." (John 12:3) *Spikenard* (nard) is a perfumed oil made from a plant grown far to the east, in the Himalayas (verify the source); *odour* means fragrance.

**Means:** John times the scene with care: 'six days before the passover'. The countdown to the cross has begun, and the whole second half of the Gospel, from here to John 19, will cover one week. Jesus returns to Bethany, which he left when the council decided to kill him (John 11:53–54), and so walks back into danger. The family he loves makes him a supper, and each member of it acts in character. Martha serves, as she did in Luke 10:40; the Greek verb John uses is the one Jesus will use in John 12:26 for serving him. Lazarus sits at the table, a living man at a meal, the sign of John 11 made visible. And Mary, as always, is at Jesus' feet (Luke 10:39; John 11:32). A 'pound' was a Roman measure of about twelve ounces (verify), a large amount of a very precious perfume. Anointing a guest's head was a known courtesy (Psalm 23:5; Luke 7:46), but Mary anoints his feet and wipes them with her hair, which a respectable woman would not usually let down in public (verify). It is an act of humility and extravagant love, holding nothing back. John adds the kind of detail an eyewitness remembers: 'the house was filled with the odour of the ointment'. What she did filled the whole house. Mark and Matthew tell of the same act, and Jesus there says it will be told 'throughout the whole world' (Mark 14:9); here the fragrance fills the house, and the gospel carries it everywhere.

**Asks:** What does your love for Jesus cost you? Is there anything you would pour out for him without calculating, as Mary did, and is there anything you are keeping in the jar?

#### John 12:4–8 · Judas and the Bag
**Says:** "Then saith one of his disciples, Judas Iscariot, Simon's son, which should betray him," (John 12:4) "Why was not this ointment sold for three hundred pence, and given to the poor?" (John 12:5) "This he said, not that he cared for the poor; but because he was a thief, and had the bag, and bare what was put therein." (John 12:6) "Then said Jesus, Let her alone: against the day of my burying hath she kept this." (John 12:7) "For the poor always ye have with you; but me ye have not always." (John 12:8) *Pence* here means Roman denarii, a day's wage each (Matthew 20:2); *the bag* is the disciples' money box; *bare* means carried, and here carried off; *against the day* means for the day, in preparation for it.

**Means:** Judas does not attack Mary's love directly. He puts a price on it, three hundred pence, nearly a year's wages for a labourer, and he dresses his objection in concern for the poor. It sounds wise and generous. John, the narrator, strips it bare. Judas said it 'not that he cared for the poor', but because 'he was a thief'. He kept the common purse (John 13:29), and 'bare what was put therein'. The word John uses for 'bare' can mean simply to carry, but it was also used in the everyday Greek of the time for carrying off, pilfering, and the STEPBible lexicon lists this verse under that sense (see the Deep Dive). John's narrator names him, from the start, as the one 'which should betray him': the man who calls worship a waste will soon sell his Master for money (Matthew 26:14–15). Jesus answers with a gentle command and an explanation. 'Let her alone' defends the worshipper. 'Against the day of my burying hath she kept this' interprets her act: whether or not Mary fully understood it, she was anointing his body ahead of his burial (Mark 14:8). Then Jesus sets the whole matter in time. 'For the poor always ye have with you' echoes Deuteronomy 15:11, which goes on to command generosity to the poor; Jesus does not excuse neglect of them. But 'me ye have not always'. There was a moment for this act, and it was now, days before his death. Love for Christ and love for the poor are not rivals; but love for the poor that despises worship of Christ is not what it pretends to be.

**Asks:** Have you ever called devotion to Christ a waste: time given to prayer, money given to his work, a life given to his service? What might that reveal about what your heart treasures? And do you care for the poor because you love Christ, or talk about the poor to avoid him?

#### John 12:9–11 · They Consulted to Put Lazarus Also to Death
**Says:** "Much people of the Jews therefore knew that he was there: and they came not for Jesus' sake only, but that they might see Lazarus also, whom he had raised from the dead." (John 12:9) "But the chief priests consulted that they might put Lazarus also to death;" (John 12:10) "Because that by reason of him many of the Jews went away, and believed on Jesus." (John 12:11) *Consulted* means plotted, took counsel; *by reason of him* means because of him.

**Means:** The news spreads, and people come out from Jerusalem to see two things: Jesus, and the man he raised. Lazarus has become a living witness without saying a word; John records no speech of his anywhere in the Gospel. The chief priests' answer is chilling in its logic. They plotted to kill Jesus to protect their place (John 11:48, 53); now they plot to kill Lazarus too, 'because that by reason of him many of the Jews went away, and believed'. They cannot deny the sign, so they plan to destroy the evidence. John sets the irony before us plainly: the leaders plan to kill a man who has already been raised by the one who is the resurrection and the life (John 11:25). Unbelief does not always come from lack of evidence. Here it comes from the fear of what believing would cost. The phrase 'went away' suggests people leaving their old allegiance to the leaders and turning to Jesus.

**Asks:** What does it tell you about the human heart that the chief priests' answer to a resurrection was a murder plot? Has what Christ has done in your life become a reason for others to come to him, as Lazarus was, even when you are not saying anything?

#### John 12:12–16 · Hosanna, and a King on an Ass's Colt
**Says:** "On the next day much people that were come to the feast, when they heard that Jesus was coming to Jerusalem," (John 12:12) "Took branches of palm trees, and went forth to meet him, and cried, Hosanna: Blessed is the King of Israel that cometh in the name of the Lord." (John 12:13) "And Jesus, when he had found a young ass, sat thereon; as it is written," (John 12:14) "Fear not, daughter of Sion: behold, thy King cometh, sitting on an ass's colt." (John 12:15) "These things understood not his disciples at the first: but when Jesus was glorified, then remembered they that these things were written of him, and that they had done these things unto him." (John 12:16) *Hosanna* is a Hebrew cry, *save now*; *thereon* means on it; *Sion* is Zion, Jerusalem; an *ass's colt* is a young donkey.

**Means:** The festival crowd goes out 'to meet him', the way a city went out to welcome a king or a victorious general. Their palm branches were a sign of rejoicing and national victory (see Setting the Scene), and their shout is from Psalm 118:25–26. *Hosanna* began as a prayer, 'Save now, I beseech thee, O LORD' (Psalm 118:25), and became a shout of praise; it is a prayer they do not understand they are praying. They add a title the psalm does not have: 'the King of Israel'. That is exactly what Nathanael confessed at the beginning (John 1:49), and exactly what the crowd in Galilee had tried to force on Jesus by making him king (John 6:15). This time Jesus does not withdraw. He accepts the title, but he interprets it by what he does. He finds a young ass and sits on it, and John quotes Zechariah 9:9. John's form of the quotation opens with 'Fear not', perhaps drawing on Isaiah 40:9 or Zephaniah 3:16 (verify), where Zion is told not to fear because her God is in her midst. Zechariah's King is 'just, and having salvation; lowly, and riding upon an ass' (Zechariah 9:9), and he goes on to 'speak peace unto the heathen' (Zechariah 9:10). This is not a war-horse king. He comes in peace, in lowliness, to bring salvation. Then John adds one of his narrator's notes: the disciples did not understand 'at the first'. Only 'when Jesus was glorified', after the cross and the resurrection, did they remember and see that the Scriptures were about him. John has said the same of the temple saying (John 2:22). Understanding comes after the hour, by the Spirit who brings Jesus' words to remembrance (John 14:26).

**Asks:** What kind of king do you want Jesus to be? Are there ways you shout *Hosanna* while hoping for a king who will give you what you want, rather than the lowly King who came to die? And what parts of Scripture have you only understood looking back, after Christ was more fully revealed to you?

#### John 12:17–19 · The World Is Gone After Him
**Says:** "The people therefore that was with him when he called Lazarus out of his grave, and raised him from the dead, bare record." (John 12:17) "For this cause the people also met him, for that they heard that he had done this miracle." (John 12:18) "The Pharisees therefore said among themselves, Perceive ye how ye prevail nothing? behold, the world is gone after him." (John 12:19) *Bare record* means bore witness, testified; *prevail nothing* means achieve nothing, get nowhere.

**Means:** John explains why the crowd came out. There were two groups: the people who had been at the tomb when Jesus called Lazarus out, who 'bare record', and the festival crowd who had heard about the sign and came to see. Witness is one of John's great themes (John 1:7; 5:31–39; 19:35; 21:24), and here it is ordinary people telling what they saw. The Pharisees' reaction is exasperation: 'Perceive ye how ye prevail nothing'. Their plans are not working. And then one of John's ironies: 'behold, the world is gone after him'. They mean it as an exaggeration, the way people say *everybody* is doing something. But John's readers know it is truer than they meant. In the very next verse, the world arrives: Greeks come to see Jesus (John 12:20–21). The one sent because 'God so loved the world' (John 3:16) is about to draw all men to himself (John 12:32).

**Asks:** What have you seen Christ do that you could 'bare record' of this week? And where have you, like the Pharisees, been trying to hold back what God is doing, and finding that you 'prevail nothing'?

#### John 12:20–23 · Sir, We Would See Jesus
**Says:** "And there were certain Greeks among them that came up to worship at the feast:" (John 12:20) "The same came therefore to Philip, which was of Bethsaida of Galilee, and desired him, saying, Sir, we would see Jesus." (John 12:21) "Philip cometh and telleth Andrew: and again Andrew and Philip tell Jesus." (John 12:22) "And Jesus answered them, saying, The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified." (John 12:23) *Desired him* means asked him; *would see* means wish to see.

**Means:** These Greeks were most likely Gentiles who worshipped the God of Israel and came up to the feast (see Setting the Scene). They come to Philip, who has a Greek name and came from Bethsaida, a town near largely Gentile territory (verify); perhaps that is why they chose him. Their request is simple and beautiful: 'Sir, we would see Jesus'. In John, to 'see' can mean more than to look; it can mean to come to know and believe (John 1:39; 6:40; 14:9). Philip, not sure what to do, goes to Andrew, and the two of them go to Jesus, the same two who brought others to him at the start (John 1:40–46; 6:5–9). John does not tell us whether the Greeks got their interview. Instead Jesus answers with a sentence that turns the Gospel on its hinge: 'The hour is come'. Until now the hour has always been 'not yet' (John 2:4; 7:30; 8:20). Now it has come, and the coming of Gentiles is the signal. Jesus had said he had 'other sheep' not of this fold (John 10:16), and John said his death would 'gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad' (John 11:52). The nations are knocking; the gathering can happen only one way, through his death. And Jesus calls that hour the hour 'that the Son of man should be glorified'. In Daniel 7:13–14, the one like a Son of man receives 'dominion, and glory, and a kingdom' so that 'all people, nations, and languages, should serve him' (Daniel 7:14). Jesus will receive that glory, but, as the next verse shows, by way of a grave.

**Asks:** Who around you is saying, in words or in their life, 'Sir, we would see Jesus'? Will you, like Philip and Andrew, bring them to him? And are you ready to hear that the way to see Jesus' glory is to look at his cross?

#### John 12:24–26 · Except a Corn of Wheat Fall into the Ground and Die
**Says:** "Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit." (John 12:24) "He that loveth his life shall lose it; and he that hateth his life in this world shall keep it unto life eternal." (John 12:25) "If any man serve me, let him follow me; and where I am, there shall also my servant be: if any man serve me, him will my Father honour." (John 12:26) *Verily* means truly; *corn* in the KJV means grain, here a single grain or seed; *abideth* means remains.

**Means:** 'Verily, verily' marks a solemn saying. Jesus explains his glory with a picture every farmer knows. A grain of wheat kept in the barn is safe, but it 'abideth alone': one grain, forever. Only if it is sown, falls into the ground, and 'dies', its old form broken open, does it bear 'much fruit'. Jesus is speaking first of himself. He is the grain. His death is not the end of his work but the way it multiplies: from one death comes a harvest of people from every nation, including Greeks like those at the door. Paul uses the same picture for the resurrection body: 'that which thou sowest is not quickened, except it die' (1 Corinthians 15:36). Then Jesus turns the picture on his followers. 'He that loveth his life shall lose it'. 'Life' here is the ordinary life of this world, the self and its concerns, not the 'life eternal' at the end of the verse; John uses two different Greek words (see the Deep Dive). To 'hate' one's life 'in this world' is a Hebrew way of speaking comparatively: to love it less, to refuse to let it be first, when it competes with Christ (compare Luke 14:26 with Matthew 10:37). It is not self-contempt or a death wish; it is putting one's own life below Christ, and so keeping it 'unto life eternal'. The Synoptic Gospels record similar sayings (Mark 8:35; Matthew 10:39; Luke 9:24). Verse 26 defines discipleship in three phrases. Service means following: 'let him follow me', and he is going to the cross. Following leads to fellowship: 'where I am, there shall also my servant be', a promise that looks past the cross to the Father's house (John 14:3; 17:24). And the reward is the Father's honour: 'him will my Father honour'. The rulers, we will soon hear, wanted the praise of men (John 12:43); Jesus offers the honour of the Father.

**Asks:** Is there anything in your life you are keeping 'alone', safe in the barn, that Christ is calling you to let fall into the ground? What would it look like this week to serve him by following him, even where it costs you, trusting the Father's honour instead of men's?

#### John 12:27–30 · Now Is My Soul Troubled
**Says:** "Now is my soul troubled; and what shall I say? Father, save me from this hour: but for this cause came I unto this hour." (John 12:27) "Father, glorify thy name. Then came there a voice from heaven, saying, I have both glorified it, and will glorify it again." (John 12:28) "The people therefore, that stood by, and heard it, said that it thundered: others said, An angel spake to him." (John 12:29) "Jesus answered and said, This voice came not because of me, but for your sakes." (John 12:30) *For this cause* means for this very purpose; *spake* means spoke.

**Means:** Having spoken of the grain that dies, Jesus feels the weight of what it means. 'Now is my soul troubled'. This is not a calm philosopher facing death. It is the Son of God in true human flesh, shaken at the approach of the cross, the same word John used of his distress at Lazarus's tomb (John 11:33). John has no Gethsemane scene; many readers see this as John's version of the struggle the other Gospels place in the garden (Matthew 26:38–39; Mark 14:34–36; see the Council's second question). 'And what shall I say' is a real question. The KJV prints 'Father, save me from this hour' as a prayer, the natural cry of his humanity, which he at once sets aside: 'but for this cause came I unto this hour'. Many modern versions print it as a question, *shall I say, Father, save me from this hour?*, which he then rejects. Either way, the direction of his will is the same: he came for this hour, and he will not turn from it. And he prays the prayer that governs everything: 'Father, glorify thy name'. The Father answers aloud, one of the few times in the Gospels a voice speaks from heaven (compare Matthew 3:17; 17:5). 'I have both glorified it', in the Son's life and signs (John 2:11; 11:4, 40), 'and will glorify it again', in the Son's death, resurrection, and exaltation (John 13:31–32; 17:1–5). The crowd hears it and divides, as crowds do in John. Some say it thundered; others, an angel. Thunder was sometimes a sign of God's presence in the Old Testament (Exodus 19:16–19; Psalm 29:3), but they miss that it was a voice with words. Jesus says it came 'for your sakes': he did not need to be assured, but they needed to hear the Father's witness to the Son (compare John 11:42).

**Asks:** What does it mean to you that Jesus' soul was troubled as he faced the cross, and that he went on anyway? When your own soul is troubled, is your first prayer *save me from this*, and can you learn to pray with him, 'Father, glorify thy name'?

#### John 12:31–33 · Now Is the Judgment of This World
**Says:** "Now is the judgment of this world: now shall the prince of this world be cast out." (John 12:31) "And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me." (John 12:32) "This he said, signifying what death he should die." (John 12:33) *Prince* means ruler; *signifying* means showing, indicating.

**Means:** Jesus' double 'Now' announces what the cross will be. It is 'the judgment of this world': the world, in John the human race organised in rebellion against God (John 1:10; 7:7), will show what it is by crucifying the Son, and will stand condemned for it (John 16:8–11). And it is the defeat of the world's ruler: 'now shall the prince of this world be cast out'. Jesus calls Satan by this title three times in John (John 12:31; 14:30; 16:11). At the cross, when the devil seems to win, he is thrown out of his usurped place (compare Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14; 1 John 3:8). Then comes the positive side: 'And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me'. The 'I' is emphatic. 'If' does not express doubt; it means *when*. 'Lifted up' is John's double-edged word: it means lifted physically on the cross and exalted in glory (John 3:14; 8:28; see the Deep Dive), and Isaiah had said the Servant 'shall be exalted and extolled, and be very high' (Isaiah 52:13) just before describing his suffering. John makes sure we do not miss the meaning: 'This he said, signifying what death he should die'. Crucifixion was a death of being raised up from the earth. The cross, the lowest shame, is the throne from which he draws 'all men', Jews and Greeks alike, to himself. How the phrase 'all men' should be understood is discussed in View A / View B on the participant pages.

**Asks:** Do you see the cross as the place where Satan was defeated, or do you live as though the enemy still has the last word over you? And has the lifted-up Christ drawn you to himself? If so, how could you make his cross plain to someone else, so that they might be drawn too?

#### John 12:34–36 · Who Is This Son of Man?
**Says:** "The people answered him, We have heard out of the law that Christ abideth for ever: and how sayest thou, The Son of man must be lifted up? who is this Son of man?" (John 12:34) "Then Jesus said unto them, Yet a little while is the light with you. Walk while ye have the light, lest darkness come upon you: for he that walketh in darkness knoweth not whither he goeth." (John 12:35) "While ye have light, believe in the light, that ye may be the children of light. These things spake Jesus, and departed, and did hide himself from them." (John 12:36) *The law* here means the Scriptures as a whole; *whither* means where; *children of light* means people who belong to the light.

**Means:** The crowd understands that 'lifted up' means death, and it collides with their theology. They have heard 'out of the law' (the Scriptures generally, as in John 10:34) that 'Christ abideth for ever': texts such as Psalm 89:36, 'His seed shall endure for ever', Isaiah 9:7, and Daniel 7:14 promised an everlasting Messiah and kingdom. How can the Messiah die? And 'who is this Son of man'? The question is honest and confused, and it is a classic Johannine misunderstanding. The Scriptures do teach that the Christ abides for ever, and he will, because he will rise and never die again (Romans 6:9). But they also teach that the Servant must suffer (Isaiah 53:8–12). Jesus does not argue. He answers with urgency. 'Yet a little while is the light with you'. The light is himself (John 8:12; 9:5), and he will soon be gone from their sight. So they must 'walk while ye have the light', because 'darkness' is coming, and a man in the dark does not know where he is going (compare John 11:9–10). The answer to their question is not a debate but a decision: 'believe in the light, that ye may be the children of light'. Faith in Christ makes a person belong to the light (Ephesians 5:8; 1 Thessalonians 5:5). Then comes one of the saddest sentences in John: Jesus 'departed, and did hide himself from them'. His public ministry is over. The light withdraws from those who will not walk in it.

**Asks:** Are there questions about Jesus that you have used, like the crowd, to put off believing him? What would it mean for you to walk in the light you already have, today, rather than waiting until every question is answered?

#### John 12:37–41 · Isaiah Saw His Glory
**Says:** "But though he had done so many miracles before them, yet they believed not on him:" (John 12:37) "That the saying of Esaias the prophet might be fulfilled, which he spake, Lord, who hath believed our report? and to whom hath the arm of the Lord been revealed?" (John 12:38) "Therefore they could not believe, because that Esaias said again," (John 12:39) "He hath blinded their eyes, and hardened their heart; that they should not see with their eyes, nor understand with their heart, and be converted, and I should heal them." (John 12:40) "These things said Esaias, when he saw his glory, and spake of him." (John 12:41) *Esaias* is the Greek form of Isaiah; *report* means message; *the arm of the Lord* means God's power in action; *converted* means turned back to God.

**Means:** Here John, the narrator, steps back and looks over the whole public ministry. He calls the miracles 'signs' in Greek, and the signs were 'so many', and done 'before them', openly. Yet 'they believed not on him'. John does not treat this as a failure of God's plan. He finds it foretold in two passages of Isaiah. The first is Isaiah 53:1, the opening of the song of the suffering Servant: 'who hath believed our report?' The prophet foresaw that the message about the Servant, and the 'arm of the Lord' revealed in him, would be widely disbelieved. The second is Isaiah 6:10, from the prophet's call, where God sent Isaiah to a people who would hear and not understand. John's wording follows neither the Hebrew nor the Greek Old Testament exactly (verify); he gives the sense, and puts it strongly: 'Therefore they could not believe'. God 'hath blinded their eyes, and hardened their heart'. This is judicial hardening: God giving people over to the blindness they have chosen. John has already said why they did not come to the light, that 'men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil' (John 3:19), and verse 37 puts their unbelief first. Both are true together: they would not believe, and in the end they could not, because God confirmed them in their choice (see the Debate Corner). Then John adds the astonishing verse 41: Isaiah said these things 'when he saw his glory, and spake of him'. In Isaiah 6 the prophet saw 'the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up' (Isaiah 6:1), 'the King, the LORD of hosts' (Isaiah 6:5). John says it was Jesus' glory he saw. The one on Isaiah's throne is the one who will be lifted up on the cross. That is as direct a claim to Christ's deity as any in the Gospel (see the Council's third question).

**Asks:** Does it trouble you or comfort you that God is sovereign even over unbelief? How does it warn you not to put off walking in the light? And what does it do to your view of Jesus that the prophet who cried *Holy, holy, holy* before the throne was looking at him?

#### John 12:42–43 · They Loved the Praise of Men
**Says:** "Nevertheless among the chief rulers also many believed on him; but because of the Pharisees they did not confess him, lest they should be put out of the synagogue:" (John 12:42) "For they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God." (John 12:43) *Nevertheless* means yet, all the same; *put out of the synagogue* means expelled from the Jewish community's worship, as the healed blind man was (John 9:22, 34).

**Means:** John balances his verdict. The unbelief was widespread, but not total. 'Among the chief rulers also many believed on him'. John may be thinking of men like Nicodemus and Joseph of Arimathaea, who was 'a disciple of Jesus, but secretly for fear of the Jews' (John 19:38). But their faith was hidden. 'They did not confess him', because the Pharisees had threatened to put confessors out of the synagogue (John 9:22), and that meant losing their place, their standing, their community. John names the root: 'they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God'. The word translated 'praise' is the same word translated 'glory' in verse 41 (see the Deep Dive). Isaiah saw the glory of Christ; these rulers preferred the glory men give. Jesus had already warned about this: 'How can ye believe, which receive honour one of another, and seek not the honour that cometh from God only?' (John 5:44). John does not say whether their faith was genuine and weak, or not saving at all; he leaves it as a warning. At least two of them, Joseph and Nicodemus, later came into the open at the cross (John 19:38–39). Secret faith is not where a disciple can stay.

**Asks:** Where do you believe in Christ but keep it quiet because of what it might cost you with others? Whose praise do you most want, and what would it look like this week to seek the honour that comes from God only?

#### John 12:44–46 · I Am Come a Light into the World
**Says:** "Jesus cried and said, He that believeth on me, believeth not on me, but on him that sent me." (John 12:44) "And he that seeth me seeth him that sent me." (John 12:45) "I am come a light into the world, that whosoever believeth on me should not abide in darkness." (John 12:46) *Cried* here means called out loudly; *whosoever* means whoever.

**Means:** John has told us that Jesus hid himself (John 12:36). These final verses are not a new scene but a summary, in Jesus' own voice, of his public message, placed here as the last word before he turns to his disciples in John 13 (verify; most interpreters read it so). 'Jesus cried', a loud public proclamation, as at the feast (John 7:28, 37). The first claim is about the Father. To believe on Jesus is not to trust a mere man but to trust 'him that sent me'. And to see Jesus is to see the Father (John 14:9). The Son is so perfectly the Father's messenger and image that there is no going around him to God, and no seeing him without seeing God (John 1:18). The second claim is the light. 'I am come a light into the world' gathers up the prologue (John 1:4–9), the feast of tabernacles (John 8:12), and the healing of the blind man (John 9:5). The purpose is saving: 'whosoever believeth on me should not abide in darkness'. Everyone is born in darkness; the question is whether we remain there. The invitation is open, 'whosoever'. Even after John 12:37–41, the call goes out to any who will believe.

**Asks:** When you pray to Jesus, do you know that you are speaking to the Father who sent him? Where are you still choosing to 'abide in darkness' in some part of your life, when the light has come?

#### John 12:47–50 · The Word That Shall Judge Him
**Says:** "And if any man hear my words, and believe not, I judge him not: for I came not to judge the world, but to save the world." (John 12:47) "He that rejecteth me, and receiveth not my words, hath one that judgeth him: the word that I have spoken, the same shall judge him in the last day." (John 12:48) "For I have not spoken of myself; but the Father which sent me, he gave me a commandment, what I should say, and what I should speak." (John 12:49) "And I know that his commandment is life everlasting: whatsoever I speak therefore, even as the Father said unto me, so I speak." (John 12:50) *Rejecteth* means refuses, sets aside; *of myself* means on my own authority.

**Means:** Jesus holds together two truths that seem to pull apart. He did not come to judge: 'I came not to judge the world, but to save the world' (compare John 3:17). The purpose of his first coming is rescue. Yet judgment is real. The one who 'rejecteth me' is not left without a judge: 'the word that I have spoken, the same shall judge him in the last day'. Jesus does not need to bring a new accusation; the words he spoke, heard and refused, will stand as the evidence. Elsewhere he says the Father 'hath committed all judgment unto the Son' (John 5:22); here he says his word will be the standard of that judgment. Both are true: the Son who judges will judge by the word he spoke. Then Jesus explains why his word has such weight. He did not speak 'of myself'. The Father gave him a commandment, what to say and how to say it (John 7:16; 8:28; 14:10). And that commandment 'is life everlasting'. The Father's message through the Son is not a burden but eternal life (John 17:3). So Jesus' public ministry ends as it began, with an offer of life (John 1:4; 3:16). Whoever hears him is hearing the Father, and the words that offer life are the same words that will judge those who refuse it.

**Asks:** Have you received Christ's words as the Father's commandment of life, or are you hearing them and setting them aside? If his words will be the standard on the last day, how should that change the way you listen to them this week?

### 🧨 Cultural Landmines and Misconceptions
**One anointing, or two, or three?** The Gospels tell of a woman anointing Jesus in four places. Luke 7:36–50 tells of 'a woman in the city, which was a sinner' who anoints his feet in a Pharisee's house in Galilee, early in the ministry (Luke 7:37). That is a different occasion. Matthew 26:6–13 and Mark 14:3–9 tell of an anointing at Bethany in the house of Simon the leper, shortly before the Passover, where a woman pours ointment of spikenard on his head, some complain of waste and name a price of more than three hundred pence (Mark 14:5), and Jesus says she has anointed his body for burial. Most interpreters take this to be the same event as John 12:1–8, with John naming the woman as Mary, telling us she anointed the feet, and naming Judas as the complainer (verify). She may well have anointed both head and feet. The differences in order and detail are discussed in the Council's first question. Since Pope Gregory the Great in the sixth century, Western tradition often merged Mary of Bethany, the sinful woman of Luke 7, and Mary Magdalene into one person (verify); the Gospels do not, and nothing in John suggests that Mary of Bethany had been a notorious sinner.

**Palm Sunday as a pageant.** Many churches remember the entry into Jerusalem with children waving palms, and it is right to rejoice. But the modern reader can miss how political the scene was. Palms, *Hosanna*, and the title 'the King of Israel' were the language of national deliverance, and the Pharisees and chief priests saw the danger (John 11:48; 12:19). Jesus' answer to that hope was an ass's colt, not a war-horse: a king of peace, going to die (Zechariah 9:9–10). The pageant misses the cross; the politics misses the lowliness. John shows both, and the disciples themselves understood it only later (John 12:16).

**Hating your life.** 'He that hateth his life' can land badly on a modern reader, especially one who already struggles with self-contempt, depression, or thoughts of self-harm. Jesus is not commanding self-loathing. In Hebrew idiom, 'hate' can mean *love less* or *put second* (compare Genesis 29:30–31; Luke 14:26 with Matthew 10:37; see the Deep Dive). The same Gospel says God so loved the world (John 3:16), and Jesus lays down his life for his sheep because he values them (John 10:11). The verse asks us to let Christ, not self, sit on the throne, and it promises that the one who does so keeps his life for ever.

**Who were the Greeks?** Some readers imagine Greek philosophers or tourists. They 'came up to worship at the feast' (John 12:20): they were almost certainly Gentiles who worshipped Israel's God, sometimes called God-fearers (compare Acts 10:2; 17:4; verify the term's use), and they came as worshippers, not sightseers. They stand at the edge of Israel's worship, just as the court of the Gentiles stood at the edge of the temple. Their request is the first sign that the whole world is about to be drawn to Christ (John 12:19, 32).

**'The Jews' and the hardened heart.** John 12:37–40 has sometimes been misused to claim that the Jewish people as a whole are blind or rejected. John himself will not allow it. In this chapter 'many of the Jews went away, and believed on Jesus' (John 12:11), many of the rulers believed (John 12:42), and the disciples, Mary, Martha, and Lazarus were all Jews. Isaiah's words were first spoken about Israel in his own day, and Paul, quoting the same kind of text, speaks of 'blindness in part' until the fulness of the Gentiles comes in, and of a future mercy (Romans 11:7–8, 25–26). The warning of John 12:37–40 is for every hearer who has seen the light and turned from it, in every nation, including the church.

**Thunder, or a voice?** Modern readers sometimes explain the voice of John 12:28 as a clap of thunder that the disciples interpreted religiously. That is the explanation John reports from part of the crowd and sets aside (John 12:29). John says a voice came from heaven with words, and Jesus says it came for their sakes (John 12:30). The text presents a real act of God, heard by some as words and by others as noise, as happened again when Paul met the risen Christ (compare Acts 9:7 with Acts 22:9; verify).

## 🔴 Tier 3 — Expert
### 🏗️ Theological Framework
John 12 closes the Book of Signs (John 1:19–12:50) and opens the hour. Every thread the Gospel has spun, the signs, the witness, the misunderstandings, the hour, the glory, the light, and the gathering of the nations, is pulled together here and handed on to the passion. Read through the Reformed conviction that Scripture interprets Scripture, the chapter gathers around seven heads.

*The hour and the glory: a theology of the cross.* The hour that was 'not yet' (John 2:4; 7:30; 8:20) now 'is come' (John 12:23), and Jesus calls it the hour in which the Son of man is glorified. In the other Gospels the cross is the humiliation and the resurrection the vindication; John does not deny that order (Philippians 2:8–9), but he joins them in one word. The Son's glory was seen in his signs (John 1:14; 2:11), but it is seen above all where the world sees only shame: 'Now is the Son of man glorified, and God is glorified in him' (John 13:31). Reformed theology, following Scripture, distinguishes Christ's estate of humiliation, which includes his death and burial, from his estate of exaltation, which begins with the resurrection (Westminster Shorter Catechism, Questions 27–28, paraphrased, verify). John's word *lifted up* holds the two together: the lifting on the cross is the first step of the lifting to the Father's right hand (John 12:32; Acts 2:33). Luther's contrast between a theology of glory, which looks for God in power and success, and a theology of the cross, which finds him revealed in suffering and shame (Heidelberg Disputation, 1518, theses 19–21, summarized, verify), is John 12 put in a sentence. The Father answers the Son's prayer, 'Father, glorify thy name', by the cross (John 12:28), so that God's glory, the chief end of all his works (Isaiah 48:11; Romans 11:36), is most fully displayed in the death of his Son.

*The many sides of the atonement.* John 12 shows the cross from four angles at once. It is *fruitful death*: one grain dies so that there may be 'much fruit' (John 12:24), the pattern of Isaiah 53:10, where the Servant, made 'an offering for sin', shall 'see his seed'. It is *victory*: 'now shall the prince of this world be cast out' (John 12:31), what Gustaf Aulén called the classic or *Christus Victor* view of the atonement (*Christus Victor*, 1931, summarized, verify). It is *judgment*: 'Now is the judgment of this world' (John 12:31). And it is *drawing*: lifted up, the Son will draw all men to himself (John 12:32). Reformed theology has held that these belong together, and that substitution is what makes the victory possible: the devil's power over us lay in our guilt, and when Christ bore the curse, the accuser lost his case (Colossians 2:14–15; Hebrews 2:14–15; Revelation 12:10). The grain dies *for* the harvest; the prince is cast out because the debt is paid.

*Prophet, priest, and king.* The Westminster Confession teaches that Christ is the one Mediator, who executes the offices of prophet, priest, and king (chapter 8, section 1, and Shorter Catechism, Questions 23–26, paraphrased, verify). John 12 displays all three. The King enters his city on a colt, as Zechariah foretold, and accepts the title 'the King of Israel' (John 12:13–15), a kingship 'not of this world' (John 18:36). The Priest offers himself: the grain falls into the ground, and he is lifted up 'signifying what death he should die' (John 12:24, 33). And the Prophet speaks the Father's words exactly: 'the Father which sent me, he gave me a commandment, what I should say' (John 12:49). His last public words are prophetic words, and 'his commandment is life everlasting' (John 12:50).

*Divine hardening and human responsibility.* John 12:37–41 is one of the New Testament's clearest statements that God is sovereign over unbelief. The people 'believed not' (John 12:37), and John says they 'could not believe' because God 'hath blinded their eyes, and hardened their heart' (John 12:39–40). Reformed theology calls this judicial hardening: God, as a righteous judge, withholds the grace by which sinners' minds might be enlightened, sometimes withdraws gifts they had, and gives them over to their own desires and to the temptations of the world and Satan, so that they harden themselves even under the means God uses to soften others (Westminster Confession, chapter 5, section 6, paraphrased, verify). The sin remains entirely theirs: in the parallel quotations of Isaiah 6:10, 'their eyes they have closed' (Matthew 13:15; Acts 28:27). And the offer of the gospel still goes out to all: 'whosoever believeth on me should not abide in darkness' (John 12:46). The Canons of Dort insist that the promise of the gospel is to be declared to all without distinction, and that those who perish do so through their own fault, not through any defect in Christ's sacrifice (second head, articles 5–6, paraphrased, verify). The Debate Corner weighs the alternatives.

*The deity of Christ in Isaiah's vision.* 'These things said Esaias, when he saw his glory, and spake of him' (John 12:41). In context 'his' is almost certainly Christ, as nearly all interpreters agree, since John has just spoken of believing 'on him' (John 12:37) and goes on to rulers who 'believed on him' (John 12:42). Isaiah 6 describes the prophet seeing 'the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up', the 'King, the LORD of hosts' (Isaiah 6:1, 5), whose glory fills the earth (Isaiah 6:3). John identifies that glory as the glory of the Son. The same Lord declares, 'my glory will I not give to another' (Isaiah 42:8). If John can say Isaiah saw Christ's glory, then Christ is not another; he shares the one divine glory, which is what John 1:1, 1:14, and 17:5 teach. Some scholars also note that Isaiah uses the language of *high* and *lifted up* both of the throne vision (Isaiah 6:1) and of the Servant (Isaiah 52:13), and that John quotes both halves of Isaiah together here, perhaps because he sees the enthroned Lord and the lifted-up Servant as one person (see Scholarly Insights, Bauckham).

*The sent Son who reveals and judges.* John 12:44–50 gathers John's *sending* Christology into a closing summary. The Son is so fully the Father's agent that faith in him is faith in the Father, and sight of him is sight of the Father (John 12:44–45; 14:9). This is not modalism: the Son and the one who sent him are distinct persons, yet the Son reveals the Father perfectly because he is 'the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person' (Hebrews 1:3). The same words that give life will judge at the last day (John 12:48), so the authority of Christ's words is absolute: to reject them is to reject God. The church receives those words through the apostolic witness the Spirit brought to remembrance (John 14:26; 20:30–31).

*The gathering of the nations.* The Greeks' arrival is the trigger of the hour (John 12:20–23). John has prepared for it: 'the world is gone after him' (John 12:19), 'other sheep I have' (John 10:16), and the gathering 'in one' of the scattered children of God (John 11:52). The lifted-up Son draws 'all men' (John 12:32). Whatever else that phrase means (see View A / View B), it means that the cross breaks the wall between Jew and Gentile (Ephesians 2:14) and gathers a people 'out of every kindred, and tongue, and people, and nation' (Revelation 5:9). The mission of the church to the nations is born at the foot of the cross.

### ⚔️ Debate Corner
**The question:** What does John mean when he says 'Therefore they could not believe' because God 'hath blinded their eyes, and hardened their heart' (John 12:39–40)? "He hath blinded their eyes, and hardened their heart; that they should not see with their eyes, nor understand with their heart, and be converted, and I should heal them." (John 12:40)

**Position 1 · Foreseen and permitted unbelief: God foretold it and gave the people over to what they had freely chosen.** This is the reading of John Chrysostom (verify), of Arminian and Wesleyan theology, and of many others. Holders argue that John 12:37 comes first: they 'believed not' after many signs, by their own choice. The prophecy foretold that unbelief; it did not cause it. 'Could not' describes the moral inability that follows a long refusal, as a man who has shut his eyes long enough cannot see the light. The parallel quotations make the people the actors: 'their eyes they have closed' (Matthew 13:15; Acts 28:27). And God's heart is plain: he has 'no pleasure in the death of the wicked' (Ezekiel 33:11), and in the same chapter Jesus offers light to 'whosoever' (John 12:46). They want to protect God's goodness and the sincerity of the gospel offer.

**Position 2 · Corporate and historical hardening: God hardened Israel as a people, for a time, to open the door to the Gentiles.** Many modern interpreters read the passage chiefly in terms of salvation history (verify particular writers). Holders argue that Isaiah 6 and Isaiah 53 speak of the nation, that John places the hardening immediately after the Greeks arrive (John 12:20–23), and that Paul explains such hardening as 'blindness in part' that has 'happened to Israel, until the fulness of the Gentiles be come in' (Romans 11:25). On this view the passage is not about the eternal destiny of individuals but about why the Messiah was rejected by his own nation and how that rejection served the mission to the world. They want to protect against reading the text as a statement about the Jewish people's permanent rejection, and to keep John's eye on the story of Israel and the nations.

**Position 3 · Judicial hardening: God, as sovereign judge, actively confirmed sinners in the unbelief they freely chose, and he did so to fulfil his purpose.** This is the Reformed reading, held by Augustine in its essentials, by Calvin, and by the Westminster Confession (chapter 5, section 6) (verify each). Holders argue that John does not say merely that God foresaw their unbelief, but that 'they could not believe, because' Isaiah said God would blind and harden them (John 12:39–40). The verbs are active, and God is their subject: 'He hath blinded', 'hardened'. Yet the hardening is a judgment on sin, not the creation of sin in the innocent: it falls on people who had seen 'so many miracles' and 'believed not' (John 12:37), who 'loved darkness rather than light' (John 3:19). God gives them over to what they love (compare Romans 1:24; 9:18; 2 Thessalonians 2:10–11).

**Most consistent with Reformed hermeneutics.** Scripture interpreting Scripture favours Position 3, and it does so by keeping what is true in the other two. (1) *The text makes God the actor.* John does not soften Isaiah; he sharpens it. The Hebrew of Isaiah 6:10 commands the prophet to 'Make the heart of this people fat' (Isaiah 6:10); the parallels in Matthew 13:15 and Acts 28:27 describe the people closing their own eyes; John writes 'He hath blinded' (John 12:40). The three together say that God hardens, the prophet's preaching is the means, and the people are responsible. Position 1 is right about the people's guilt, but cannot account for John's 'could not' and 'because'. (2) *The hardening is judicial, not arbitrary.* Verse 37 comes before verse 39. The people had the light (John 12:35–36) and refused it. God did not take innocent people and make them unbelievers; he confirmed guilty people in their chosen darkness. That is how Paul speaks of God 'giving up' those who suppress the truth (Romans 1:24) and sending 'strong delusion' on those who 'received not the love of the truth' (2 Thessalonians 2:10–11). (3) *The hardening serves God's saving purpose.* Position 2 is right that John places the passage at the turn to the nations, and that the hardening is 'in part' and not final for Israel as a people (Romans 11:25–26). But John applies Isaiah to particular hearers in front of Jesus (John 12:37, 42–43), not only to a nation in the abstract, and he explains their personal unbelief by it. (4) *The offer is still sincere.* In the very next paragraph Jesus cries out to 'whosoever' (John 12:46) and says he came 'to save the world' (John 12:47). The Reformed position does not deny that; it holds, with the Canons of Dort, that the gospel is to be offered to all, that those who refuse it perish by their own fault, and that those who believe do so by grace (second head, articles 5–7, paraphrased, verify). God's sovereignty explains unbelief without excusing it; his offer invites belief without making grace unnecessary.

**Pastoral Warning:** This doctrine is a warning to be heeded, not a label to be applied. Held carelessly, each position harms a church. Position 1, used alone, can leave the impression that God is a spectator of human choices, and can breed a casual confidence that a person may come to Christ whenever he likes: John 12:35–36 says the light may not wait. Position 2, used alone, can turn a searching word into a lecture about other people's history, so that no one in the room feels its edge. And Position 3 can be abused worst of all: by members who decide that a relative, a neighbour, or a people is 'hardened' and stop praying for them, or by tender believers who fear that their struggles prove they are reprobate. The first abuse is forbidden by Jesus' own final cry, which offers light to 'whosoever' (John 12:46); no one on earth is appointed to read another's final state. The second is answered gently: a person who is troubled that they may be hardened, and wants to believe, is showing exactly what hardening removes, and should be pointed to "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." (Matthew 11:28) and to the promise that 'him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out' (John 6:37). Teach John 12:39–40 the way John does: as the explanation of unbelief that has already been shown, followed immediately by the invitation to believe while there is light.

### 🔤 Original Language Deep Dive
Every Greek definition below comes from one source: the brief lexicon published by STEPBible.org (Tyndale House, Cambridge), whose Greek entries are drawn largely from G. Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament*. It is called 'the STEPBible lexicon' below. The Greek text followed is the Textus Receptus behind the KJV, as the tools print it.

**Supper and serving.** *Diēkonei* (G1247, John 12:2, V-IAI-3S), 'served', an imperfect: Martha *was serving* throughout the meal. The same verb returns in Jesus' saying, 'If any man serve me', *diakonē* (G1247, John 12:26, V-PAS-3S), where the noun 'servant' is *diakonos* (G1249, John 12:26, N-NSM), the word from which the English *deacon* comes. John sets Martha's table service within the larger service Jesus asks: following him (John 12:26).

**The ointment.** *Litran* (G3046, John 12:3, N-ASF), 'a pound', a Roman pound of about twelve ounces or 327 grams (verify). *Murou* (G3464, John 12:3, N-GSN), 'ointment', perfumed oil. *Nardou* (G3487, John 12:3, N-GSF), 'spikenard', nard. *Pistikēs* (G4101, John 12:3, A-GSF), an adjective the KJV leaves untranslated or folds into 'spikenard'. The STEPBible lexicon gives its senses as *having the gift of persuasion*, *faithful, trusty* of persons, and, of things, *trustworthy, genuine*, and lists 'genuine nard' here and in Mark 14:3. Some have suggested it is the name of a kind of nard (verify); either way, Mary's ointment was the real thing, not a diluted imitation. *Polutimou* (G4186, John 12:3, A-GSF), 'very costly', of great price. *Ēleipsen* (G218, John 12:3, V-AAI-3S), 'anointed', the ordinary verb for rubbing on oil, not the religious word for anointing a king or priest. *Osmēs* (G3744, John 12:3, N-GSF), 'odour', fragrance; Paul uses the noun for the 'savour' of the knowledge of Christ spread by his servants (2 Corinthians 2:14).

**The thief and the box.** *Kleptēs* (G2812, John 12:6, N-NSM), 'a thief', the same word Jesus used of the one who 'cometh not, but for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy' (John 10:10). *Glōssokomon* (G1101, John 12:6, N-ASN), 'the bag'. The STEPBible lexicon explains that the word first meant a case for holding the reeds or tongues of musical instruments, and then, as in the Greek Old Testament, any box or chest; it lists John 12:6 and 13:29. It was probably a small money box rather than a bag (verify). *Ebastazen* (G941, John 12:6, V-IAI-3S), 'bare'. The STEPBible lexicon gives the ordinary senses *to take up, to bear, to carry*, and then a sense found in later writers, *to carry off, steal*, under which it lists John 12:6 and John 20:15, where Mary Magdalene says to the supposed gardener, 'if thou have borne him hence'. The imperfect suggests a habit: he *used to* carry off what was put in. The KJV's 'bare' is accurate as far as it goes; the context and the lexicon show that John meant pilfering.

**Burial.** *Entaphiasmou* (G1780, John 12:7, N-GSM), 'burying'. The STEPBible lexicon defines it as *preparation for burial* and lists only Mark 14:8 and this verse: both Gospels use this rare word for the same act. *Tetērēken* (G5083, John 12:7, V-RAI-3S), 'hath she kept', a perfect: she has kept it and it stands kept, for this day. See the textual notes below for the other reading.

**Palms, meeting, and Hosanna.** *Baia* (G902, John 12:13, N-APN), 'branches'. The STEPBible lexicon defines it as *a palm-branch*, lists John 12:13 as its only New Testament use, and notes that in the Greek Old Testament it appears at 1 Maccabees 13:51, where the Jews entered the citadel of Jerusalem with palm branches and songs after Simon's victory (a book outside the Protestant canon; the context summarized here, verify). *Phoinikōn* (G5404, John 12:13, N-GPM), 'of palm trees'. *Hupantēsin* (G5222, John 12:13, N-ASF), 'to meet'. The STEPBible lexicon defines it as *a going to meet*, and lists Matthew 8:34 and 25:1 with this verse; in Matthew 25:1 the virgins go out to meet the bridegroom. It was the word for a public welcome. *Hōsanna* (G5614, John 12:13, PRT), 'Hosanna'. The STEPBible lexicon traces it to two Hebrew words, the verb for *save*, *yasah* (H3467), and the particle of entreaty *na* (H4994), which the Hebrew lexicon glosses *I (we) pray, now, please*. It is the cry of Psalm 118:25, 'Save now, I beseech thee, O LORD', turned into acclamation.

**The hour and the glory.** *Elēluthen* (G2064, John 12:23, V-2RAI-3S), 'is come', a perfect: the hour *has come* and is here. *Hōra* (G5610, John 12:23, N-NSF), 'the hour'. The STEPBible lexicon moves from *any time or period fixed by nature* to *a twelfth part of day or night* and then to *a definite point of time*. John's special use belongs to the last: the appointed time of Jesus' death and glory. The noun occurs 25 times in the Greek of John. *Doxasthē* (G1392, John 12:23, V-APS-3S), 'should be glorified' (the tool notes that its data file spells this form differently; the transliteration given is the one that matches the Greek). The STEPBible lexicon lists three senses for *doxazō*: *to think, suppose*; *to magnify, extol, praise*; and, in the Greek Old Testament and the New Testament, *to clothe with splendour, glorify*, under which it lists the Johannine uses of Christ (John 7:39; 8:54). The verb occurs 23 times in the Greek of John. John 12:28 has three forms together: the prayer *doxason* (G1392, John 12:28, V-AAM-2S), 'glorify', an aorist imperative, and the Father's answer, *edoxasa* (G1392, John 12:28, V-AAI-1S), 'I have glorified', and *doxasō* (G1392, John 12:28, V-FAI-1S), 'I will glorify'. Past and future glory meet in the hour. *Phōnē* (G5456, John 12:28, N-NSF), 'a voice', the word for the Father's voice here and for the shepherd's voice the sheep hear (John 10:3–4, 16).

**The grain.** *Kokkos* (G2848, John 12:24, N-NSM), 'corn', a single grain. The STEPBible lexicon defines it as *a grain* and lists, besides this verse, the mustard seed (Matthew 13:31; 17:20) and Paul's sown seed in 1 Corinthians 15:37. *Sitou* (G4621, John 12:24, N-GSM), 'of wheat'. *Pesōn* (G4098, John 12:24, V-2AAP-NSM), 'fall', literally *having fallen*. *Apothanē* (G599, John 12:24, V-2AAS-3S), 'die', the ordinary verb for dying, used of Jesus' own death (John 11:50–51; 12:33). *Monos* (G3441, John 12:24, A-NSM), 'alone'. *Menei* (G3306, John 12:24, V-PAI-3S), 'abideth', John's great verb *to remain, abide*. It appears three times in this chapter with three subjects: the grain that 'abideth alone' if it will not die (John 12:24); the Christ who, the crowd says, *menei* (G3306, John 12:34, V-PAI-3S), 'abideth for ever'; and the believer who should not *meinē* (G3306, John 12:46, V-AAS-3S), 'abide in darkness'. Christ abides for ever precisely because he did not refuse to die; and because he died, believers need not abide in the dark. *Karpon* (G2590, John 12:24, N-ASM), 'fruit', which will become the key word of John 15.

**Two words for life, and hating.** John 12:25 uses *psuchēn* (G5590, John 12:25, N-ASF), 'his life', twice, and then *zōēn* (G2222, John 12:25, N-ASF) *aiōnion* (G166, John 12:25, A-ASF), 'life eternal'. The STEPBible lexicon's first sense of *psuchē* is *breath, breath of life, life*, and it lists John 10:11, where the good shepherd gives his *psuchē*; its second sense is *the soul*, the seat of will, desires, and affections, or the person, the self. *Zōē*, by contrast, is John's word for the life of God given in Christ (John 1:4; 3:16; 20:31). The saying plays on the two: the one who clings to his natural life, his self, loses it; the one who holds it loosely keeps it, transformed into eternal life. *Philōn* (G5368, John 12:25, V-PAP-NSM), 'he that loveth', and *misōn* (G3404, John 12:25, V-PAP-NSM), 'he that hateth', are present participles: a settled way of life, not a single act. The STEPBible lexicon notes that the Hebrew word for *hate* is sometimes used with the modified sense of *indifference to or relative disregard for one thing in comparison with another*, citing Genesis 29:30–31, Deuteronomy 21:15–16, and Malachi 1:3, and says that *miseō* probably carries that sense in certain New Testament sayings, beginning with Matthew 6:24 (the tool shows only the start of that list). That supports reading John 12:25 comparatively, as Luke 14:26 and Matthew 10:37 together suggest. *Apolesei* (G622, John 12:25, V-FAI-3S), 'shall lose', the verb that also means *destroy, perish* (John 3:16; 10:28), and *phulaxei* (G5442, John 12:25, V-FAI-3S), 'shall keep', to guard, keep safe.

**Following and honour.** *Akoloutheitō* (G190, John 12:26, V-PAM-3S), 'let him follow', a present imperative: keep on following. It is the verb of the first call (John 1:43) and of the last (John 21:19, 22). *Timēsei* (G5091, John 12:26, V-FAI-3S), 'will honour'.

**The troubled soul.** *Tetaraktai* (G5015, John 12:27, V-RPI-3S), 'is troubled', a perfect passive: *has been troubled*, and remains so. The STEPBible lexicon lists the verb's metaphorical sense *to trouble, disquiet, perplex*, and sets John 12:27 beside the Greek of Psalm 6, where the psalmist says his soul is troubled (in the KJV, 'My soul is also sore vexed', Psalm 6:3; verify the numbering). The subject is *psuchē* (G5590, John 12:27, N-NSF), 'my soul', the same word as in John 12:25: the one who has just said that the man who hates his *psuchē* keeps it now feels the full weight of laying his own down. *Sōson* (G4982, John 12:27, V-AAM-2S), 'save', an aorist imperative. The Greek has no punctuation marks of its own; editors and translators decide whether to print this as a prayer or a question (see the Council's second question).

**Judgment and the ruler cast out.** *Krisis* (G2920, John 12:31, N-NSF), 'judgment', the word of John 3:19 and 5:22–30. *Archōn* (G758, John 12:31, N-NSM), 'prince'. The STEPBible lexicon defines it as *a ruler, chief*, and lists, among its uses, *members of the Sanhedrin*, including 'the chief rulers' of John 12:42, and *of the devil*, 'the prince of this world' in John 12:31, 14:30, and 16:11. The noun occurs 7 times in the Greek of John. The same word names the rulers who believed but would not confess (John 12:42) and the ruler of the world who is cast out (John 12:31): John does not make the rulers devils, but the word invites the reader to ask whose rule a man is living under. *Ekblēthēsetai* (G1544, John 12:31, V-FPI-3S), 'shall be cast', with *exō* (G1854, John 12:31, PREP), 'out'. The STEPBible lexicon gives *to drive, cast or send out, to expel*, and lists the construction with *exō* at John 6:37, where Jesus promises that the one who comes to him he will 'in no wise cast out'. In the Gospel the Pharisees cast out the healed man who confessed Christ (John 9:34); at the cross it is the world's prince who is cast out, and no one who comes to Christ ever will be.

**Lifted up and drawn.** *Hupsōthō* (G5312, John 12:32, V-APS-1S), 'if I be lifted up', an aorist passive subjunctive, and the crowd's *hupsōthēnai* (G5312, John 12:34, V-APN), 'must be lifted up'. The STEPBible lexicon defines *hupsoō* as *to lift or raise up*, listing John 3:14, 8:28, and 12:32, 34, and gives the metaphorical sense *to exalt, uplift*, listing Acts 2:33 and 5:31, where the risen Christ is exalted to God's right hand, and James 4:10 and 1 Peter 5:6, where God exalts the humble. The verb occurs 5 times in the Greek of John, all of Jesus' lifting up. The lexicon does not say that one use in John combines the two senses; that is an interpretation, but it is one John invites by explaining the saying as a reference to 'what death he should die' (John 12:33) within a Gospel that calls that death his glorification (John 12:23). *Helkusō* (G1670, John 12:32, V-FAI-1S), 'will draw'. The STEPBible lexicon lists literal uses, drawing a sword (John 18:10), a net (John 21:6), or a person dragged off (Acts 16:19; 21:30; James 2:6), and one metaphorical sense, *to draw, lead, impel*, under which it lists only John 6:44 and 12:32. The verb occurs 8 times in the Greek New Testament. The lexicon does not settle the debate of View A and View B, but it shows that John uses the same metaphorical verb in both drawing sayings. *Pantas* (G3956, John 12:32, A-APM), 'all men', is masculine plural, *all persons*; see the textual notes.

**Signs, unbelief, and inability.** *Sēmeia* (G4592, John 12:37, N-APN), 'miracles', John's word 'signs'. *Episteuon* (G4100, John 12:37, V-IAI-3P), 'they believed not', an imperfect: they *went on not believing*. *Ēdunanto* (G1410, John 12:39, V-INI-3P), 'they could', imperfect of *to be able*, negated: they *were not able*. *Tetuphlōken* (G5186, John 12:40, V-RAI-3S), 'he hath blinded', and *pepōrōken* (G4456, John 12:40, V-RAI-3S), 'hardened', both perfects: a blinding and hardening that stands. The STEPBible lexicon explains *pōroō* from a noun meaning *a stone* and *a callus*, and defines the verb *to petrify, harden, form a callus*; it lists John 12:40 for the metaphorical use with 'heart', and Mark 6:52, 8:17, Romans 11:7, and 2 Corinthians 3:14 for others. The picture is of skin that has grown hard and numb by long use. *Epistraphōsin* (G1994, John 12:40, V-2APS-3P), 'be converted', to turn around, and *iasōmai* (G2390, John 12:40, V-ADS-1S), 'I should heal'.

**When he saw his glory.** *Hote* (G3753, John 12:41, CONJ), 'when', the Textus Receptus reading (see the textual notes). *Doxan* (G1391, John 12:41, N-ASF), 'his glory'. The same noun appears twice in John 12:43, *doxan* (G1391, John 12:43, N-ASF), where the KJV translates it 'praise': 'they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God'. The noun occurs 3 times in the Greek of John 12, all in these two verses. John's point is sharp in Greek: Isaiah saw the glory of Christ; the rulers loved the glory of men more than the glory of God. *Ēgapēsan* (G25, John 12:43, V-AAI-3P), 'they loved', the verb of John 3:16 and 3:19.

**Confess and put out.** *Hōmologoun* (G3670, John 12:42, V-IAI-3P), 'they did (not) confess', an imperfect: they *kept on not confessing*. The STEPBible lexicon gives the senses *to speak the same language*, *to agree with*, and *to agree, confess, acknowledge*, and lists John 1:20, where John the Baptist 'confessed, and denied not', and John 12:42 under the absolute use. To confess is to say openly what God says about Christ. *Aposunagōgoi* (G656, John 12:42, A-NPM), 'put out of the synagogue'. The STEPBible lexicon defines it as *expelled from the congregation, excommunicated*. It occurs 3 times in the Greek New Testament, all in John (John 9:22; 12:42; 16:2), and Jesus warns his disciples that it will happen to them (John 16:2).

**Light, rejection, word, and commandment.** *Phōs* (G5457, John 12:46, N-NSN), 'a light', and *skotia* (G4653, John 12:46, N-DSF), 'darkness', the pair of John 1:5. *Athetōn* (G114, John 12:48, V-PAP-NSM), 'he that rejecteth', to set aside, nullify. *Logos* (G3056, John 12:48, N-NSM), 'the word', the word Jesus spoke, which will judge 'in the last day'. *Entolē* (G1785, John 12:50, N-NSF), 'commandment', and *zōē* (G2222, John 12:50, N-NSF), 'life': the Father's commandment to the Son is eternal life.

**Textual notes.** These should be explained calmly, without scorn for either side; none of them changes any doctrine. (1) *John 12:1.* The Textus Receptus reads 'where Lazarus was which had been dead'; many early manuscripts lack *which had been dead* (verify). (2) *John 12:7.* The Textus Receptus reads 'against the day of my burying hath she kept this', with a perfect tense; many early manuscripts read a construction usually translated *that she may keep it for the day of my burial* or *let her keep it* (verify the witnesses). The shorter-text reading raises the question of what was left to keep if she poured it all out; interpreters answer that she kept it for this day, or that the rest was kept for the burial (verify). Either way, Jesus links her act to his burial. (3) *John 12:17.* The Textus Receptus reads 'when he called Lazarus'; some early manuscripts read *that he called*, which changes who the witnesses are (those who had been present, or those who reported that it happened) (verify). (4) *John 12:32.* A few early witnesses read *all things* rather than *all men* (verify the witnesses, which are said to include the papyrus P66 and the first hand of Codex Sinaiticus); the great majority read *all men*, as the Textus Receptus does. (5) *John 12:41.* The Textus Receptus reads *hote*, 'when he saw his glory'; many early manuscripts read *hoti*, *because he saw his glory* (verify). Either way, Isaiah spoke of Christ's glory, and saw it.

### 🚷 Preaching Mistakes to Avoid
- **Information without life:** a sermon on the price of nard, the chronology of Holy Week, the palm branches of the Maccabees, and the punctuation of John 12:27 that never brings the hearers to the grain that fell into the ground for them, or asks them whether they have been drawn to the lifted-up Christ (John 12:24, 32).
- **Heat without light:** a sermon that thunders against the cowardice of the secret believers (John 12:42–43) or the blindness of unbelievers (John 12:40) without first confessing the preacher's own love of the praise of men, and without ending, as Jesus did, with the offer of light to 'whosoever' (John 12:46).
- **The grain as a moral lesson:** preaching John 12:24–25 as a general law of self-sacrifice, *you must die to live*, as if we could bear fruit by dying well. The grain is first Christ. Our losing of life follows his death and depends on it (John 12:26; Galatians 2:20).
- **Hosanna without the colt:** a triumphal Palm Sunday sermon that celebrates Jesus as a king of success and forgets the lowly King of Zechariah 9:9, going to his death; or, the opposite, a sermon so focused on the crowd's fickleness that Christ's kingship is lost (John 12:13–15).
- **John 12:8 as an excuse:** using 'the poor always ye have with you' to justify neglecting the poor, when Deuteronomy 15:11 and the rest of Scripture command open-handed generosity, and the one who pretended to care for the poor was the thief (John 12:6).
- **John 12:40 as a verdict on others:** preaching the hardening passage so that hearers decide who is beyond hope, or so that anxious believers fear they are hardened. Preach it as John does: an explanation of unbelief, a warning to walk while there is light, and a prelude to the cry of John 12:44–50.
- **'The Jews' as villains:** preaching the chapter as a story of Jewish unbelief, when Mary, Martha, Lazarus, the disciples, the believing crowds, and the Lord himself were Jews, and when the warning is aimed at every hearer who loves the praise of men (John 12:11, 42–43).

## 🟣 Tier 4 — Christocentric, Humility, Life & Light
### 👑 Every Answer in Jesus
John 12 demolishes every ground of self-glory. Our devotion is no ground: Mary's pound of ointment was lovely, but its worth was that it was poured on him, for his burial (John 12:7). Our sense of what is sensible is no ground: Judas's calculation sounded wise and was theft (John 12:5–6). Our religious power is no ground: the chief priests could plot against a raised man and could not stop the world going after Jesus (John 12:10, 19). Our enthusiasm is no ground: the crowd shouted the right words and wanted the wrong king (John 12:13). Our understanding is no ground: the disciples did not grasp what they had done until Jesus was glorified (John 12:16). Our standing is no ground: the rulers who believed loved men's glory more than God's (John 12:42–43). And our life itself is no ground, because the man who loves his life loses it (John 12:25).

And every good thing the chapter holds is found in Christ. The worth of worship is his, because it is poured on him. Kingship is his, and he wears it in lowliness (John 12:15). The hour is his, set by the Father and embraced by the Son (John 12:23, 27). Fruitfulness is his, the harvest of the grain that fell into the ground (John 12:24). Glory is his, the glory Isaiah saw on the throne and the glory of the cross (John 12:28, 41). Victory is his, for the prince of this world is cast out (John 12:31). The power to draw sinners is his, not ours (John 12:32). Light is his (John 12:46). Judgment is his, by his word (John 12:48). And life everlasting is his, in the commandment the Father gave him to speak (John 12:50). This is what Paul means when he says of Christ, "In whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." (Colossians 2:3) Judas thought wisdom was knowing the price of things; the rulers thought wisdom was keeping their seats; the crowd thought wisdom was the Christ who never dies. The wisdom of God was a grain of wheat falling into the ground. The chapter humbles us by showing that we are Judas with his calculations, the crowd with its hopes, the disciples with their slow understanding, and the rulers with their secret faith. And it makes Christ glorious by showing him, with his soul troubled, choosing the hour for us and praying only that his Father's name be glorified.

## 📚 Doctrine
### Theology Proper · The Glory of God's Name in the Hour of the Cross
**Proposition:** The chief end of all God's works, and of the Son's mission, is the glory of God's own name. The Father has glorified his name in the Son's life and signs, and glorifies it again, supremely, in the Son's death, resurrection, and exaltation, so that the cross, which looks like God's defeat, is the fullest display of his glory.

**Textual Anchor:** "Father, glorify thy name. Then came there a voice from heaven, saying, I have both glorified it, and will glorify it again." (John 12:28), with the prayer *doxason* (G1392, John 12:28, V-AAM-2S) and the answers *edoxasa* (G1392, John 12:28, V-AAI-1S) and *doxasō* (G1392, John 12:28, V-FAI-1S); and the Son's refusal to be saved from the hour, 'for this cause came I unto this hour' (John 12:27).

**Cross-References:** "For mine own sake, even for mine own sake, will I do it: for how should my name be polluted? and I will not give my glory unto another." (Isaiah 48:11); "These words spake Jesus, and lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said, Father, the hour is come; glorify thy Son, that thy Son also may glorify thee:" (John 17:1); "For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen." (Romans 11:36)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *God's highest goal is human happiness, and his glory is a secondary concern or a kind of vanity.* Many modern people, and some popular preaching, hold this, wanting to present God as wholly for us. But the Son, at the hour of his greatest suffering for us, prays first for the Father's name (John 12:28), and the Father answers from heaven. God's glory and our good are not rivals: the cross that glorifies his name is the cross that saves us (John 12:24, 32). (2) *The cross was a tragic accident or a failure of God's plan that the resurrection repaired.* Some liberal and popular accounts hold this, wanting to take the injustice of the cross seriously. But Jesus says 'for this cause came I unto this hour' (John 12:27), and the Father says the hour glorifies his name. The injustice was real and the purpose was God's (Acts 2:23).

**Pastoral Fruit:** A settled joy that God's glory, not our comfort, is the centre of the universe, and that his glory is shown in rescuing sinners. The courage to pray 'Father, glorify thy name' in our own troubled hours. And worship that sees the cross not only as suffering to be pitied but as glory to be adored.

### Christology · The Hour of Glory: The Cross as the Son's Exaltation
**Proposition:** Jesus Christ's death on the cross was the appointed hour of his glorification: the Son of man was lifted up on the cross and, through it, lifted up to the Father's right hand, so that his humiliation and exaltation meet in one event, and his crucifixion is the throne from which he draws all men to himself.

**Textual Anchor:** "And Jesus answered them, saying, The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified." (John 12:23), with *elēluthen* (G2064, John 12:23, V-2RAI-3S) and *hōra* (G5610, John 12:23, N-NSF); "And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me." (John 12:32), with *hupsōthō* (G5312, John 12:32, V-APS-1S); and John's comment, 'This he said, signifying what death he should die' (John 12:33).

**Cross-References:** "And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up:" (John 3:14); "And being found in fashion as a man, he humbled himself, and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross." (Philippians 2:8); "Wherefore God also hath highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name:" (Philippians 2:9)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus was a good man who was unexpectedly caught up in the politics of his day and executed; the cross was the end of his mission.* Many sceptics and some historians hold this, wanting a Jesus who fits ordinary history. But Jesus announces the hour before it comes, says what death he will die (John 12:32–33), and speaks of it as his glory. (2) *Glory and suffering cannot belong together; the cross was pure humiliation, and only the resurrection was glory.* Many believers assume this, wanting to keep the cross's shame clear. The shame was real (Hebrews 12:2), but John insists that the Son is glorified in the hour itself (John 12:23; 13:31), because in it God's love, justice, and power are revealed. (3) *Christ's glory means that his followers will be spared suffering.* Prosperity teachers hold this, wanting to honour Christ's victory. But the glorified Son goes by a cross, and 'where I am, there shall also my servant be' (John 12:26).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Boasting only in the cross (Galatians 6:14). A new way of seeing our own suffering, not as a sign that God has abandoned us but as a place where his glory can be shown. And confidence that the one who was lifted up is now exalted and still drawing people to himself.

### Christology · The Lowly King of Zion
**Proposition:** Jesus is the King of Israel promised in the Scriptures, the one who comes in the name of the Lord. He fulfils Zechariah's prophecy by coming to Zion lowly, riding on an ass's colt, bringing salvation and peace, and he establishes his kingdom not by force but by his death.

**Textual Anchor:** "Took branches of palm trees, and went forth to meet him, and cried, Hosanna: Blessed is the King of Israel that cometh in the name of the Lord." (John 12:13), with *hōsanna* (G5614, John 12:13, PRT); "Fear not, daughter of Sion: behold, thy King cometh, sitting on an ass's colt." (John 12:15); and the disciples' later understanding, 'when Jesus was glorified' (John 12:16).

**Cross-References:** "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion; shout, O daughter of Jerusalem: behold, thy King cometh unto thee: he is just, and having salvation; lowly, and riding upon an ass, and upon a colt the foal of an ass." (Zechariah 9:9); "Blessed be he that cometh in the name of the LORD: we have blessed you out of the house of the LORD." (Psalm 118:26); "Jesus answered, My kingdom is not of this world: if my kingdom were of this world, then would my servants fight, that I should not be delivered to the Jews: but now is my kingdom not from hence." (John 18:36)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus was a political revolutionary, and the entry into Jerusalem was a bid for national power that failed.* Some historians and activists hold this, wanting a Jesus who challenges earthly powers. He did challenge them, but he chose a colt, not a war-horse, fulfilling a prophecy of a king who would 'speak peace unto the heathen' (Zechariah 9:10), and he said his kingdom is not of this world (John 18:36). (2) *Christ's kingship is only spiritual and inward, with no claim on public life or on the world.* Some pietists hold this, wanting to keep the gospel from politics. But the crowd's title is 'the King of Israel' (John 12:13), and Zechariah's King rules 'from sea even to sea' (Zechariah 9:10). His kingship is not of this world in origin or method, but it is over all the world in extent.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Glad submission to a King who is gentle and lowly. Freedom from the hope that any earthly leader or movement will save us. And a way of serving Christ's kingdom that imitates his lowliness instead of seeking power.

### Christology · The Lord Whom Isaiah Saw, the Son Who Reveals the Father
**Proposition:** Jesus Christ is the LORD whose glory Isaiah saw enthroned in the temple, sharing the one divine glory that God gives to no other. He is also the Son sent by the Father, so perfectly one with him in will and word that to believe on him is to believe on the Father, and to see him is to see the Father.

**Textual Anchor:** "These things said Esaias, when he saw his glory, and spake of him." (John 12:41), with *doxan* (G1391, John 12:41, N-ASF); "Jesus cried and said, He that believeth on me, believeth not on me, but on him that sent me." (John 12:44) "And he that seeth me seeth him that sent me." (John 12:45)

**Cross-References:** "In the year that king Uzziah died I saw also the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up, and his train filled the temple." (Isaiah 6:1); "Jesus saith unto him, Have I been so long time with you, and yet hast thou not known me, Philip? he that hath seen me hath seen the Father; and how sayest thou then, Shew us the Father?" (John 14:9); "Who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power, when he had by himself purged our sins, sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high;" (Hebrews 1:3)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus is a created being, the first and highest of God's creatures, but not the LORD himself.* Arians in the ancient church and Jehovah's Witnesses today hold this, wanting to protect the uniqueness of the one God. But Isaiah saw 'the King, the LORD of hosts' (Isaiah 6:5), who will not give his glory to another (Isaiah 42:8), and John says it was Christ's glory he saw (John 12:41). (2) *Father and Son are one person under two names, so that 'him that sent me' is Jesus himself in another mode.* Modalists (Oneness Pentecostals today) hold this, wanting to protect God's oneness. But the Son speaks of the Father as the one who sent him, commanded him, and spoke to him (John 12:44, 49–50); the sender and the sent are distinct, and yet to see one is to see the other. (3) *We can know God truly apart from Jesus, through nature, conscience, or other religions.* Many today hold this, wanting to respect sincere seekers. Creation and conscience do reveal God's power and law (Romans 1:20), but Jesus says faith in the Father is faith in him, and the Father is seen in him (John 12:44–45; 1:18).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Worship of Jesus with the awe Isaiah felt before the throne. Assurance that when we look at Jesus we are seeing exactly what God is like, with nothing hidden behind him. And boldness in prayer, because the Father we approach is the Father the Son reveals.

### Anthropology & Sin · Hearts That See and Will Not Believe
**Proposition:** The fallen human heart is able to see abundant evidence of God's work and still refuse to believe, because its problem is not a lack of evidence but a love of darkness, of money, of its own place, and of the praise of men. Sin can hide under charitable words, religious office, and secret faith.

**Textual Anchor:** "But though he had done so many miracles before them, yet they believed not on him:" (John 12:37), with *episteuon* (G4100, John 12:37, V-IAI-3P); "This he said, not that he cared for the poor; but because he was a thief, and had the bag, and bare what was put therein." (John 12:6); the plot against Lazarus (John 12:10–11); and "For they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God." (John 12:43)

**Cross-References:** "And this is the condemnation, that light is come into the world, and men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil." (John 3:19); "How can ye believe, which receive honour one of another, and seek not the honour that cometh from God only?" (John 5:44); "For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows." (1 Timothy 6:10)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *People reject Christ only because they have not been given enough evidence; more signs or better arguments would bring everyone to faith.* Many sincere evangelists and many sceptics hold this, wanting faith to be reasonable. Evidence matters, and John records the signs so that we might believe (John 20:31). But the chief priests' answer to a man raised from the dead was to plot his death (John 12:10), and the crowd saw 'so many miracles' and did not believe (John 12:37). The heart, not the evidence, is the problem. (2) *Human beings are basically good, and their motives can be taken at face value.* Much modern thought holds this, wanting to respect people's dignity. But Judas spoke of the poor and meant his purse (John 12:5–6), and the rulers' secret faith gave way to the love of men's praise (John 12:43). Scripture does respect human dignity; it also tells the truth about the heart (Jeremiah 17:9).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Honest self-examination about our own motives, especially where our words sound generous or pious. Freedom from the pressure to win every argument, since faith is God's gift and not only the result of evidence. And a healthy fear of the love of money and the love of praise, which the chapter shows can live close to Jesus and still betray him.

### Soteriology & Grace · The Grain That Dies and the Life That Is Kept
**Proposition:** Eternal life comes to sinners through Christ's death: he is the grain of wheat that falls into the ground and dies, so that from his one death comes a great harvest of redeemed people. Those whom he draws to himself receive that life by faith, and, united to him, they lose their lives in this world for his sake and keep them unto life eternal.

**Textual Anchor:** "Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit." (John 12:24), with *kokkos* (G2848, John 12:24, N-NSM), *apothanē* (G599, John 12:24, V-2AAS-3S), and *karpon* (G2590, John 12:24, N-ASM); "He that loveth his life shall lose it; and he that hateth his life in this world shall keep it unto life eternal." (John 12:25); and the Father's commandment that 'is life everlasting' (John 12:50).

**Cross-References:** "Yet it pleased the LORD to bruise him; he hath put him to grief: when thou shalt make his soul an offering for sin, he shall see his seed, he shall prolong his days, and the pleasure of the LORD shall prosper in his hand." (Isaiah 53:10); "Thou fool, that which thou sowest is not quickened, except it die:" (1 Corinthians 15:36); "For whosoever will save his life shall lose it; but whosoever shall lose his life for my sake and the gospel's, the same shall save it." (Mark 8:35)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *We gain eternal life by our own self-sacrifice; the grain saying is a moral law that anyone can follow.* Moralists of many kinds hold this, wanting to take Jesus' call to self-denial seriously. But the grain is first Christ, whose death alone bears fruit (John 12:24, 32–33); our losing of life is the fruit of his death, not the cause of our life (Galatians 2:20). (2) *Jesus' death was only an example of love, not the means by which life is actually given.* Moral-influence theories hold this, wanting a cross that moves us. It does move us, but Jesus says his death 'bringeth forth much fruit' and 'will draw', words of effect, not only example (John 12:24, 32), and Isaiah says the Servant is 'an offering for sin' (Isaiah 53:10). (3) *Eternal life is only life after death.* Many believers assume this, wanting to keep heaven in view. But 'life eternal' in John begins now for those who believe (John 5:24), and it is kept through death, not only after it (John 12:25).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Assurance that our life rests on his death, not on how well we die to ourselves. Freedom to let things fall into the ground (money, plans, reputation), trusting him with the harvest. And hope for fruit we may never see, as Stephen's death bore fruit in Saul.

### Soteriology & Grace · Judicial Hardening and the Open Invitation
**Proposition:** God is sovereign over unbelief as well as faith. As a righteous judge he blinds and hardens those who persistently refuse the light, confirming them in the darkness they have chosen, so that their unbelief fulfils Scripture and remains wholly their own guilt. Yet the light is freely and sincerely offered to whosoever will believe, and no one who comes to Christ is turned away.

**Textual Anchor:** "Therefore they could not believe, because that Esaias said again," (John 12:39) "He hath blinded their eyes, and hardened their heart; that they should not see with their eyes, nor understand with their heart, and be converted, and I should heal them." (John 12:40), with *tetuphlōken* (G5186, John 12:40, V-RAI-3S) and *pepōrōken* (G4456, John 12:40, V-RAI-3S); set beside "I am come a light into the world, that whosoever believeth on me should not abide in darkness." (John 12:46)

**Cross-References:** "Therefore hath he mercy on whom he will have mercy, and whom he will he hardeneth." (Romans 9:18); "For this people's heart is waxed gross, and their ears are dull of hearing, and their eyes they have closed; lest at any time they should see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and should understand with their heart, and should be converted, and I should heal them." (Matthew 13:15); "Say unto them, As I live, saith the Lord GOD, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live: turn ye, turn ye from your evil ways; for why will ye die, O house of Israel?" (Ezekiel 33:11)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *God only foresees unbelief; he has no active role in hardening anyone.* Arminian and Wesleyan theology holds this, wanting to guard God's goodness. But John says 'they could not believe, because' God 'hath blinded their eyes' (John 12:39–40), and Paul says God 'hardeneth' whom he will (Romans 9:18). See the Debate Corner. (2) *If God hardens, people are not responsible for their unbelief.* Fatalists and some critics of Christianity hold this, wanting to remove guilt or to accuse God. But the hardening falls on those who had already seen and 'believed not' (John 12:37), and Scripture also says 'their eyes they have closed' (Matthew 13:15). (3) *Because God hardens some, the gospel should be offered only to those who show signs of election.* Hyper-Calvinists hold this, wanting to honour God's sovereignty. But Jesus cries out to 'whosoever' (John 12:46) and says he came 'to save the world' (John 12:47); Ezekiel 33:11 shows God's heart in the call.

**Pastoral Fruit:** A holy urgency to walk in the light while it shines (John 12:35–36). Humility, because believers too once walked in darkness and were brought out by grace. Freedom to offer Christ to everyone without calculating who will respond. And comfort for tender consciences: whoever wants to come is welcome (John 6:37).

### Ecclesiology · One People Drawn from the Nations
**Proposition:** Through his death Christ gathers into one people those from Israel and from every nation whom he draws to himself. The church is that gathered people: servants who follow him, who are with him where he is, and whom the Father honours.

**Textual Anchor:** The Greeks' request, "The same came therefore to Philip, which was of Bethsaida of Galilee, and desired him, saying, Sir, we would see Jesus." (John 12:21), answered by 'The hour is come' (John 12:23) and by Jesus' promise to draw all men to himself when he is lifted up (John 12:32), with *helkusō* (G1670, John 12:32, V-FAI-1S); and "If any man serve me, let him follow me; and where I am, there shall also my servant be: if any man serve me, him will my Father honour." (John 12:26), with *diakonos* (G1249, John 12:26, N-NSM).

**Cross-References:** "And other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring, and they shall hear my voice; and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd." (John 10:16); "For he is our peace, who hath made both one, and hath broken down the middle wall of partition between us;" (Ephesians 2:14); "And they sung a new song, saying, Thou art worthy to take the book, and to open the seals thereof: for thou wast slain, and hast redeemed us to God by thy blood out of every kindred, and tongue, and people, and nation;" (Revelation 5:9)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The church belongs to one nation, class, or culture, and others are welcome only if they become like us.* Ethnic and cultural exclusivism in many forms holds this, often unconsciously, wanting to preserve a familiar identity. But the hour comes when Gentiles ask to see Jesus, and his death gathers people from every nation into one (John 12:20–23, 32; 11:52). (2) *Being a Christian is a private matter between the individual and God, and the gathered church is optional.* Many modern believers hold this, wanting to avoid the failures of institutions. But Christ draws people 'unto me', and so to one another, into 'one fold' (John 10:16), and his servants are where he is, together (John 12:26).

**Pastoral Fruit:** A church that welcomes outsiders as Philip and Andrew did, bringing them to Jesus. Joy in the diversity of Christ's people. And a view of service as following Christ together, not performing for others' approval.

### Eschatology · The World Judged, the Prince Cast Out, the Word at the Last Day
**Proposition:** At the cross the world was judged and Satan, the prince of this world, was cast out from his usurped rule, so that his defeat is already decisive though not yet final. At the last day the Son will judge every person, and the words he spoke will be the standard by which those who rejected him are judged.

**Textual Anchor:** "Now is the judgment of this world: now shall the prince of this world be cast out." (John 12:31), with *krisis* (G2920, John 12:31, N-NSF), *archōn* (G758, John 12:31, N-NSM), and *ekblēthēsetai* (G1544, John 12:31, V-FPI-3S); and "He that rejecteth me, and receiveth not my words, hath one that judgeth him: the word that I have spoken, the same shall judge him in the last day." (John 12:48), with *eschatē* (G2078, John 12:48, A-DSF).

**Cross-References:** "And having spoiled principalities and powers, he made a shew of them openly, triumphing over them in it." (Colossians 2:15); "For the Father judgeth no man, but hath committed all judgment unto the Son:" (John 5:22); "Because he hath appointed a day, in the which he will judge the world in righteousness by that man whom he hath ordained; whereof he hath given assurance unto all men, in that he hath raised him from the dead." (Acts 17:31)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Satan still holds unchallenged power over the world, and believers must defeat him by their own spiritual techniques.* Some forms of spiritual-warfare teaching hold this, wanting to take evil seriously. Evil is serious, but the prince was cast out at the cross (John 12:31; Colossians 2:15); believers resist a defeated enemy by trusting the victor (1 John 3:8; James 4:7). (2) *There will be no final judgment; a loving God will simply welcome everyone.* Universalists hold this, wanting to honour God's love. Jesus did come to save and not to judge (John 12:47), but he says plainly that his word 'shall judge him in the last day' (John 12:48), and God has appointed the day (Acts 17:31). (3) *The judgment is wholly future, and nothing is decided now.* Some believers assume this, wanting to keep the last day in view. But Jesus says 'Now is the judgment of this world' (John 12:31): how we respond to him now already reveals where we stand (John 3:18–19).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Courage in spiritual conflict, because the enemy is already cast out. Seriousness about how we hear Christ's words, since they will be the standard on the last day. And urgency in evangelism, because the Judge is also the Saviour who came to save.

### Christian Walk · Serving, Following, and Confessing
**Proposition:** A disciple of Christ serves him by following him wherever he goes, even to loss and death; loves his own life in this world less than Christ; and confesses Christ openly rather than hiding faith for fear of losing the praise of men. The disciple is promised Christ's presence and the Father's honour.

**Textual Anchor:** "If any man serve me, let him follow me; and where I am, there shall also my servant be: if any man serve me, him will my Father honour." (John 12:26), with *akoloutheitō* (G190, John 12:26, V-PAM-3S); 'he that hateth his life in this world shall keep it unto life eternal' (John 12:25); and the warning of the rulers who 'did not confess him' (John 12:42), with *hōmologoun* (G3670, John 12:42, V-IAI-3P).

**Cross-References:** "And he said to them all, If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow me." (Luke 9:23); "Whosoever therefore shall confess me before men, him will I confess also before my Father which is in heaven." (Matthew 10:32); "I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me: and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself for me." (Galatians 2:20)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Faith is private; it is enough to believe in the heart without saying anything.* Many in secular cultures hold this, wanting to avoid giving offence. But John holds up the believing rulers who 'did not confess him' as a warning, not a model (John 12:42–43), and Paul joins confession with the mouth to belief in the heart (Romans 10:9). (2) *Serving Christ means doing great things for him that others will notice.* Many zealous believers assume this, wanting to be useful. But service in John 12 is first following (John 12:26), and it may look like Martha at the table or Mary at his feet (John 12:2–3). The honour that matters is the Father's, not men's. (3) *Hating one's life means despising oneself or seeking suffering for its own sake.* Some ascetics and some troubled consciences hold this, wanting to be wholehearted. But the saying is comparative (see the Deep Dive), and its goal is life, not death: the one who hates his life 'shall keep it unto life eternal' (John 12:25).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Everyday courage to be known as Christ's. A lighter grip on the things of this life. Quiet, unseen service offered to Christ. And the deep comfort of the promise that where he is, his servants will be.

### Assumed Here
- **Pneumatology:** John 12 does not teach about the Holy Spirit directly. It assumes his work when it says the disciples understood the entry into Jerusalem only 'when Jesus was glorified' (John 12:16): John has already explained that the Spirit would be given after Jesus was glorified (John 7:39), and Jesus will promise that the Spirit will bring his words to their remembrance (John 14:26) and glorify him (John 16:14). The effectual drawing of John 12:32 is also, elsewhere, the Spirit's work of new birth (John 3:5–8).

## ⚖️ The Council's Hard Questions
### ❓ Question 1 · One Anointing or Two, and on Which Day
**Moderator:** John says Mary anointed Jesus' feet 'six days before the passover' (John 12:1). Matthew and Mark tell of a woman anointing his head in the house of Simon the leper, and place it after they say the Passover was two days away (Matthew 26:2, 6–7; Mark 14:1, 3). Are these the same event? And if they are, who has the order wrong?

**Historian:** The two accounts share too much to be different events, in my judgment and that of most commentators (verify): the village of Bethany, the days before Passover, a costly ointment of spikenard, a complaint of waste valued at about three hundred pence, the poor, and Jesus' defence of the woman linking her act to his burial (Mark 14:3–9; John 12:1–8). Luke 7:36–50 is different: a Pharisee's house, early in the ministry, a sinful woman, a lesson about forgiveness. The differences between John and Mark are the kind witnesses often have. John names the woman and the grumbler; Mark does not. Mark mentions the head; John the feet. She may well have done both.

**Literary Scholar:** On the timing, look at how Mark arranges his material. He sets the anointing between the chief priests' plot (Mark 14:1–2) and Judas going to them to betray Jesus (Mark 14:10–11). That is one of Mark's framing patterns: two stories set inside each other so that each interprets the other (verify the pattern's frequency in Mark). The woman's lavish gift for his burial stands against the priests' plot and Judas's greed. Mark does not say 'two days before the Passover she anointed him'; he tells the plot at that point and then goes back to the anointing, with a loose 'being in Bethany' (Mark 14:3). John gives a precise date. Most harmonies therefore follow John's timing (verify).

**Church Historian:** The early church knew these differences and was not troubled by them. Augustine, in his *Harmony of the Gospels*, argued that Matthew and Mark relate the anointing in retrospect, and that it happened on the day John gives (verify the reference). He also took Luke's story to be a separate occasion (verify). In the West, the tradition that merged Mary of Bethany with the sinful woman of Luke 7 and with Mary Magdalene grew later and has no support in the text (see Cultural Landmines).

**Theologian:** What matters most is that all three accounts agree on what Jesus said: she anointed him for his burial (Matthew 26:12; Mark 14:8; John 12:7). The Evangelists arrange their material to teach, and they do not contradict each other on the facts they affirm. The doctrine of Scripture's truthfulness does not require every Gospel to follow the same order; it requires that what each affirms is true.

**Resolution:** John 12:1–8 and Matthew 26:6–13 and Mark 14:3–9 are most likely one event, told from different angles, and Luke 7:36–50 is a different occasion. John gives the date, six days before the Passover; Mark and Matthew place the account where it best explains the plot and the betrayal. Mary likely anointed both his head and his feet. All agree on the heart of it: Jesus received her costly love as preparation for his burial. "Then said Jesus, Let her alone: against the day of my burying hath she kept this." (John 12:7)

### ❓ Question 2 · Father, Save Me from This Hour: A Prayer or a Question
**Moderator:** In the KJV Jesus says, 'Now is my soul troubled; and what shall I say? Father, save me from this hour: but for this cause came I unto this hour' (John 12:27). Did Jesus actually pray to be saved from the cross, and then take it back? Many modern versions print it as a question. Which is right, and how does it relate to Gethsemane?

**Philologist:** The Greek manuscripts have almost no punctuation; the decision is the editor's or translator's. The words themselves are an aorist imperative, 'save', in a sentence that begins 'what shall I say'. Grammatically, both readings work. The KJV takes the words as a real petition, followed by 'but'. Many modern editions put a question mark after 'this hour', so that the petition is an option Jesus considers and rejects: *shall I say, Father, save me from this hour? No, for this cause came I.* The Word Study note on 'troubled' is important either way: the verb is a perfect passive, *my soul has been troubled and is troubled*, and the STEPBible lexicon links it with the troubled soul of the psalms.

**Church Historian:** The church has read it both ways. Calvin read it as a real prayer, a sudden cry of Christ's human nature in the face of death, at once checked and corrected by his obedience; he saw no sin in it, because it was the natural shrinking of a true man from death, submitted immediately to God's will (verify). Others, ancient and modern, prefer the question, because it shows Jesus' resolve more clearly (verify particular writers).

**Theologian:** Either way, the doctrine is the same. Christ has a true human soul with true human affections, and the prospect of the cross troubled him deeply, as Hebrews says: he 'offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears unto him that was able to save him from death' (Hebrews 5:7). The historic doctrine that Christ has two wills, divine and human, the human always submitting to the divine (the Third Council of Constantinople, 680–681, summarized, verify), is the church's account of this moment. The human will shrinks from death; the obedient will embraces the Father's purpose. What we must not say is that Jesus wavered in his commitment to go to the cross. 'For this cause came I unto this hour' decides it.

**Pastor:** And for our people, this is enormous. Many believers think that being troubled in the face of suffering is a failure of faith. Here the sinless Son says 'Now is my soul troubled'. He does not pretend. Then he turns it into prayer: 'Father, glorify thy name'. That is a pattern anyone can follow on the worst night of their life: honest trouble, then surrender to God's glory. The Synoptic Gethsemane prayer, 'not as I will, but as thou wilt' (Matthew 26:39), is the same movement at greater length.

**Resolution:** The KJV's punctuation, a real petition followed by submission, is grammatically sound and has good support; many modern editions print a question, which is also possible. Either way, the text shows a true human soul truly troubled, and a will wholly given to the Father: Jesus does not ask to be spared in the end, but that the Father's name be glorified. This scene is John's form of the struggle the other Gospels place in Gethsemane, and it is placed where it is to show that he entered the hour willingly. "Father, glorify thy name. Then came there a voice from heaven, saying, I have both glorified it, and will glorify it again." (John 12:28)

### ❓ Question 3 · Whose Glory Did Isaiah See
**Moderator:** John writes, 'These things said Esaias, when he saw his glory, and spake of him' (John 12:41). Whose glory? In Isaiah 6 the prophet sees the LORD on his throne. Is John really saying Isaiah saw Jesus? And how could he?

**Philologist:** The pronouns point to Jesus. John has just said 'they believed not on him' (John 12:37), and goes on at once to rulers who 'believed on him' (John 12:42). 'His glory' and 'spake of him' in between most naturally mean the same person. The word for glory is the same word John uses of the Son's glory in John 1:14 and 17:5, and it is the word the KJV renders 'praise' in the next verse, where the rulers loved men's glory. I should add the textual note: the Textus Receptus reads 'when he saw', many early manuscripts 'because he saw' (verify). The claim about Christ is the same in both.

**Theologian:** Then John is making one of the strongest statements of Christ's deity in the New Testament. Isaiah saw 'the King, the LORD of hosts' (Isaiah 6:5), the one of whom the seraphim sang 'Holy, holy, holy' (Isaiah 6:3), and that same LORD says 'my glory will I not give to another' (Isaiah 42:8). If Isaiah saw Christ's glory, Christ is the LORD. This is not a new idea in John: the Word 'was God' (John 1:1), and the Son had glory with the Father 'before the world was' (John 17:5). How could Isaiah see him? The same way Abraham 'saw' his day (John 8:56): the Son, who is the eternal Word, was the one who revealed God in the Old Testament, and the prophet saw his glory in vision. The Trinity does not mean that the Father alone appeared in the Old Testament; the Son has always been the one who makes the Father known (John 1:18).

**Literary Scholar:** And look at John's pairing of the quotations. He has just quoted Isaiah 53:1, the opening of the song of the Servant who 'shall be exalted and extolled, and be very high' (Isaiah 52:13), and then Isaiah 6, where the Lord is 'high and lifted up' (Isaiah 6:1). Some scholars, Richard Bauckham among them, argue that John saw the enthroned Lord of Isaiah 6 and the lifted-up Servant of Isaiah 52–53 as one and the same (verify). If that is right, 'his glory' includes the glory of the cross: Isaiah saw the one on the throne who would be lifted up on a cross, and the same people who did not believe the report of the Servant were those whom Isaiah was sent to harden.

**Teacher:** In the group I would read Isaiah 6:1–5 aloud, and then John 12:41, and simply ask: *who is on the throne?* Then read John 1:14 and ask what John saw. Participants will see for themselves that John is saying the Jesus they are studying is the LORD Isaiah worshipped. It turns a doctrine into an act of worship.

**Resolution:** John is saying that the glory Isaiah saw in the temple, the glory of the LORD of hosts, was the glory of the Son, and that Isaiah spoke of him. This is one of the New Testament's clearest testimonies to Christ's deity, and it fits John's teaching that the eternal Word, who shares the Father's glory, is the one who has always made God known. The one on Isaiah's throne is the one lifted up on the cross. "In the year that king Uzziah died I saw also the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up, and his train filled the temple." (Isaiah 6:1)

### 🔦 The Life & Light Audit
**Pastor:** If a hearer obeyed this lesson exactly, then tonight they would read John 12:24–26 and name to Christ one thing they have been keeping for themselves. Within a day they would give something costly to a lonely or shut-in person (a meal, an hour, a gift); take one searching friend or coworker one step toward Jesus; confess privately where they have hidden their faith; say one plain sentence in a place where they usually stay silent; and pray by name for someone they consider hardened, then do them a kindness. What would come alive in them is a loosened grip: joy in giving rather than keeping, the freedom of a disciple who has stopped living for men's praise. And in others, the fragrance of Christ: a lonely person who is remembered, a seeker who is brought to Jesus. The dark place lit would be the hearer's own hidden faith first, and then the darkness of people who 'knoweth not whither' they go (John 12:35).

**Theologian:** The practices keep source discipline. None asks the believer to be the grain that saves; Christ is the grain, and the believer's losing of life is described as following him and trusting him with the harvest (John 12:24–26). The drawing is his, not ours (John 12:32): the believer only brings people to him, as Philip and Andrew did. The Abundance Guard is kept by the chapter itself, which makes the path to life a grain falling into the ground, and the lesson never promises that following Christ will be comfortable. The Light practices turn first on the hearer's own secret faith (Q25, the Inward practices), and the Love Guard is built into the Outward ones: the hard-hearted are prayed for and served, not denounced, because Jesus came 'not to judge the world, but to save the world' (John 12:47).

**Teacher:** A Level 1 participant can do the Inward Life practice tonight: read three verses and pray one sentence. The Outward practices name the kind of person to reach (someone lonely or shut in; someone searching who has asked about Jesus; someone new to church; someone living a closed, fearful life; someone in a conversation where the participant usually keeps quiet; someone they think of as hardened) and leave a blank for the name. The Doing objective is one act of losing life for Christ, on one chosen day.

**Fix:** The Audit found three risks, and the unit addresses them. First, 'He that hateth his life' can be heard as a call to self-contempt or to earning life by sacrifice; so the Cultural Landmines, the Deep Dive, the Christian Walk doctrine, and the Preaching Mistakes explain the comparative sense, and every practice traces the believer's giving to Christ's prior death. Second, the hardening passage can be used to write people off; so the Debate Corner's Pastoral Warning, the Common Errors table, the Light practices (praying for and serving the one thought hardened), and the Shepherding Notes all tie John 12:40 to the offer of John 12:46–47. Third, the secret-believer passage invites hearers to judge others' cowardice; so Q25 and the Inward Light practice turn it on the participant first, and the naming round reads only the second row of the Light Planner, never the private first row.

### ✅ Answer Key
**Q1.** Six days before the Passover, at Bethany, at a supper where Martha served and Lazarus sat at the table (John 12:1–2). Mary took a pound of very costly ointment of spikenard, anointed Jesus' feet, and wiped them with her hair, and the house was filled with the fragrance (John 12:3).

**Q2.** Judas asked why the ointment was not sold for three hundred pence and given to the poor (John 12:4–5). John says he did not care for the poor but was a thief who had the money box and took what was put in it (John 12:6). Jesus said to leave her alone, that she had kept it for the day of his burial, and that they would always have the poor but not always have him (John 12:7–8).

**Q3.** They came not only to see Jesus but to see Lazarus, whom he had raised from the dead (John 12:9). The chief priests plotted to kill Lazarus too, because on account of him many of the Jews were leaving and believing on Jesus (John 12:10–11).

**Q4.** They took palm branches, went out to meet him, and cried, 'Hosanna: Blessed is the King of Israel that cometh in the name of the Lord' (John 12:12–13). Jesus sat on a young ass, fulfilling Zechariah 9:9, 'behold, thy King cometh, sitting on an ass's colt' (John 12:14–15). The disciples understood only after Jesus was glorified (John 12:16).

**Q5.** Certain Greeks who had come up to worship at the feast asked Philip; Philip told Andrew, and they both told Jesus (John 12:20–22). Jesus answered, 'The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified' (John 12:23).

**Q6.** If it does not fall into the ground and die, it remains alone; if it dies, it brings forth much fruit (John 12:24). The one who loves his life will lose it, and the one who hates his life in this world will keep it unto life eternal; whoever serves him must follow him, will be where he is, and will be honoured by the Father (John 12:25–26).

**Q7.** He said his soul was troubled; he asked what he should say, spoke of the prayer 'Father, save me from this hour', and set it aside, because he came for this hour; he prayed, 'Father, glorify thy name' (John 12:27–28). The voice said, 'I have both glorified it, and will glorify it again'; some said it thundered, others that an angel spoke to him (John 12:28–29).

**Q8.** He would draw all men to himself; John says this showed what death he would die, by being lifted up on a cross (John 12:32–33). The crowd had heard from the Scriptures that the Christ abides for ever, and could not see how the Son of man could be lifted up (John 12:34). He urged them to walk while they had the light and to believe in the light, so that they might be children of light (John 12:35–36).

**Q9.** Together they teach that God's King comes in lowliness bringing salvation (Zechariah 9:9); that the Servant is exalted and very high, and yet marred and wounded for others (Isaiah 52:13–53:5); that the Lord whom Isaiah saw high and lifted up is the one his hearers would not understand (Isaiah 6:1, 9–10); that the Son of man must be lifted up as the serpent was, so that believers may live (John 3:14–15); that what is sown must die to be made alive (1 Corinthians 15:36); and that the cross is the Christian's only boast (Galatians 6:14). Jesus' glory comes through his death because his death is the way the King saves, the Servant bears sin, and the grain bears fruit (John 12:23–24, 32–33).

**Q10.** Judas measured the ointment by its price and his own greed; Jesus measured it by love for him and by what was about to happen (John 12:5–7). 'Me ye have not always' means that his death was days away, so this was the one moment for this act (John 12:8). Her pouring out a costly gift is a small picture of Jesus pouring out his own life, 'an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweetsmelling savour' (Ephesians 5:2; John 12:24).

**Q11.** The crowd expected a national deliverer and king, greeted with palms and a victory psalm (John 12:13; Psalm 118:25–26). Jesus showed himself the lowly King of Zechariah 9:9, bringing salvation and peace, on his way to die (John 12:14–15; Zechariah 9:10). The disciples understood only after the cross and resurrection, when the Spirit brought his words to remembrance and they could see the Scriptures fulfilled (John 12:16; 2:22; 14:26).

**Q12.** The Greeks' coming signals that the gathering of the nations, promised in John 10:16 and 11:52, has begun, and that gathering can happen only through Jesus' death (John 12:20–24). His death is the grain that bears much fruit, and his being lifted up draws all men, Gentiles as well as Jews, to him (John 12:24, 32). People 'see Jesus' by looking to him crucified and believing (John 3:14–15; 12:21).

**Q13.** Both views are held by faithful Christians. View A is supported by the context, since Greeks have just asked to see Jesus and 'all men' naturally means Gentiles as well as Jews (John 12:20–21, 32), and by John 6:44, where the same metaphorical verb describes a drawing that ends in being raised up at the last day. View B is supported by the universal language of John 1:9, 3:16–17, and 12:47. The context favours View A: the drawing is effective, and it reaches all kinds of people. Both views must keep the sincere invitation to 'whosoever' (John 12:46). See the Deep Dive and the Theological Framework.

**Q14.** Stephen loved Christ more than his own life, so he spoke boldly and died praying, rather than protect himself (Acts 7:59–60; John 12:25). As he died he saw the Son of man at God's right hand, so he went where his Lord was (Acts 7:56; John 12:26). The fruit he never saw included the scattering of the church and the preaching of the word everywhere, and the conversion of Saul, who watched him die (Acts 8:4; 22:20). Answers to the personal question will vary.

**Q15.** Daniel was protecting his reputation and his comfortable place at work, like the rulers who did not confess Christ for fear of being put out (John 12:42–43). When he spoke, he lost nothing that mattered and gained freedom and joy, and he pointed Priya to Christ (Matthew 10:32). Answers to the personal question will vary; encourage honesty rather than quick promises.

**Q16.** Watts saw glory in the cross, as Jesus called his death the hour of his glorification (John 12:23), and he let go of what he had counted gain, as Jesus calls us to hate our life in this world (John 12:25; Philippians 3:8). Answers to the personal questions will vary.

**Q17.** The cross is God's plan, not a mistake: Jesus came for this hour (John 12:27). It is glory because in it the Father's name is glorified, Satan is cast out, and the Son draws people to himself (John 12:28, 31–32). Like the grain, his death bears much fruit: many are saved through it (John 12:24; Isaiah 53:10).

**Q18.** Answers will vary. He felt the full weight of the cross as a true man and chose it anyway, for us (John 12:27; Hebrews 5:7). Mary's gift shows that love for him is personal, costly, and humble, and does not calculate (John 12:3).

**Q19.** Answers will vary. We humble ourselves by confessing our love of praise and asking God to make his approval our aim (John 5:44; James 4:10). Christ's glory matters more when we remember that he sought his Father's glory, not his own (John 12:28; 8:50).

**Q20.** Answers will vary: time given to someone lonely, money given where no one will know, a plan laid down, a reputation risked by speaking of Christ. Doing it for his glory means doing it quietly and praying 'Father, glorify thy name' (John 12:26, 28; Matthew 6:3–4).

**Q21.** Model answer, not the only answer: planning my week, my money, and my future as if I owned them; worrying about what others think; serving so as to be noticed (John 12:25, 43). Christ is more glorious when we see that he, the Lord of glory, went down into the ground for us, so that everything we have comes from his death (John 12:24; Philippians 2:8–9).

**Q22.** Model answer, not the only answer: a widowed neighbour who has withdrawn from everyone; a relative who has been betrayed and now trusts no one; a friend so absorbed in career or possessions that nothing else matters; someone grieving (John 12:24). Encourage members to write a specific name.

**Q23.** Model answer, not the only answer: generosity (they no longer give because they were cheated); safety (they are afraid); the joy of worship; fruitfulness; the courage to be known (John 12:3, 5, 10, 24, 42).

**Q24.** Model answer, not the only answer: Christ gives himself, the grain that died so they could live, his company, and the Father's honour (John 12:24–26), and he draws people to himself (John 12:32). We withhold his fragrance when we keep our time, hospitality, or money for ourselves rather than pouring it out for someone who needs to see his love (John 12:3; 2 Corinthians 2:15).

**Q25.** Model answer, not the only answer: at work I never mention church or prayer; in my family I avoid spiritual conversations; online I share everything except my faith (John 12:42). I am protecting my image as reasonable, likeable, or professional (John 12:43). The honest answer belongs in the private first row of the Light Planner.

**Q26.** Model answer, not the only answer: a call to forgive someone; a sin I know I should give up; a step of baptism, membership, or service I keep postponing (John 12:35–36). Walking in it means taking one step this week, since the light may not wait (John 12:35).

**Q27.** Model answer, not the only answer: a friend drifting without purpose; a relative who has rejected faith after a hurt; a coworker who mocks Christians (John 12:35). Carry light by praying for them, doing them good, listening, and speaking gently about Christ, offering rather than accusing (John 12:46–47; 1 Peter 3:15).

### 🗺️ Life & Light Leader's Map
**Worked Life Planner Row:** *Who:* 'Gary, my neighbour, whose wife left two years ago; he has stopped answering the door and spends every evening alone with the television'. *What the thief has stolen:* his trust, his hope, his willingness to risk anything, and the sense that anyone cares; he is a grain that 'abideth alone' (John 12:24). *What Christ gives instead:* the one who gave himself so that lonely people might live (John 12:24), and who promises his company to those who follow him (John 12:26). *What I will do:* on Thursday evening, knock on his door with a meal I have cooked, tell him I have been thinking of him, and ask if he would like to come to lunch with my family on Sunday; if he says no, leave the meal and try again next week. *Done?* Tick it when done, and write one line about how it went.

**Worked Light Planner Row:** *Where is it dark (second row):* 'my team at work, where faith is never mentioned and people joke about religion'. *What is hidden there:* my own faith, and my colleagues' questions, which they never ask because no one seems safe to ask (John 12:42–43). *What light Christ shines:* 'I am come a light into the world' (John 12:46), and he came to save, not to judge (John 12:47). *How I carry it there:* on Monday, when someone asks about my weekend, say simply that I went to church and that it helped me with something I have been carrying; do not preach; if someone asks more, answer in two or three sentences; pray for the team by name on the way home.

**When Someone Has No One to Name:** Walk through their circles with them: the neighbour who lives alone; the colleague who never talks about home; the church member who has stopped coming; the relative who has been hurt and withdrawn; the friend who has asked, in some way, 'Sir, we would see Jesus'. Remind them that Philip and Andrew did not create the Greeks' interest; they only brought them to Jesus (John 12:21–22). If they still cannot name anyone, ask them to pray tonight, 'Lord, show me one person who is shut in on themselves', and to bring the name next week.

**When Someone Points the Light at Others:** When a member names a church leader, a politician, or a relative as one of the 'rulers' who hide their faith, or as someone 'hardened', bring them gently back to the first row and to John 12:42–43. Say something like: 'The rulers in John loved the praise of men. Where do I? Let's start there.' Remind them that the Love Guard forbids using the light to shame, that no one on earth can declare another person hardened, and that Jesus' last public words were 'I came not to judge the world, but to save the world' (John 12:47). If the member persists, speak with them after the session.

### 🐑 Shepherding Notes
- **Secret believers in the group.** Some members may be believers whose families, workplaces, or cultures make open confession costly, even dangerous: a spouse who is hostile to faith, a family from another religion, a workplace where faith affects promotion. Do not shame them with John 12:42–43. Honour the real cost, point them to Joseph and Nicodemus, who came into the open at the cross (John 19:38–39), and help them find one safe, wise step, not a reckless one.
- **Those who fear they are hardened.** A tender member may read John 12:39–40 and fear that God has blinded them. Tell them gently that the hardened do not grieve over their hardness; their concern is a sign of God's work. Point them to John 6:37 and John 12:46, and pray with them.
- **The grieving and the bereaved.** 'Six days before the passover' and 'the day of my burying' (John 12:1, 7) bring death close again after John 11. Members who have recently buried someone may find the lesson tender. Let them know that Jesus received Mary's gift for his burial with love.
- **Those who struggle with self-contempt.** 'He that hateth his life' (John 12:25) can wound someone who already hates themselves, or who struggles with depression or thoughts of self-harm. Explain the comparative sense clearly (see Cultural Landmines), and if you have any concern for someone's safety, speak with them privately that day and help them get appropriate care.
- **Those with money worries, and those with wealth.** Mary's pound of ointment and Judas's bag (John 12:3–6) can stir guilt in both the poor and the comfortable. Keep the focus on love for Christ, not on a dollar amount, and on Jesus' defence of the woman, not on criticism of anyone's giving.
- **Members with Jewish family or friends.** Explain that John 12:37–40 is not a verdict on the Jewish people; many believed (John 12:11, 42), and the warning is to every hearer who turns from the light.
- **The Light Planner's first row.** It is private. Never ask anyone to read it aloud or to share it. If someone chooses to speak about their own heart, receive it with gentleness and pray for them; do not probe for details.

### 🎯 Teaching Angles
**For a Tier 1 Group:** Bring a packet of seeds and, if possible, a small plant or an ear of wheat. Hold up one seed and ask what will happen if you keep it in the packet for a year. Then ask what happens when you plant it. Read John 12:24 together. Ask: who is the seed Jesus was talking about? Show the plant or the ear of wheat and say, *This is what came from one seed.* Then give each person a seed to take home and plant as a reminder. Ask: what is one thing you could give away this week for Jesus, like the seed that is planted? Close by reading John 12:46 and asking what it means that Jesus is the light.

**For a Tier 2 Group:** Trace three lines through the chapter. The line of glory: 'glory' and 'glorified' and their forms occur 6 times in the KJV of John 12 (John 12:16, 23, 28, 41); ask who glorifies whom, and how the cross can be called glory. Then note that the Greek word behind 'glory' in John 12:41 is also behind 'praise' in John 12:43 (the Deep Dive), and ask what the rulers loved instead of Christ's glory. The line of light: 'light' occurs 6 times in the KJV of John 12 (John 12:35–36, 46); ask what Jesus urges, why it is urgent, and how the chapter's last public words change the tone. The line of belief: 'believe' and its forms occur 10 times in the KJV of John 12; list who believes, who does not, and who believes but hides it (John 12:11, 36–39, 42, 44, 46–47). Then set John 12:23 beside John 2:4, 7:30, and 8:20, where 'hour' is 'not yet', and read John 13:1 and 17:1 to see where it leads; 'hour' occurs 3 times in the KJV of John 12.

**Tough Question 1:** *If God blinded their eyes, how is it fair to blame them for not believing?* John says they had already seen many signs and 'believed not' (John 12:37); the hardening was God's judgment on people who had refused the light, not something forced on innocent people. Scripture holds both truths: God hardens, and people close their own eyes (John 12:40; Matthew 13:15). And Jesus' very next words invite 'whosoever' to believe (John 12:46). See the Debate Corner.

**Tough Question 2:** *Did Jesus really want to get out of going to the cross?* As a true man, he felt the horror of the cross, and his soul was troubled (John 12:27; Hebrews 5:7). The KJV prints 'Father, save me from this hour' as a prayer, which he at once sets aside; many versions print it as a question. Either way, his will was set: 'for this cause came I unto this hour', and his prayer was 'Father, glorify thy name' (John 12:27–28). See the Council's second question.

**Tough Question 3:** *Does Jesus draw everyone, or only some? And if he draws only some, what about the rest?* The context is the Greeks' request, and 'all men' includes Gentiles as well as Jews (John 12:20–21, 32). John 6:44 uses the same verb for a drawing that ends in resurrection. Many Christians, including the Reformed tradition, read the drawing as effectual for all kinds of people; others read it as a universal pull that can be resisted. Both agree that everyone who comes is received (John 6:37), and that Christ's invitation goes to 'whosoever believeth' (John 12:46). See View A / View B and the Deep Dive.
