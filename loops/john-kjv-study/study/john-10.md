# John 10 · The Good Shepherd (KJV)
> Unit john-10 · John 10:1–42 · loop john-kjv-study v2.0 · DRAFT for human doctrine review

## 🧭 At a Glance
### 🧵 Golden Thread
Jesus is the door and the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep and keeps them so that none can pluck them from his hand, and he is one with the Father.

### 🌗 Life & Light Line
John 10 answers the death that thieves, robbers, and hirelings bring on God's flock: sheep climbed in upon by men who come 'for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy' (John 10:10), left to the wolf by leaders who 'careth not for the sheep' (John 10:13), and, in the chapter just before, a healed man thrown out by the very shepherds of Israel (John 9:34). Christ gives instead his own life, laid down of himself and taken again (John 10:17–18), and with it a life that is abundant and eternal, held in two hands that no one can open (John 10:10, 28–29). The chapter exposes the darkness of people who circle Jesus and demand that he 'tell us plainly' (John 10:24) while refusing the works that already speak, and who pick up stones rather than hear (John 10:31); the light Christ gives instead is his own voice, which his sheep know and follow (John 10:4, 27), and the works that show 'the Father is in me, and I in him' (John 10:38).

### 🎯 Objectives
- Contrast the good shepherd with the thief, the robber, and the hireling (John 10:1–15), naming from the text what each one wants from the sheep and what each one gives.
- State the promise of John 10:27–29 in your own words, explaining who holds the sheep, what they are given, and why the chapter grounds their safety in the Shepherd and the Father rather than in the sheep.
- **Doing:** Within the next day, read John 10:27–29 aloud to one believer who is afraid, discouraged, or wandering, and tell them in one plain sentence whose hand holds them.

## 🏛️ Core Foundations
### ✝️ Christological Root
**Gospel Foundation:** John 10 rests on what Jesus says he is and what he says he will do. He says, "I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep." (John 10:11) He says his death will be his own act, not something done to him against his will: "No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again. This commandment have I received of my Father." (John 10:18) And he says what his death and life secure for the sheep: "And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand." (John 10:28) Behind all three stands the claim that sends the crowd for stones: "I and my Father are one." (John 10:30)

**The Bridge:** In John 9 the shepherds of Israel cast out a sheep that Jesus had healed, and Jesus went and found him (John 9:34–35). John 10 follows without a break and explains what the reader has just watched. The leaders are thieves and hirelings; Jesus is the door of the fold and the shepherd who calls his own by name. The doctrines grounded here are Christ the only door of salvation (John 10:7, 9); his voluntary, substitutionary death for his sheep and his power to rise (John 10:11, 15, 17–18); the Father's gift of the sheep to the Son, and the Son's knowing and calling of his own (John 10:3, 14, 26–29); the eternal security of the sheep in the hands of the Son and the Father (John 10:28–29); the one flock gathered from Israel and the nations under one shepherd (John 10:16); and the unity of the Father and the Son, which the leaders rightly heard as a claim to be God (John 10:30, 33, 38).

**Canonical Echoes:** Jesus (John 10:11) → Promise (Psalm 23:1; Isaiah 40:11; Ezekiel 34:11–16, 23) → Doctrine (Hebrews 13:20; 1 Peter 2:25) → Life (1 Peter 5:4; Revelation 7:17)

**What Gets Lost:** Taught without Christ at the centre, John 10 becomes a lesson in leadership, a pastoral image to hang on a nursery wall, or a proof text for one side of a debate. The good shepherd becomes a model manager who knows his team by name. John 10:10 becomes a promise of a richer, easier life. John 10:28 becomes a slogan that lets a person live as he likes, or a riddle that makes a tender conscience afraid. With Christ at the centre, every piece holds. The shepherd is good because he dies for the sheep; the sheep are safe because he rose again and holds them; the voice they follow is his; and the reason his hearers took up stones is that the shepherd of Psalm 23, the LORD himself, was standing in Solomon's porch and saying, 'I and my Father are one'.

### ⚔️ Spiritual Warfare
**The Lie:** You belong to whoever feeds you, and anyone who sounds like a shepherd is one. The lie comes in three voices in this chapter. The thief says: *follow me, and I will give you what you want*, while he climbs in 'some other way' (John 10:1) to take what the flock can give him. The hireling says: *I will look after you*, and runs at the first sight of the wolf (John 10:12). And the accuser says to the sheep that Christ has found: *you are not safe; one bad day and you will slip through his fingers.*

**The Truth:** "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me:" (John 10:27) "My Father, which gave them me, is greater than all; and no man is able to pluck them out of my Father's hand." (John 10:29)

**The Battleground:** The ear, the heart's sense of safety, and the leadership of the church. The ear is the first ground: every day many voices call, and the sheep must learn which one is their shepherd's (John 10:4–5). The heart is the second: the fear of being lost, which the enemy uses to drive believers into despair or into trying to hold themselves in Christ's hand by their own grip (John 10:28). And the church's leadership is the third: the places where men who should be shepherds act as hirelings or thieves (John 10:10, 12–13; Acts 20:29).

**The Weapon:** The Shepherd's own voice, heard in his word and believed. Jesus answered the thief and the hireling not with a technique but with himself: 'I am the door' (John 10:9), 'I am the good shepherd' (John 10:11). The sheep resist the stranger by knowing the true voice so well that the false one sounds wrong: 'a stranger will they not follow' (John 10:5). And they meet the accuser with the Shepherd's promise, which rests on his hand and not on theirs: 'neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand' (John 10:28).

### 🌱 Life Pathway
**The Theft:** In John 10 the thief has a face. He is the one who 'climbeth up some other way' (John 10:1) into a fold that is not his, to take what the sheep can give: their wool, their meat, their loyalty, their money, their admiration. He comes 'for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy' (John 10:10). Beside him stands the hireling, who does not steal outright but does something almost as deadly: he works for pay, 'whose own the sheep are not', and when the wolf comes he 'leaveth the sheep, and fleeth' (John 10:12). The flock ends up caught and scattered. The reader has just seen what this looks like in real life: the man Jesus healed was interrogated, reviled, and cast out by the men who should have fed him (John 9:28, 34). The thief steals belonging, safety, and pasture, and in the end he takes life itself.

**The Source:** Christ, who says, "The thief cometh not, but for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy: I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly." (John 10:10) His means in this chapter are his voice, calling each of his own by name (John 10:3); his leading, going before them (John 10:4); the door, which is himself, through whom they go in and out and find pasture (John 10:9); above all his death, laid down for the sheep, and his resurrection, taken up again (John 10:11, 17–18); and his gift of eternal life, which he gives and keeps in his hand (John 10:28).

**The Channel:** Life passes to others through a believer who hears the Shepherd's voice and becomes, under him, an under-shepherd: one who feeds instead of fleecing, stays instead of fleeing, and knows people by name. Peter, who heard this chapter, was later told three times to feed Christ's sheep (John 21:15–17), and he passed the charge on to other elders: 'Feed the flock of God which is among you' (1 Peter 5:2). The believer is never the shepherd of anyone's soul in Christ's place; the sheep are always 'my sheep', his (John 10:27). We are channels of his care: "for without me ye can do nothing." (John 15:5)

**The Abundance Guard:** Abundant life is fullness of life in Christ, not prosperity, ease, or freedom from suffering. John 10:10 is the verse most often bent into a promise of money and comfort, yet the very next verse says that this abundance comes through the shepherd's death (John 10:11), and the chapter ends with Jesus threatened with stones and escaping arrest (John 10:31, 39). The sheep of John 10 still meet wolves; the promise is that the wolf cannot take them out of his hand (John 10:12, 28). Psalm 23, behind this chapter, walks 'through the valley of the shadow of death' (Psalm 23:4) with the shepherd, not around it.

### 💡 Light Pathway
**The Darkness:** The darkness of John 10 is a darkness of the ear. Some of Jesus' hearers 'understood not' what he said (John 10:6). Others decided that he 'hath a devil, and is mad' (John 10:20). At the feast of the dedication the leaders surround him and say, 'How long dost thou make us to doubt' (John 10:24), as if the problem were his lack of plainness, when he has already told them and shown them (John 10:25). Jesus names the root: 'ye believe not, because ye are not of my sheep' (John 10:26). And when he speaks most plainly of all, 'I and my Father are one' (John 10:30), they reach for stones (John 10:31). There is a second darkness too, the darkness of false voices, strangers and thieves who come dressed as shepherds (John 10:5, 8).

**The Source:** Christ, who says, "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me:" (John 10:27) "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." (2 Corinthians 4:6)

**The Lamp:** In John 10 the lamp is the Shepherd's voice and the Shepherd's works. His voice is his word, spoken plainly and repeated (John 10:7, 11, 14, 25). His works are the signs done 'in my Father's name' that 'bear witness of me' (John 10:25), such as the blind eyes just opened (John 10:21). The Scriptures are a lamp too: Jesus answers the charge of blasphemy by opening Psalm 82 and insisting that 'the scripture cannot be broken' (John 10:35). For us the lamp is the same: Scripture opened and obeyed, the voice of the Shepherd learned so well that strangers' voices are exposed, hidden sin confessed, the unfruitful works of darkness reproved (Ephesians 5:11), and good works that glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16).

**The Love Guard:** Light without love is glare. Jesus spoke sharply about thieves, robbers, and hirelings (John 10:1, 8, 12), but he said it to leaders who had just thrown a helpless man out (John 9:34), and he said it while offering himself as the door through which 'if any man enter in, he shall be saved' (John 10:9). Even to men holding stones he spoke of 'good works' and appealed to them to believe (John 10:32, 38). The good shepherd exposes the thief in order to protect the sheep, not to shame them, and he never uses the truth as a stick to beat the flock. Christ's light is always the light of one who lays down his life for those he corrects.

### 🏺 Setting the Scene
John 10 follows straight on from John 9 with no change of scene. Jesus has just told the Pharisees who cast out the healed man, 'your sin remaineth' (John 9:41), and he goes on speaking to them: 'Verily, verily, I say unto you' (John 10:1). The healed man is the proof in front of everyone's eyes of what the chapter describes: the shepherds of Israel threw a sheep out, and the true shepherd went and found him (John 9:34–35). That first part of the chapter (John 10:1–21) belongs to the season after the feast of tabernacles in the autumn (John 7:2). Then John moves the scene forward about two months, to 'the feast of the dedication' in 'winter' (John 10:22).

Shepherding was part of everyday life in Israel. At night sheep were kept in a fold, an enclosure with a wall of stone or thorns and one opening; sometimes several flocks shared a fold, with a porter or doorkeeper at the entrance (verify the details for first-century Judea). In the morning each shepherd came to the door and called his own sheep, and they came to his voice and followed him out to pasture; travellers in the Middle East have long described shepherds calling their sheep by name and leading rather than driving them (verify). The Old Testament had already used this picture for God and his people: 'The LORD is my shepherd' (Psalm 23:1); 'Give ear, O Shepherd of Israel' (Psalm 80:1). It used it for Israel's kings and leaders too, and in Ezekiel 34 God condemns the shepherds of Israel who fed themselves and not the flock, and promises to seek his scattered sheep himself and to set over them 'one shepherd' (Ezekiel 34:2, 11–12, 23).

The feast of the dedication (what Jewish people today call Hanukkah) remembered the day in 164 BC when Judas Maccabeus and his men cleansed and rededicated the temple after the Syrian king Antiochus IV Epiphanes had defiled it with pagan sacrifice. The story is told in 1 Maccabees 4, a book of the Apocrypha, not of the Bible (verify the date and details). It was an eight-day winter feast, and the Jewish historian Josephus calls it 'Lights' (verify). It carried memories of a leader who drove out the enemy and set the temple right, which helps explain the question the leaders press on Jesus in Solomon's porch, a covered colonnade on the east side of the temple courts (Acts 3:11; 5:12): 'If thou be the Christ, tell us plainly' (John 10:24). Listen as you read for three things: whose voice the sheep follow, what the shepherd gives that the thief takes, and what the leaders hear Jesus claiming about himself and the Father.

**View A:** When Jesus quotes 'I said, Ye are gods' (John 10:34), the 'gods' of Psalm 82 are the judges and rulers of Israel. God had given them his own work to do, judging in his name, and so Scripture could call them by a title of authority; yet they judged unjustly, and God rebukes them: "How long will ye judge unjustly, and accept the persons of the wicked? Selah." (Psalm 82:2) On this view Jesus argues: if Scripture could call unjust judges 'gods' because God's word gave them his office, how much more can the one the Father set apart and sent call himself the Son of God (John 10:35–36).

**View B:** The 'gods' of Psalm 82 are the people of Israel as a whole, who received the word of God at Sinai: "He sheweth his word unto Jacob, his statutes and his judgments unto Israel." (Psalm 147:19) Jesus' own phrase points this way: they are called gods 'unto whom the word of God came' (John 10:35). Later Jewish teachers read the psalm of Israel at Sinai (verify the rabbinic sources). On this view Jesus argues: if Scripture could call people 'gods' merely because God's word came to them, how much more the one who is himself sent from the Father into the world.

### 👣 Case Study · Scripture in Action
Simon Peter heard the good shepherd say, 'I lay down my life for the sheep' (John 10:15). A few months later he tried to turn it around: "Peter said unto him, Lord, why cannot I follow thee now? I will lay down my life for thy sake." (John 13:37) That same night he denied three times that he knew Jesus (John 18:17, 25–27). If any sheep ever looked as though it had slipped out of the Shepherd's hand, it was Peter. But the Shepherd had already prayed for him: "But I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not: and when thou art converted, strengthen thy brethren." (Luke 22:32) After the resurrection Jesus found Peter by the lake, as he had found the man born blind, and called him by name three times: 'Simon, son of Jonas, lovest thou me' (John 21:15–17). He did not only restore Peter as a sheep; he made him a shepherd: "He saith to him again the second time, Simon, son of Jonas, lovest thou me? He saith unto him, Yea, Lord; thou knowest that I love thee. He saith unto him, Feed my sheep." (John 21:16) Years later Peter wrote to scattered believers that they had been 'as sheep going astray' and were now 'returned unto the Shepherd and Bishop of your souls' (1 Peter 2:25), and he told the elders to feed the flock, not as hirelings, 'not for filthy lucre, but of a ready mind' (1 Peter 5:2). Set Peter beside Judas, who walked with the same Shepherd and was lost (John 17:12), and the difference is not that Peter was stronger. It is that Peter was one of the Shepherd's sheep, and the Shepherd kept him.

### ⛪ Doctrine in My Own Words
- **Theology Proper:** The Father and the Son are one God, distinct persons who share one life, one power, and one work, so that the Father is in the Son and the Son in the Father (John 10:30, 38).
- **Christology:** Jesus is the good shepherd who laid down his life for his sheep by his own will, as the Father commanded, and took it up again by his own power (John 10:11, 17–18).
- **Christology:** Jesus is the door: the one way into God's fold, through whom anyone who enters is saved (John 10:7, 9).
- **Anthropology & Sin:** Human beings are sheep who cannot shepherd themselves, preyed on by thieves and hirelings, and those who are not his sheep will not believe him even when they see his works (John 10:10, 12, 26).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** The Father gave the Son his sheep, and the Son knows them, calls them by name, gives them eternal life, and brings in others from outside Israel's fold (John 10:3, 14, 16, 29).
- **Soteriology & Grace:** Christ's sheep will never perish, because the Son and the Father hold them and no one can snatch them away (John 10:28–29).
- **Ecclesiology:** There is one flock under one shepherd, and those who lead it must feed it as under-shepherds, not fleece it as thieves or abandon it as hirelings (John 10:12, 16).
- **Christian Walk:** Christ's sheep hear his voice, know it from the voice of strangers, and follow him (John 10:4–5, 27).

### 👑 Christocentric & Humility · Level 4
Every problem in John 10 is answered in Jesus. The sheep are exposed to thieves and robbers who climb in some other way; he is the door, and the only way in (John 10:1, 9). They are scattered by the wolf while the hireling runs; he is the shepherd who stays and dies (John 10:11–12). They are nameless to the men who use them; he calls his own by name (John 10:3). Some of his sheep are outside Israel's fold; he must bring them (John 10:16). Death threatens the shepherd himself; he lays his life down of himself and takes it again (John 10:18). The leaders demand a plain answer; he gives it in words and works (John 10:25, 30). The sheep are weak and afraid; they are held in two hands that no one can open (John 10:28–29). And when the leaders reach for stones because a man makes himself God, the chapter shows the truth the other way round: God, in the Son, had made himself a man, to be the shepherd who dies for his flock (John 1:14; 10:33).

Every truth in the chapter is found in him. Salvation is in him, the door (John 10:9). Life, abundant and eternal, is his to give (John 10:10, 28). Knowledge of God is in him, who knows the Father as the Father knows him (John 10:15). Unity is in him, the one shepherd of the one flock (John 10:16). Safety is in him and in the Father, who are one (John 10:28–30). The chapter humbles every reader, because it calls us sheep: not lions, not shepherds of ourselves, but animals that need to be led, fed, and kept, and that cannot keep themselves. It humbles every leader, because it measures shepherds by whether they would die for the flock. And it makes Christ glorious, because the one who is one with the Father stooped to be the shepherd who is killed for the sheep.

**Why this verse:** It is the Lesson Map's choice for this lesson, and it holds the whole chapter in one sentence. It is one of Jesus' 'I am' sayings, which run through John's Gospel and echo the name of God (John 8:58; Exodus 3:14). ## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy
### 📌 Core Concept
In Bible times a shepherd kept his sheep at night in a sheepfold, a pen with a wall around it and one opening. In the morning he came to the opening, called his sheep, and they came to him, because they knew his voice. He even had a name for each one. Then he walked in front of them and led them to grass and water. Jesus said, *I am like that shepherd, and you can be my sheep.* He said he is the good shepherd, and that a good shepherd is so good that he will die to save his sheep. That is exactly what Jesus did on the cross. But he also said that nobody could take his life from him: he would give it, and then he would take it back again, and he did, when he rose from the dead. Jesus also warned about thieves, who sneak into the pen to steal sheep, and about hired helpers who run away when a wolf comes because they don't really care about the sheep. Jesus is not like them. He knows his sheep by name, he gives them life that never ends, and he holds them in his hand so tightly that nobody can snatch them away. And he said that his Father holds them too, and that he and his Father are one. Some people got so angry at that that they picked up stones to throw at him, because they understood he was saying he is God.

### 📖 Key Verses
"The thief cometh not, but for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy: I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly." (John 10:10) Jesus did not come to take things from us, but to give us life, full and overflowing.

"I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep." (John 10:11) Jesus loves his sheep so much that he died for them on the cross.

"My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me:" (John 10:27) Jesus' sheep listen to him, he knows each one, and they go where he leads.

"And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand." (John 10:28) Nobody is strong enough to take one of Jesus' sheep away from him.

### 🪞 Analogy
Imagine a big, noisy playground after school, with dozens of children shouting and running around. A mother comes to the gate and calls out her little girl's name. She doesn't shout very loudly, and there are lots of other grown-ups calling too. But her daughter stops in the middle of a game, turns around, and runs straight to her. Why? Because she knows that voice better than any other voice in the world, and she knows that voice loves her. A stranger could call her name too, but she would not run to a stranger. That is what Jesus means when he says his sheep hear his voice and follow him. And once the little girl has her hand in her mother's hand, she is safe, not because she is holding on so tightly, but because her mother is holding on to her.

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium
### 📜 Walkthrough
#### John 10:1–5 · The Shepherd Who Enters by the Door
**Says:** "Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that entereth not by the door into the sheepfold, but climbeth up some other way, the same is a thief and a robber." (John 10:1) "But he that entereth in by the door is the shepherd of the sheep." (John 10:2) "To him the porter openeth; and the sheep hear his voice: and he calleth his own sheep by name, and leadeth them out." (John 10:3) "And when he putteth forth his own sheep, he goeth before them, and the sheep follow him: for they know his voice." (John 10:4) "And a stranger will they not follow, but will flee from him: for they know not the voice of strangers." (John 10:5) *Verily* means truly; *sheepfold* is a walled pen for sheep; *porter* means doorkeeper; *putteth forth* means brings out.

**Means:** There is no break between John 9 and John 10. Jesus is still speaking to the Pharisees who said 'We see' (John 9:40–41), and the doubled 'Verily, verily', Jesus' solemn way of introducing a weighty saying in John, does not begin a new scene but presses home what has just happened. Those men had judged, reviled, and thrown out a man whose eyes Jesus had opened (John 9:28, 34). Now Jesus draws a picture that every hearer knew from daily life. There is a fold, and it has a door. The shepherd uses the door; he has a right to the sheep, the porter knows him, and he does not need to sneak in. Anyone who 'climbeth up some other way' has no right there at all, and he is 'a thief and a robber'. The two words are not quite the same: the first is one who steals by stealth and cunning, the second one who plunders by force (the STEPBible lexicon draws this distinction; see the Deep Dive). Leaders can do both: they can take from God's people quietly, by manipulation and flattery, or openly, by threat and power, as the synagogue court had just done.

The heart of the picture is the relation between the shepherd and the sheep. 'The sheep hear his voice'; he 'calleth his own sheep by name'; he 'leadeth them out'. When he brings them out, 'he goeth before them', so that they follow instead of being driven, and they follow because 'they know his voice'. A stranger's voice has the opposite effect: they 'will flee from him'. John means us to hear an echo of the chapter before. The verb translated 'putteth forth' in John 10:4 is the same Greek verb translated 'cast out' in John 9:34 (see the Deep Dive). The Pharisees cast the man out of the synagogue in anger; the shepherd brings his own sheep out of the fold in love, to lead them to pasture. The healed man is the first example: he had never seen Jesus when he was sent to Siloam, yet he knew the voice that found him (John 9:35–38).

Readers have asked who the porter is: the Father who gives the sheep to the Son (John 6:37), John the Baptist who opened the way (John 1:31), or the Holy Spirit. John does not say, and it is wiser to read the porter as part of the scene, the doorkeeper who recognises the true shepherd, than to press every detail into a separate meaning.

**Asks:** The sheep are safe from strangers not because they have studied every false voice, but because they know the true one so well. How well do you know your Shepherd's voice in his word? Could you tell if someone were speaking in his name what he never said?

#### John 10:6–10 · I Am the Door
**Says:** "This parable spake Jesus unto them: but they understood not what things they were which he spake unto them." (John 10:6) "Then said Jesus unto them again, Verily, verily, I say unto you, I am the door of the sheep." (John 10:7) "All that ever came before me are thieves and robbers: but the sheep did not hear them." (John 10:8) "I am the door: by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved, and shall go in and out, and find pasture." (John 10:9) "The thief cometh not, but for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy: I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly." (John 10:10) *Parable* here means a figure of speech, a veiled saying; *pasture* is grazing land.

**Means:** 'This parable spake Jesus unto them'. John never uses the word the other Gospels use for Jesus' parables. His word here means a figure of speech, a saying whose meaning is veiled until it is explained (see the Deep Dive). 'But they understood not'. John has shown us this again and again: Nicodemus did not understand new birth (John 3:4), the Samaritan woman thought of well water (John 4:11), the crowd in Capernaum could not see how he could give them his flesh (John 6:52), and in the temple 'They understood not that he spake to them of the Father' (John 8:27). The men who claimed to see do not understand a simple picture of sheep, because they do not see themselves in it.

So Jesus says it again, more plainly, and with the words that mark his great sayings in John: 'I am the door of the sheep'. This is the third of the 'I am' sayings with an image, after 'the bread of life' (John 6:35) and 'the light of the world' (John 8:12). In John 10:1–5 the door was the right way for a shepherd to come in; now Jesus says he is the door himself. There is no other way into God's fold, no other entrance to salvation, and no other right to lead God's sheep, than through him. Later he will say it without a picture: 'no man cometh unto the Father, but by me' (John 14:6).

'All that ever came before me are thieves and robbers'. Jesus does not mean the prophets or John the Baptist, who came to point to him (John 5:39, 46; 10:41). He means those who came in their own name claiming what belongs only to him (John 5:43): self-appointed deliverers and leaders who used the people for their own ends. Josephus describes a number of such men in the first century who gathered followers with promises of deliverance and led them to destruction (verify the particular accounts). The present tense, 'are', includes the men standing in front of him. And 'the sheep did not hear them': God's true people were not finally captured by them.

'By me if any man enter in, he shall be saved'. The door is open to anyone ('any man'), and entering is the only condition. Then Jesus describes the saved life in shepherd's language: 'shall go in and out, and find pasture'. To go in and out was Israel's way of describing the whole of a life lived in safety under a good leader: Moses asked God for a shepherd 'Which may go out before them, and which may go in before them' (Numbers 27:17). The saved are safe inside and fed outside: 'He maketh me to lie down in green pastures' (Psalm 23:2).

Then John 10:10, the verse that frames the whole chapter. The thief's purpose is always the same: 'to steal, and to kill, and to destroy'. The second verb is one often used of killing an animal for a sacrifice or a feast, and the STEPBible lexicon lists this verse under its plain sense, to slay, to kill; the thief treats the sheep as meat. Jesus' purpose is the opposite: 'that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly'. The life is the life John speaks of everywhere, the life of God given in the Son (John 1:4; 5:24; 17:3). 'More abundantly' means more than enough, over and above (the STEPBible lexicon lists this verse under that sense). It is not more money or more comfort; it is life overflowing from the one who gives it, and the very next verse says how he gives it.

**Asks:** Many voices promise you a fuller life if you come in their way. Jesus says there is one door. What 'other way' have you been tempted to try for the life that only he gives? And what does it mean for you today to 'go in and out' through him?

#### John 10:11–13 · The Good Shepherd and the Hireling
**Says:** "I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep." (John 10:11) "But he that is an hireling, and not the shepherd, whose own the sheep are not, seeth the wolf coming, and leaveth the sheep, and fleeth: and the wolf catcheth them, and scattereth the sheep." (John 10:12) "The hireling fleeth, because he is an hireling, and careth not for the sheep." (John 10:13) An *hireling* is a hired worker, paid by the day or the job; *catcheth* means seizes, snatches; *careth not* means has no concern.

**Means:** 'I am the good shepherd'. This is the fourth 'I am' with an image, and the one that gives the chapter its name. Behind it stands the whole Old Testament. God was Israel's shepherd from the beginning, the 'shepherd, the stone of Israel' (Genesis 49:24), and David sang, 'The LORD is my shepherd' (Psalm 23:1). God gave Israel human shepherds, above all David: God 'took him from the sheepfolds' to feed his people (Psalm 78:70–71). When those shepherds failed, God said he would come and shepherd his flock himself (Ezekiel 34:11–16), and would 'set up one shepherd over them', his servant David (Ezekiel 34:23). When Jesus says 'I am the good shepherd', he stands in both places at once: the LORD who shepherds his people, and the promised son of David.

The word 'good' is not the most common word for moral goodness. It means good in the sense of excellent, fine, what a thing ought to be (see the Deep Dive): Jesus is the shepherd who is everything a shepherd should be. And he immediately defines what that is: 'the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep'. The verb here is the one translated 'lay down' in John 10:15, 17, and 18, and it is John's own way of speaking of a willing death (John 13:37; 15:13; 1 John 3:16). A shepherd in Israel might risk his life for his sheep, as David did against a lion and a bear (1 Samuel 17:34–35). But a shepherd who actually died usually left his flock scattered: 'smite the shepherd, and the sheep shall be scattered' (Zechariah 13:7). This shepherd dies *for* the sheep, on their behalf, and, as the prophet had said, in their place: 'the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all' (Isaiah 53:6). His death is their life.

Then the contrast. The hireling works for wages, and the sheep are not his own. When he 'seeth the wolf coming', he does what a man does who has nothing at stake: he 'leaveth the sheep, and fleeth'. The wolf 'catcheth them, and scattereth the sheep'. John 10:13 gives the reason, and it is the key to the whole contrast: 'because he is an hireling, and careth not for the sheep'. The problem is not that he is paid; Scripture says 'The labourer is worthy of his reward' (1 Timothy 5:18). The problem is that he is in it for himself. Jesus is describing the leaders who had just shown how little they cared for a sheep (John 9:34), and he is describing every religious leader, in any age, who serves the flock only as long as it serves him. Paul warned the Ephesian elders that 'grievous wolves' would come 'not sparing the flock' (Acts 20:29), and Jesus warned of 'false prophets, which come to you in sheep's clothing' (Matthew 7:15).

**Asks:** The difference between the shepherd and the hireling shows up only when the wolf comes. Whom has Christ put in your care, and what would you do if caring for them became costly? Where have you been a hireling, and where has the Good Shepherd stayed with you when others ran?

#### John 10:14–16 · Known by Name, and Other Sheep
**Says:** "I am the good shepherd, and know my sheep, and am known of mine." (John 10:14) "As the Father knoweth me, even so know I the Father: and I lay down my life for the sheep." (John 10:15) "And other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring, and they shall hear my voice; and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd." (John 10:16) *Known of mine* means known by my own; *fold* here means a flock gathered in one place.

**Means:** Jesus repeats the claim, 'I am the good shepherd', and adds what makes him good besides his death: he knows his sheep, and they know him. The hireling does not know the sheep; they are a job. The shepherd knows each one. In Scripture, for God to 'know' people is more than to have information about them; it is to know them as his own, in covenant love. So Paul writes, 'The Lord knoweth them that are his' (2 Timothy 2:19), and Jesus will say to some on the last day, 'I never knew you' (Matthew 7:23). And the sheep know him in return: not only facts about him, but him.

Then Jesus sets that knowledge on the highest possible ground: 'As the Father knoweth me, even so know I the Father'. The bond between the shepherd and his sheep is patterned on the bond between the Father and the Son. It is a staggering thing to say: the knowing between the Good Shepherd and a simple believer is like the knowing within God himself. And again: 'I lay down my life for the sheep'.

'And other sheep I have, which are not of this fold'. The fold in this picture is Israel, from which the shepherd is leading his own. But he has sheep elsewhere, among the nations. He speaks of them as already his ('I have') though they have not yet heard him; they will. 'Them also I must bring', with the same 'must' of divine necessity that runs through John (John 3:14; 4:4; 9:4). 'They shall hear my voice': the Gentiles will hear it through the word of his disciples (John 17:20). John has already said that Jesus would die 'not for that nation only, but that also he should gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad' (John 11:52), and in John 12 the arrival of some Greeks who 'would see Jesus' (John 12:21) will tell him that his hour has come. 'And there shall be one fold, and one shepherd'. The KJV uses 'fold' twice in this verse, but the Greek uses two different words: the first means the enclosure, the second means the flock (see the Deep Dive). The unity Jesus promises is not one pen but one flock, Jews and Gentiles made one because they follow one shepherd (Ezekiel 37:24; Ephesians 2:14).

**Asks:** The Good Shepherd knows you as the Father knows the Son. Do you live as someone who is known like that, or as someone who has to earn attention? And who are the 'other sheep' near you, people you would not naturally think of as belonging, whom the Shepherd 'must bring'?

#### John 10:17–18 · Power to Lay It Down and Take It Again
**Says:** "Therefore doth my Father love me, because I lay down my life, that I might take it again." (John 10:17) "No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again. This commandment have I received of my Father." (John 10:18) *Power* here means authority, the right to act.

**Means:** 'Therefore doth my Father love me, because I lay down my life'. This does not mean the Father's love for the Son began at the cross; the Son was loved 'before the foundation of the world' (John 17:24). It means that the Father delights in the Son's willing obedience unto death, and that this love rests on him as he does it. And the death has a purpose beyond itself: 'that I might take it again'. The resurrection is not an afterthought to the cross; it is its goal.

Then comes one of the most remarkable claims in the Gospel. 'No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself'. Jesus will be arrested, tried, and crucified, and from the outside it will look as though his enemies have taken his life. John will not let us read it that way. In the garden, the soldiers will fall back at his word (John 18:6); before Pilate he will say, 'Thou couldest have no power at all against me, except it were given thee from above' (John 19:11); and on the cross he will bow his head and give up his spirit (John 19:30). Men were guilty of his death, but they did not take his life from him; he gave it.

'I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again'. The word 'power' means authority, the right and freedom to act (the STEPBible lexicon lists this verse under that sense). No mere man has the right to take up his life again after death. Jesus claims it, as he claimed at the first Passover, 'Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up' (John 2:19). Elsewhere Scripture says that God raised him (Acts 2:24), and that he was 'declared to be the Son of God with power' by the resurrection (Romans 1:4). These do not contradict one another: the Father, the Son, and the Spirit work together in every work of God.

'This commandment have I received of my Father'. The Son's authority is exercised in obedience. He lays down his life because the Father commanded it, and he takes it again because the Father commanded that too. He is the obedient Son who 'humbled himself, and became obedient unto death' (Philippians 2:8).

**Asks:** The cross was not an accident or a defeat; it was love choosing to die. How does it change the way you look at the cross to know that 'No man taketh it from me'? And how does his obedience to the Father's command shape the way you obey?

#### John 10:19–21 · A Division Again
**Says:** "There was a division therefore again among the Jews for these sayings." (John 10:19) "And many of them said, He hath a devil, and is mad; why hear ye him?" (John 10:20) "Others said, These are not the words of him that hath a devil. Can a devil open the eyes of the blind?" (John 10:21) *A devil* here means a demon; *mad* means insane, raving.

**Means:** 'There was a division therefore again'. This is the third time John reports a split over Jesus (John 7:43; 9:16), and each time it is Jesus himself who causes it. Many said, 'He hath a devil, and is mad'. The same charge had been made at the feast of tabernacles (John 7:20; 8:48, 52). It was an easy way to dismiss him without answering him: *why hear ye him?* Others were not so sure: 'These are not the words of him that hath a devil'. And they pointed to the evidence that everyone in Jerusalem was talking about: 'Can a devil open the eyes of the blind?' This ties the whole of John 10:1–21 back to John 9. The good shepherd discourse was spoken in the shadow of the healing, to the men who had cast out the healed man.

The choice these hearers faced is the choice every reader of John faces. A man who says 'I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again' is either out of his mind, or he is who he says he is. Christian writers have long pressed this point; C. S. Lewis made it famous in the twentieth century (*Mere Christianity*, book 2, verify). John simply records both responses and lets the reader decide, with the healed man standing there as evidence.

**Asks:** When Jesus' claims are too much for people, it is easier to call him mad, or a myth, or a mere teacher, than to face what he says. Which of Jesus' claims do you find it hardest to take at face value, and what 'works' of his does God use to answer your doubts?

#### John 10:22–24 · Winter at the Feast of the Dedication
**Says:** "And it was at Jerusalem the feast of the dedication, and it was winter." (John 10:22) "And Jesus walked in the temple in Solomon's porch." (John 10:23) "Then came the Jews round about him, and said unto him, How long dost thou make us to doubt? If thou be the Christ, tell us plainly." (John 10:24) *Dedication* means consecration, setting apart for God; a *porch* here is a long covered colonnade; *plainly* means openly, without riddles.

**Means:** John moves the scene forward about two months, to the last feast he names before the final Passover (John 11:55): after Passover (John 2:13; 6:4), an unnamed feast (John 5:1), and tabernacles (John 7:2), now 'the feast of the dedication'. It celebrated the cleansing and rededication of the temple under Judas Maccabeus after the Syrian king Antiochus IV had defiled it (see Setting the Scene). This is the only place the feast is named in the Bible. John adds 'it was winter', perhaps simply to explain why Jesus was walking in the shelter of 'Solomon's porch', a covered colonnade along the east side of the temple courts. The same porch would later be where the first believers gathered (Acts 3:11; 5:12).

The setting is full of meaning, though John leaves us to see it. At a feast remembering the temple's rededication, the one who called his own body 'this temple' (John 2:19–21) walks in the temple courts. At a feast remembering the liberator who drove out the enemy, the leaders want to know whether this man is the Christ. And before the chapter is over, Jesus will say that 'the Father hath sanctified' him, using the verb of setting apart and consecrating (John 10:36).

'Then came the Jews round about him'. The verb means they surrounded him, closed in on him. As in John 9, 'the Jews' here means the Jerusalem leaders and those with them; Jesus and his disciples were Jews too. They say, 'How long dost thou make us to doubt?' Word for word, the Greek says *how long do you lift up our soul*, an expression for keeping someone in suspense (see the Deep Dive). 'If thou be the Christ, tell us plainly'. The word 'plainly' means openly, boldly, in public; John uses it of Jesus' public speech (John 7:26; 18:20). They want a straight declaration. But what they mean by 'the Christ' is a political deliverer like the Maccabees, and Jesus has already refused to be made that kind of king (John 6:15).

**Asks:** The leaders blamed Jesus for their doubt: *you keep us in suspense.* It is easy to blame God's lack of clarity for our own unwillingness to believe what he has already shown. Where are you asking for more certainty before you obey what you already know?

#### John 10:25–27 · My Sheep Hear My Voice
**Says:** "Jesus answered them, I told you, and ye believed not: the works that I do in my Father's name, they bear witness of me." (John 10:25) "But ye believe not, because ye are not of my sheep, as I said unto you." (John 10:26) "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me:" (John 10:27)

**Means:** 'I told you, and ye believed not'. Jesus had not used the title 'Christ' of himself before these leaders in the way they wanted, but he had told them who he was in every other way: the Son who works as the Father works and gives life to whom he will (John 5:17–21), the bread from heaven (John 6:35), the light of the world (John 8:12), the one who was before Abraham (John 8:58). And he had done works that spoke even louder: 'the works that I do in my Father's name, they bear witness of me'. The works are witnesses, as he had said in John 5:36. The problem was never a lack of evidence.

Then Jesus names the real reason: 'ye believe not, because ye are not of my sheep'. Notice the order. He does not say *you are not my sheep because you do not believe*; he says *you do not believe because you are not my sheep*. Belonging comes first, and belief shows it. This fits what Jesus said in John 6: 'All that the Father giveth me shall come to me' (John 6:37), and 'No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him' (John 6:44). Christians have disagreed about how to read these words (see the Council's third question), but the plain sense is that coming to Christ is God's gift before it is our act.

'My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me'. Here are the marks of Christ's sheep, set out simply. They *hear* his voice: they listen, and receive his word. He *knows* them: they are his, known as his own. And they *follow* him: they go where he leads. The verbs are in the present tense; they describe a continuing way of life, not a single moment. That is how anyone can tell who the sheep are: not by a claim, but by listening and following.

**Asks:** The three marks of the sheep are simple enough for anyone to test: do you hear his voice, are you known by him, do you follow him? Which of the three is weakest in your life right now, and what would it look like to strengthen it this week?

#### John 10:28–30 · Never Perish, and I and My Father Are One
**Says:** "And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand." (John 10:28) "My Father, which gave them me, is greater than all; and no man is able to pluck them out of my Father's hand." (John 10:29) "I and my Father are one." (John 10:30) *Pluck* means snatch, seize by force.

**Means:** 'And I give unto them eternal life'. Eternal life is a gift, not wages, and the Shepherd gives it now, in the present tense (John 5:24). Then come two promises. The first: 'they shall never perish'. The Greek is as strong as the language can make it: a double negative that means *certainly not*, joined to a phrase meaning *for ever* (see the Deep Dive). The second: 'neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand'. The verb 'pluck' is the same verb used two verses earlier of the wolf who 'catcheth' the sheep (John 10:12). The wolf can snatch sheep from a hireling who runs away. He cannot snatch them from this shepherd.

Then the Shepherd adds a second hand. 'My Father, which gave them me, is greater than all'. The sheep were the Father's gift to the Son before they were ever called (John 6:37; 17:6). The Father is greater than any power that could threaten them, 'and no man is able to pluck them out of my Father's hand'. Old Testament readers would hear an echo: the LORD had said, 'neither is there any that can deliver out of my hand' (Deuteronomy 32:39), and 'there is none that can deliver out of my hand' (Isaiah 43:13). What the LORD says of his hand, Jesus says of his own hand and his Father's.

And that leads straight to the sentence that makes the leaders reach for stones: 'I and my Father are one'. The word 'one' is neuter in Greek: not *one person* but *one thing* (see the Deep Dive). Jesus does not say that he and the Father are the same person; he says 'I and my Father', two, and uses a plural verb, 'are'. But he says they are one. At the very least, in this context, they are one in their hand, their work, and their power to keep the sheep. The leaders heard more than that, and the next verse shows it (John 10:33). How the church has understood this oneness is the subject of the Council's first question.

**Asks:** Who is holding whom? If your safety depended on your grip on Christ, you would have reason to fear. It depends on his grip on you, and the Father's. How does that change the way you face your weakness, your failures, and your fear of falling away?

#### John 10:31–33 · Stones for Blasphemy
**Says:** "Then the Jews took up stones again to stone him." (John 10:31) "Jesus answered them, Many good works have I shewed you from my Father; for which of those works do ye stone me?" (John 10:32) "The Jews answered him, saying, For a good work we stone thee not; but for blasphemy; and because that thou, being a man, makest thyself God." (John 10:33) *Shewed* means showed; *blasphemy* is speech that dishonours God, here the claim to be God.

**Means:** 'Then the Jews took up stones again'. The 'again' points back to John 8:59, where they took up stones after 'Before Abraham was, I am' (John 8:58). The law commanded that a blasphemer be stoned (Leviticus 24:16), and they believed that was what Jesus was.

Jesus answers with a question that is both calm and sharp: 'Many good works have I shewed you from my Father; for which of those works do ye stone me?' The word 'good' is the same word as in 'the good shepherd' (John 10:11): works that are fine, excellent, beautiful in their goodness. He has healed a man who had been ill for thirty-eight years (John 5:5–9) and opened the eyes of a man born blind (John 9:1–7). Which of those deserves stoning?

They answer honestly: 'For a good work we stone thee not; but for blasphemy; and because that thou, being a man, makest thyself God'. That is exactly the charge made in John 5:18, where they sought to kill him because he said 'that God was his Father, making himself equal with God', and it will be the charge before Pilate: 'he made himself the Son of God' (John 19:7). Whatever else we say about John 10:30, the men who heard it understood it as a claim to be God, and Jesus did not tell them they had misunderstood. The irony is complete. They say that he, being a man, makes himself God. The truth, as John told us in his first chapter, is the reverse: the Word who 'was God' (John 1:1) became flesh (John 1:14). He did not make himself God; being God, he made himself man.

**Asks:** It is possible to see Christ's good works and still reach for a stone, because what he claims threatens us. What claim of Jesus would cost you most to accept fully, and how do his 'good works' toward you make it easier to trust him?

#### John 10:34–36 · Ye Are Gods
**Says:** "Jesus answered them, Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?" (John 10:34) "If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came, and the scripture cannot be broken;" (John 10:35) "Say ye of him, whom the Father hath sanctified, and sent into the world, Thou blasphemest; because I said, I am the Son of God?" (John 10:36) *Sanctified* here means set apart for a holy purpose, consecrated.

**Means:** Jesus answers from Scripture. 'Is it not written in your law'. He quotes a psalm, Psalm 82:6, and calls it 'law', as Jews of the time could call the whole of Scripture; he does the same in John 15:25. He says 'your law' not to distance himself from it but to press it on them: this is the Scripture you claim to honour. The psalm says, 'I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High' (Psalm 82:6). In Psalm 82, God stands 'in the congregation of the mighty' and judges 'among the gods' (Psalm 82:1), rebuking them for judging unjustly (Psalm 82:2) and warning them, 'ye shall die like men' (Psalm 82:7). Most readers take these 'gods' to be Israel's judges, who judged in God's name; others take them to be Israel as the people who received God's word at Sinai (see the View A / View B panel). On either reading they are human beings, and the psalm is not flattering them.

Then comes the argument, in a form Jewish teachers used often: from the lesser to the greater. 'If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came'. If Scripture could use that title of mere men, because God's word came to them and gave them his work to do, then how can it be blasphemy for the one 'whom the Father hath sanctified, and sent into the world' to say 'I am the Son of God'? They received the word of God; he was set apart and sent from the Father into the world. The contrast is between those to whom the word came and the one who came himself.

In the middle of the argument stands a short clause about Scripture that has become one of the Bible's own statements of its authority: 'and the scripture cannot be broken'. The verb means to loosen, annul, set aside; John uses the same verb for 'breaking' the sabbath (John 5:18) and the law of Moses (John 7:23). Jesus rests his whole argument on one word in one psalm, 'gods', and says that even that word cannot be set aside.

'Whom the Father hath sanctified'. The verb means to set apart for God, to consecrate (the STEPBible lexicon lists this verse under that sense). John records these words at the feast of the dedication, the feast of the temple set apart again for God. Jesus says the Father set him apart and sent him. He is the holy one, the true temple, consecrated by the Father himself. And far from retreating, he restates his claim: 'I am the Son of God'.

**Asks:** Jesus treated a single word of an Old Testament psalm as unbreakable. How do you treat the parts of Scripture you find hard or unfamiliar? And what would change if you read the Bible with his confidence that 'the scripture cannot be broken'?

#### John 10:37–39 · Believe the Works
**Says:** "If I do not the works of my Father, believe me not." (John 10:37) "But if I do, though ye believe not me, believe the works: that ye may know, and believe, that the Father is in me, and I in him." (John 10:38) "Therefore they sought again to take him: but he escaped out of their hand," (John 10:39)

**Means:** Jesus makes an astonishing offer to men who are holding stones: judge me by my works. 'If I do not the works of my Father, believe me not'. He does not ask for blind faith. 'But if I do, though ye believe not me, believe the works'. Even if they cannot yet accept his words, the works are there, and they point beyond themselves. The goal is not only belief in the works but knowledge of who he is: 'that ye may know, and believe, that the Father is in me, and I in him'. This mutual indwelling of the Father and the Son is John's fullest description of their oneness (John 14:10–11; 17:21). It explains John 10:30: the Father and the Son are one because each is in the other.

The Textus Receptus behind the KJV reads 'know, and believe'; many early manuscripts read *know and keep on knowing* or *know and understand* (see the textual notes in the Deep Dive). The meaning is the same either way: the works are meant to bring people to recognise the Father in the Son.

'Therefore they sought again to take him'. They understood that he had not withdrawn his claim. 'But he escaped out of their hand'. John does not tell us how, only that he did. The irony answers John 10:28: no one can snatch the sheep out of his hand, and no one can hold him in theirs until his hour comes (John 7:30; 8:20).

**Asks:** Jesus invites honest testing: look at what I do. What 'works' of his, in Scripture, in history, in your own life or the lives of others, have pointed you to the Father in him? And to whom could you say this week, *don't take my word for it; look at what he has done*?

#### John 10:40–42 · Beyond Jordan, Where John First Baptized
**Says:** "And went away again beyond Jordan into the place where John at first baptized; and there he abode." (John 10:40) "And many resorted unto him, and said, John did no miracle: but all things that John spake of this man were true." (John 10:41) "And many believed on him there." (John 10:42) *Abode* means stayed; *resorted* means came, went in numbers; *miracle* here means a sign.

**Means:** Jesus withdraws from Jerusalem to 'the place where John at first baptized', beyond the Jordan, the place John named in his first chapter as Bethabara (John 1:28). The public ministry that began with John the Baptist's witness at the Jordan comes round, at this turning point, to the same place. After this, Jesus will come near Jerusalem again only to raise Lazarus and then to die (John 11:7–8, 54).

The people who come to him there say two things about John the Baptist. 'John did no miracle': John had done no sign. That is a striking remark in a Gospel built around Jesus' signs (John 2:11; 20:30–31). John's greatness did not lie in wonders but in his witness. 'But all things that John spake of this man were true'. John had said that Jesus was 'the Lamb of God' (John 1:29), 'the Son of God' (John 1:34), and the one who 'must increase' while he himself decreased (John 3:30). Now, in the very place he said it, people can see that his witness was true. 'And many believed on him there'. In Jerusalem, surrounded by leaders who would not believe, Jesus was stoned at; across the Jordan, among ordinary people who remembered the Baptist, many believed. John's Gospel is itself a witness like that of the Baptist, written 'that ye might believe' (John 20:31).

**Asks:** John the Baptist did no signs, but his words about Jesus proved true and led many to faith after he was gone. You may never do anything spectacular. What true words about Jesus could you speak now, which might bear fruit long after?

### 🧨 Cultural Landmines and Misconceptions
**The soft, sentimental shepherd.** Many people picture the Good Shepherd from Victorian paintings: a gentle figure with a lamb on his shoulders in a green meadow. That picture is not wrong (Isaiah 40:11; Luke 15:5), but it is not the whole of John 10. In the ancient world shepherding was hard, dirty, and dangerous work, done at night and in the wild, and shepherds fought lions and bears (1 Samuel 17:34–35). Kings were called shepherds. The Good Shepherd of John 10 is tender, but he is also the King of Israel and the LORD of Psalm 23, and the chapter ends with men trying to kill him. His goodness is shown not by softness but by a death.

**'Shepherd' as a job title.** In many churches today the word 'pastor', which comes from the Latin for shepherd, is simply a job title, and a church's leaders can be thought of as staff hired to provide services. John 10 turns that around. There is one shepherd, and every other leader is an under-shepherd who must give account to him (1 Peter 5:4; Hebrews 13:17). The question for every leader is not whether he is paid, but whether he cares for the sheep or for himself (John 10:13).

**Sheep as stupid.** A common sermon line says that sheep are among the stupidest of animals, so being called sheep is an insult. Scripture's point is not intelligence but dependence: sheep need a shepherd to lead, feed, and protect them (Numbers 27:17; Mark 6:34). And John 10 credits the sheep with one kind of wisdom: they know their shepherd's voice and will not follow a stranger (John 10:4–5).

**Hanukkah and Christmas.** Because the feast of the dedication (Hanukkah) falls near Christmas, some people assume the two are related, or that Jesus was keeping a Jewish Christmas. They are not related. Hanukkah remembers the rededication of the temple in the second century BC. John mentions it because the setting fits what Jesus says: at the feast of the temple's consecration, he is the one 'whom the Father hath sanctified' (John 10:36). Christians need not observe Hanukkah, but they can honour it as part of the story of God's preserving of his people and his temple before Christ came.

**'The Jews' in John 10.** As in John 9, 'the Jews' who divide, surround Jesus, and take up stones (John 10:19, 24, 31) are the Jerusalem crowd and its leaders at that moment. Jesus, his disciples, and the 'many' who believed beyond Jordan (John 10:42) were Jews too. The chapter is not a verdict on the Jewish people, and it must never be taught as one. The 'other sheep' of John 10:16 are brought into one flock with Israel's believing remnant, not in place of Israel.

**The 'other sheep' of John 10:16 as a special group.** Some movements have read the 'other sheep' as a particular people of their own teaching: the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints teaches that they are people in the ancient Americas whom the risen Jesus visited (from the Book of Mormon's book of Third Nephi, verify), and Jehovah's Witnesses teach that they are a class of believers with an earthly rather than a heavenly hope (verify). Read in John's own context, the 'other sheep' are the Gentiles who will hear Christ's voice through the gospel and be made one flock with believing Israel (John 11:52; 12:20–21; 17:20).

## 🔴 Tier 3 — Expert
### 🏗️ Theological Framework
John 10 is a discourse joined to a confrontation, and it bears on more of systematic theology than almost any chapter of John. Read through the Reformed conviction that Scripture interprets Scripture, it gathers around seven heads.

*Christ the Shepherd-King and the LORD who shepherds.* The Old Testament gives the title shepherd both to God (Genesis 49:24; Psalm 23:1; 80:1) and to Israel's kings (2 Samuel 5:2; Psalm 78:70–72). Ezekiel 34 holds the two together: the LORD will search for his sheep himself (Ezekiel 34:11), and he will set over them one shepherd, his servant David (Ezekiel 34:23). When Jesus says 'I am the good shepherd' (John 10:11, 14) and 'there shall be one fold, and one shepherd' (John 10:16), he claims both halves of that promise. Reformed Christology reads this as one of the Gospel's clearest witnesses to the one person who is both God and the son of David, the Mediator who is truly God and truly man in one person (Westminster Confession, chapter 8, section 2, paraphrased, verify).

*The voluntary, substitutionary death and the power of the resurrection.* The shepherd lays down his life *for* the sheep, *huper* (G5228, John 10:11), the preposition of benefit and, in contexts of death, of substitution (compare John 11:50; 2 Corinthians 5:14–15, 21). His death is his own act, in obedience to the Father's command (John 10:18), not a martyr's fate imposed on him. The Westminster Confession teaches that the Lord Jesus willingly undertook the office of Mediator, and by his perfect obedience and sacrifice of himself fully satisfied the justice of the Father (chapter 8, sections 4–5, paraphrased, verify). John 10:17–18 adds that the resurrection is part of the same obedient work: the Son has authority to take his life again, and the Father's love rests on the whole.

*The extent of the atonement.* 'I lay down my life for the sheep' (John 10:15) is a key text in the debate about for whom Christ died. The Reformed doctrine of definite or particular redemption holds that Christ died with the intention of actually saving those whom the Father had given him, and that his death secures their salvation (Canons of Dort, second head, article 8, paraphrased, verify). John 10 supports it by joining the death to a people known and named (John 10:3, 14–15), given by the Father (John 10:29), and kept to the end (John 10:28), and by setting the sheep against those who are 'not of my sheep' (John 10:26). Others read the text differently. Arminian and Wesleyan theology holds that Christ died for every person, making salvation possible for all, and notes that saying he died 'for the sheep' does not in itself say he died for no one else (compare 1 John 2:2). Hypothetical universalists, such as Moïse Amyraut and John Davenant (verify), taught that Christ died sufficiently for all and with a special saving intention for the elect. Reformed theology agrees that Christ's death is sufficient for all and freely offered to all, as the door is open to 'any man' (John 10:9), but argues that the Shepherd's intention and accomplishment are for his sheep, and that John 10 presents the death, the gift, the calling, and the keeping as one unbroken work.

*Election and effectual calling.* The sheep belong to the Shepherd before they hear him: 'other sheep I have' (John 10:16); 'My Father, which gave them me' (John 10:29). Unbelief is explained by not belonging: 'ye believe not, because ye are not of my sheep' (John 10:26). Reformed theology reads these words with John 6:37, 44, and 65 as teaching unconditional election and effectual calling: the Father gives a people to the Son, and the Son calls them by name so that they hear and follow (John 10:3, 27; Westminster Confession, chapter 10, section 1, paraphrased, verify). The call is personal ('by name') and effective ('they shall hear my voice'). Yet the chapter never makes election an excuse for unbelief: the leaders are held responsible for refusing the works they have seen (John 10:25, 37–38).

*The perseverance of the saints.* 'They shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand' (John 10:28). The Reformed doctrine, sometimes summarized as *preservation and perseverance*, holds that those whom God has accepted in Christ and effectually called can neither totally nor finally fall away from grace, but will certainly persevere to the end and be saved, and that this rests not on their own free will but on the unchanging decree of election, the merit and intercession of Christ, and the abiding of the Spirit (Westminster Confession, chapter 17, sections 1–2, paraphrased, verify; Canons of Dort, fifth head). John 10:28–29 is one of its chief texts. The Debate Corner below states the alternatives.

*The unity of the Father and the Son.* 'I and my Father are one' (John 10:30), with 'the Father is in me, and I in him' (John 10:38), is one of the texts on which the church built its confession of the Trinity. The Son is distinct from the Father (he is sent, commanded, loved, and given the sheep), and yet one with him, so that his hand is the Father's hand and his works are the Father's works. The Nicene faith, that the Son is of one substance with the Father (summarized, not quoted), is the church's account of how both statements can be true.

*The authority of Scripture.* 'The scripture cannot be broken' (John 10:35) is a short clause with a large reach. Jesus rests an argument on one word of a psalm and says that it cannot be annulled. Reformed theology draws from texts like this, with Matthew 5:18 and 2 Timothy 3:16, that the whole of Scripture is the word of God, whose authority depends on God its author and not on any man or church (Westminster Confession, chapter 1, sections 4–5, paraphrased, verify).

### 📚 Scholarly Insights
- **Augustine**, *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (Tractates 45–48, verify): explains how Christ can be both the door and the shepherd, and says that pastors enter by him as the door; famously remarks on John 10:30 that 'one' answers Arius, who divided the Son's nature from the Father's, and 'are' answers Sabellius, who made them one person (verify the wording and location); and draws on John 10:28–29 for his teaching on the gift of perseverance, which he developed in *On the Gift of Perseverance* (verify).
- **John Chrysostom**, *Homilies on the Gospel of John* (Homilies 59–61, verify): contrasts the shepherd who dies with the thieves and hirelings, applies the hireling passage sharply to the clergy of his own day, and argues from John 10:30 that the Son's power is the Father's power (verify).
- **Athanasius**, *Discourses Against the Arians* (book 3, verify): argues from John 10:30 and 14:10 that the Son's oneness with the Father is not merely agreement of will, since saints and angels agree with God's will without ever saying, as the Son does, that they and the Father are one (verify).
- **Hilary of Poitiers**, *On the Trinity* (book 7, verify): reads John 10:28–30 and 10:38 as showing that the Son shares the Father's nature, since the hand that holds the sheep is one power (verify).
- **John Calvin**, *Commentary on the Gospel According to John*: on John 10:28–29 teaches that the salvation of the elect is as certain as God's power is invincible; on John 10:30 remarks that the ancient writers used the verse to prove that Christ is of one essence with the Father, but that Christ is speaking here of his agreement with the Father in power, while not denying what the church confesses elsewhere; and on John 10:34 takes the 'gods' to be magistrates, called so because God appointed them (verify each).
- **J. C. Ryle**, *Expository Thoughts on the Gospels* (John): draws from John 10 the marks of Christ's true sheep (hearing and following), the comfort of their final safety, and a solemn warning to ministers who are hirelings (verify).
- **B. F. Westcott**, *The Gospel According to St. John* (1880): suggests that 'good' in 'the good shepherd' carries the sense of an attractive, noble goodness, and notes the difference between the fold and the flock in John 10:16 (verify).
- **Leon Morris**, *The Gospel According to John* (New International Commentary, 1971): stresses the voluntary character of Christ's death in John 10:17–18 and argues that the neuter 'one' in John 10:30 points to unity of essence rather than identity of person (verify).
- **Raymond E. Brown**, *The Gospel According to John* (Anchor Bible, 1966): reads John 10:1–5 as a combination of parables, traces the Ezekiel 34 background, and discusses the textual problem at John 10:29 (verify).
- **D. A. - **Kenneth E. Bailey**, *The Good Shepherd: A Thousand-Year Journey from Psalm 23 to the New Testament* (2014, verify): traces the good-shepherd tradition through Psalm 23, Jeremiah 23, Ezekiel 34, Zechariah 10, and the Gospels, and reads John 10 as its fulfilment (verify).

### ⚔️ Debate Corner
**The question:** What does Jesus promise about the security of his sheep in "And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand." (John 10:28)?

**Position 1 · The sheep are safe from every outside power while they keep following, but they may leave.** This is the Arminian and Wesleyan reading. The Remonstrants of 1610 left the question open for further study (article 5, verify), and later Arminian theology, and John Wesley, taught that a true believer can fall away and be lost (verify). Holders argue that John 10:28 promises protection from *any man*, that is, from outside enemies, not from the sheep's own choice; that the verbs of John 10:27 are present tenses describing sheep who *keep on* hearing and following; and that Scripture's warnings would be empty if falling away were impossible (John 15:6; Hebrews 6:4–6; Hebrews 10:26; Colossians 1:23). They want to protect the seriousness of the warnings and the reality of human response.

**Position 2 · The sheep are kept by the Son and the Father, and they persevere because they are kept.** This is the Reformed reading, taught by Augustine in its essentials, by Calvin, by the Canons of Dort (fifth head), and by the Westminster Confession (chapter 17) (verify each). Holders argue that the promise is about the outcome ('they shall never perish'), not only about outside enemies; that the Father's gift and the Father's greatness stand behind it (John 10:29); and that the sheep's hearing and following are not conditions that might fail but the marks of those whom Christ keeps. Those who finally fall away show that they were never his sheep (1 John 2:19). They want to protect the sufficiency of Christ's work and the certainty of God's promise.

**Position 3 · Once a person has believed, he is secure for ever, whether or not he continues to believe or follow.** This view, sometimes called *free grace* or the *once saved, always saved* view in a strong form, is associated with Zane Hodges and, in another form, with R. T. Kendall (verify each). Holders argue that eternal life is a free gift received by a single act of faith, that following Christ is a matter of discipleship and reward rather than of salvation, and that making perseverance a mark of salvation smuggles works back into the gospel. They want to protect assurance and the freeness of grace.

**Most consistent with Reformed hermeneutics.** Scripture interpreting Scripture favours Position 2, and it does so by taking seriously what is true in both of the others. (1) *The promise concerns the outcome.* Jesus does not only say that no one will snatch the sheep; he says first, in the strongest negation Greek has, that they 'shall never perish' (see the Deep Dive). The reason he gives is not the sheep's faithfulness but the Father's greatness: the Father who gave them 'is greater than all' (John 10:29), which leaves no power, inside or outside the sheep, able to undo the Father's gift. (2) *The rest of John says the same.* The Son's commission is that 'of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing' (John 6:39), and at the end he reports that he has kept them, the one exception being 'the son of perdition' (John 17:12), who, as John has told us, was never truly one of them (John 6:64, 70–71). (3) *Scripture interprets the falling away.* 'They went out from us, but they were not of us' (1 John 2:19). The warnings of Hebrews are real, but the same writer says, "But, beloved, we are persuaded better things of you, and things that accompany salvation, though we thus speak." (Hebrews 6:9). The warnings are among the means by which God keeps his sheep following; they are not evidence that he might fail to keep them. (4) *Against Position 3*, the chapter defines the sheep by what they do: they hear his voice, and 'they follow me' (John 10:27), and those who do not believe are 'not of my sheep' (John 10:26). Security that has nothing to do with hearing and following is not the security of John 10. (5) *Against Position 1*, the present tenses of John 10:27 describe the sheep; they are not presented as a condition that some sheep fail to meet. Every sheep described in the verse hears, is known, follows, receives eternal life, and never perishes.

Position 1 is right that the warnings of Scripture are real and must be preached, and that no one may claim the promise of John 10:28 while refusing the voice of John 10:27. Position 3 is right that assurance rests on Christ and not on the quality of our performance. Position 2 holds both: the Shepherd keeps his sheep, and the sheep he keeps follow him.

**Pastoral Warning:** This debate reaches every church, because every church has members who fear they have fallen away and members who presume they never can. Held carelessly, each position does real harm. Position 1, taught without the Shepherd's hand, can leave tender believers living in dread, checking their own grip every day, and it can turn the Christian life into anxious self-preservation. Position 3, taught without the Shepherd's voice, can comfort people who have never followed Christ and have no desire to, and give them a false peace on the road to judgment (Matthew 7:23). And Position 2 itself can be misused in both directions: turned into a slogan that excuses sin, or into an endless inspection of one's own fruit for signs of election. Teach John 10:28–29 as Jesus gave it: to his sheep, as comfort, in the same breath as the call to hear and follow. When a believer fears that he has fallen away, point him to the hand, not to his grip. When a professing believer lives as though Christ's voice does not matter, point him to the voice, and do not let him hide behind the hand.

### 🔤 Original Language Deep Dive
Every Greek and Hebrew definition below comes from one source: the brief lexicon published by STEPBible.org (Tyndale House, Cambridge), whose Greek entries are drawn largely from G. Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament*. It is called 'the STEPBible lexicon' below.

**The door and the fold.** *Thura* (G2374, John 10:7, N-NSF), 'door'. The STEPBible lexicon lists its ordinary uses (a house door, a tomb door) and then its metaphorical use of Christ, 'the door of the sheep', at John 10:7 and 9. *Aulēn* (G833, John 10:1, N-ASF), 'fold'. The lexicon's first sense is an open courtyard, and hence an enclosure in the open, a sheepfold, listing John 10:1 and 16; its second sense is the courtyard of a house or palace, under which it lists John 18:15, the high priest's courtyard where Peter will deny his Lord. *Poimnē* (G4167, John 10:16, N-NSF), 'flock'. The lexicon lists its literal uses (Matthew 26:31; Luke 2:8; 1 Corinthians 9:7) and its metaphorical use 'of Christ's followers' at John 10:16. In John 10:16, then, the Greek has two words: the sheep are 'not of this *aulē*', this enclosure, but there shall be 'one *poimnē*', one flock. The KJV renders both 'fold'; many earlier and later English versions, beginning with Tyndale, read 'one flock' (verify), and some have argued that the KJV's 'fold' followed the Latin Vulgate (verify). The difference matters: Jesus promises one flock under one shepherd, not a single enclosure.

**The porter, the call, and the leading out.** *Thurōros* (G2377, John 10:3, N-NSM), 'doorkeeper'. *Kalei* (G2564, John 10:3, V-PAI-3S), 'he calls', with 'by name'. *Exagei* (G1806, John 10:3, V-PAI-3S), 'he leads out'; the Greek Old Testament uses this verb often of God's bringing Israel out of Egypt (verify), which some readers hear behind John 10:3. Then *ekbalē* (G1544, John 10:4, V-2AAS-3S), 'he puts forth', which is the verb used when the Pharisees cast the healed man out, *exebalon* (G1544, John 9:34, V-2AAI-3P). The STEPBible lexicon defines the verb as to drive or cast out, to expel. The leaders expelled him; the shepherd brings out his own, with the same verb and the opposite heart.

**Thief and robber.** *Kleptēs* (G2812, John 10:1, N-NSM) and *lēstēs* (G3027, John 10:1, N-NSM). Under *kleptēs* the STEPBible lexicon adds a note on synonyms, drawn from R. C. Trench's *Synonyms of the New Testament*: the *lēstēs* is a robber or brigand who plunders openly and with violence, and the *kleptēs* is a thief who steals in secret, by fraud and cunning. The lexicon also lists John 10:8 as a metaphorical use of *kleptēs* for false teachers. John uses *lēstēs* one more time, with deep irony: "Then cried they all again, saying, Not this man, but Barabbas. Now Barabbas was a robber." (John 18:40) The crowd will choose the robber and reject the shepherd.

**A figure of speech.** *Paroimian* (G3942, John 10:6, N-ASF). The STEPBible lexicon gives the sense a wayside saying, a byword or proverb (so 2 Peter 2:22), and in John figurative discourse, 'a parable, allegory' (John 10:6; 16:25, 29). The word occurs 5 times in the Greek New Testament, four of them in John. John never uses *parabolē*, the word the other Gospels use for Jesus' parables. At the end of the Farewell the disciples will say that Jesus now speaks plainly and not in this kind of figure (John 16:29).

**Steal, kill, destroy, and perish.** *Klepsē* (G2813, John 10:10, V-AAS-3S), 'steal'. *Thusē* (G2380, John 10:10, V-AAS-3S), 'kill'. The STEPBible lexicon gives three senses: to offer first fruits, to sacrifice by slaying a victim (Acts 14:13), and to slay or kill, under which it lists John 10:10, with the killing of the fatted calf (Luke 15:23) and the Passover (1 Corinthians 5:7). *Apolesē* (G622, John 10:10, V-AAS-3S), 'destroy'. The same verb returns at John 10:28 in the middle voice: *apolōntai* (G622, John 10:28, V-2AMS-3P), 'they shall perish'. The lexicon lists John 10:28 with John 3:15–16 and 17:12 under the metaphorical sense 'of loss of eternal life'. The thief comes to destroy the sheep; the Shepherd says they will never be destroyed.

**Life, abundantly.** *Zōēn* (G2222, John 10:10, N-ASF), 'life', John's constant word for the life of God given in the Son (John 1:4; 20:31). *Perisson* (G4053, John 10:10, ADV), 'abundantly'. The lexicon's first sense is 'more than sufficient, over and above, abundant', and it lists John 10:10 there. The word describes quantity overflowing, not a particular kind of goods; nothing in it points to money.

**The good shepherd.** *Poimēn* (G4166, John 10:11, N-NSM), 'shepherd'. The STEPBible lexicon notes that in the Greek Old Testament it renders the Hebrew *raah* (H7462), whose senses the lexicon lists as to pasture, tend, shepherd, and figuratively of a ruler or teacher and of people as a flock. It lists John 10:2 and 12 under the literal sense and John 10:11, 14, and 16 with Hebrews 13:20 and 1 Peter 2:25 under the metaphorical use 'of Christ'; its only use for church leaders is Ephesians 4:11, 'pastors'. The word occurs 6 times in the Greek of John, all in this chapter, and 18 times in the Greek New Testament. *Kalos* (G2570, John 10:11, A-NSM), 'good'. The lexicon gives two main senses: first, of outward form, 'fair, beautiful', and second, of what is 'well adapted to its ends, good, excellent'; it quotes Hermann Cremer's remark that *kalos* is related to *agathos*, the more common word for goodness, as the appearance is to the essence. That supports reading 'the good shepherd' as the shepherd whose goodness is excellent and visible, the true and model shepherd, without claiming more than the word can carry. The same adjective describes Jesus' 'good works', *kala* (G2570, John 10:32, A-APN): his works show the goodness of the shepherd.

**Laying down the life.** *Tithēsin* (G5087, John 10:11, V-PAI-3S), 'lays down', translated 'giveth' in John 10:11 and 'lay down' in John 10:15, 17, and 18. The STEPBible lexicon's basic sense is to place, lay, set. The phrase 'to lay down the life' is distinctive of John's writings (John 13:37–38; 15:13; 1 John 3:16). The verb occurs 5 times in John 10. *Psuchēn* (G5590, John 10:11, N-ASF), 'life'. The lexicon's first sense is 'breath, breath of life, life', where it lists John 10:11; and it lists John 10:24 under its second sense, the soul as the seat of the will, desires, and affections. The same word appears in both places: the shepherd lays down his *psuchē*, and the leaders say he keeps their *psuchē* in suspense. *Huper* (G5228, John 10:11, PREP), 'for', on behalf of; in contexts of death Paul uses it for Christ's death in our place (2 Corinthians 5:21).

**The hireling and the wolf.** *Misthōtos* (G3411, John 10:12, N-NSM), 'hired', and as a noun 'a hired servant, hireling'. The lexicon lists only Mark 1:20 (the hired servants in Zebedee's boat) and John 10:12–13, and notes that in the Greek Old Testament it renders the Hebrew for a hired worker (Exodus 12:45). *Lukon* (G3074, John 10:12, N-ASM), 'wolf'. *Harpazei* (G726, John 10:12, V-PAI-3S), 'catches'. The lexicon defines the verb as 'to seize, catch up, snatch away, carry off by force', and lists John 10:12 and 10:28–29 together. The verb occurs 4 times in John: of the crowd who would 'take him by force, to make him a king' (John 6:15), of the wolf (John 10:12), and of anyone who would snatch the sheep from the Son's hand or the Father's: *harpasei* (G726, John 10:28, V-FAI-3S) and *harpazein* (G726, John 10:29, V-PAN). *Skorpizei* (G4650, John 10:12, V-PAI-3S), 'scatters'.

**Knowing.** *Ginōskō* (G1097, John 10:14, V-PAI-1S), 'I know', and *ginōskomai* (G1097, John 10:14, V-PPI-1S), 'I am known', which is the Textus Receptus reading (see the textual notes). The STEPBible lexicon notes that in the Greek Old Testament the verb chiefly renders the Hebrew *yada*, and that it is used of God's recognition of his own (1 Corinthians 8:3; Galatians 4:9). In John 10:15 the same verb describes the Father and the Son: *ginōskei* (G1097, John 10:15, V-PAI-3S), 'knoweth'.

**Must bring, and authority.** *Agagein* (G71, John 10:16, V-2AAN), 'to bring'. *Exousian* (G1849, John 10:18, N-ASF), 'power'. The STEPBible lexicon's first sense is 'liberty or power to act', and it lists John 10:18 under that sense, with the construction 'to have authority to' do something. *Airei* (G142, John 10:18, V-PAI-3S), 'takes', the same verb as *aireis* (G142, John 10:24, V-PAI-2S) in the leaders' 'How long dost thou make us to doubt'. *Labein* (G2983, John 10:18, V-2AAN), 'to take' again. *Entolēn* (G1785, John 10:18, N-ASF), 'commandment'.

**Division.** *Schisma* (G4978, John 10:19, N-NSN), 'a split, dissension'. It occurs 3 times in the Greek of John (John 7:43; 9:16; 10:19), each time a division over Jesus.

**The feast, the winter, the porch.** *Egkainia* (G1456, John 10:22, N-NPN), 'dedication'. The STEPBible lexicon explains the word from 'in' and 'new', a renewal; notes that the Greek Old Testament uses it for the dedication of the rebuilt temple and of Jerusalem's wall (Ezra 6:16–17; Nehemiah 12:27); and defines it here as the anniversary of the cleansing of the temple from the defilements of Antiochus Epiphanes. It occurs once in the Greek New Testament. *Cheimōn* (G5494, John 10:22, N-NSM), 'winter'. *Stoa* (G4745, John 10:23, N-DSF), 'porch'; the lexicon defines it as a portico, used of the covered colonnade in the temple (John 10:23; Acts 3:11; 5:12), and lists also the porches at Bethesda (John 5:2). *Ekuklōsan* (G2944, John 10:24, V-AAI-3P), 'surrounded'. *Parrēsia* (G3954, John 10:24, N-DSF), 'plainly', from the noun for boldness or openness of speech.

**Never perish.** John 10:28 joins two Greek negatives, *ou* (G3756, John 10:28) and *mē* (G3361, John 10:28), to an aorist subjunctive, *apolōntai* (G622, John 10:28, V-2AMS-3P), and adds 'unto the age', *aiōna* (G165, John 10:28, N-ASM). Grammarians describe the double negative with the aorist subjunctive as the most emphatic way Greek has of denying a future possibility (verify with a standard grammar, such as Blass, Debrunner, and Funk). The KJV's 'never' gathers both the double negative and 'unto the age' into one word.

**Given, and greater.** *Dedōken* (G1325, John 10:29, V-RAI-3S), 'has given', a perfect tense: the Father's gift stands. *Meizōn* (G3173, John 10:29, A-NSM-C), 'greater', masculine in the Textus Receptus, so that it is the Father who is 'greater than all' (see the textual notes for the alternative reading).

**One.** *Hen* (G1520, John 10:30, A-NSN), 'one', with *esmen* (G1510, John 10:30, V-PAI-1P), 'we are'. The numeral is neuter, not the masculine *heis* that would mean *one person*; and the verb is plural. The STEPBible lexicon lists John 10:30, with John 17:11, under the sense 'metaphorically, of union and concord'. That is an honest limit on what the word alone proves: the neuter numeral says that the Father and the Son are one *thing* while remaining two, and it does not by itself define the unity as a unity of essence. The case for more than agreement comes from the context: the Son's hand is the Father's hand in the keeping of the sheep (John 10:28–29), the hearers understood him to make himself God and he did not correct them (John 10:33, 36), and he explains the unity as mutual indwelling (John 10:38; compare John 1:1; 14:9–10). See the Council's first question.

**Stones and blasphemy.** *Ebastasan* (G941, John 10:31, V-AAI-3P), 'took up', literally carried. *Lithasōsin* (G3034, John 10:31, V-AAS-3P), 'that they might stone'. *Blasphēmias* (G988, John 10:33, N-GSF), 'blasphemy', and *blasphēmeis* (G987, John 10:36, V-PAI-2S), 'thou blasphemest'. *Theon* (G2316, John 10:33, N-ASM-T), 'God', in 'makest thyself God'.

**Ye are gods.** *Gegrammenon* (G1125, John 10:34, V-RPP-NSN), 'written', a perfect passive participle: it stands written. *Nomō* (G3551, John 10:34, N-DSM), 'law', used here of a psalm, as at John 15:25. *Theoi* (G2316, John 10:34, N-NPM), 'gods', translating the Hebrew *elohim* (H430) of Psalm 82:6. The STEPBible lexicon notes that the name means 'gods', a plural with an intensive, singular meaning when it refers to God. The Hebrew word is used of human judges in a few passages, and the KJV translates it 'judges' in Exodus 21:6 (verify the Hebrew there and at Exodus 22:8–9).

**Cannot be broken.** *Luthēnai* (G3089, John 10:35, V-APN), 'to be broken'. The STEPBible lexicon's senses run from 'to loose, unbind, release' to 'dissolve, break up, destroy', with John 2:19 ('Destroy this temple') under the latter. John uses the same verb of breaking the sabbath, *eluen* (G3089, John 5:18, V-IAI-3S), and of breaking the law of Moses (John 7:23). *Graphē* (G1124, John 10:35, N-NSF), 'scripture'.

**Sanctified and sent.** *Hēgiasen* (G37, John 10:36, V-AAI-3S), 'sanctified'. The STEPBible lexicon's first sense is 'to dedicate, separate, set apart for God', and under it, 'of persons: Christ', it lists John 10:36 and John 17:19; it notes that in the Greek Old Testament the verb chiefly renders the Hebrew root for holiness. The Son whom the Father consecrated speaks these words at the feast of the dedication, and he will later say, "And for their sakes I sanctify myself, that they also might be sanctified through the truth." (John 17:19) *Apesteilen* (G649, John 10:36, V-AAI-3S), 'sent'. *Huios* (G5207, John 10:36, N-NSM), 'Son'.

**Know, believe, and escape.** *Gnōte* (G1097, John 10:38, V-2AAS-2P), 'that ye may know', and *pisteusēte* (G4100, John 10:38, V-AAS-2P), 'and believe', the Textus Receptus reading. *Exēlthen* (G1831, John 10:39, V-2AAI-3S), 'he went out', from 'their hand', *cheiros* (G5495, John 10:39, N-GSF), the same noun as the Shepherd's 'hand' (John 10:28). *Emeinen* (G3306, John 10:40, V-AAI-3S), 'he abode', John's word for abiding. *Sēmeion* (G4592, John 10:41, N-ASN), 'sign', John's word for Jesus' miracles; John the Baptist did none.

**Textual notes.** These should be explained calmly, without scorn for either side; none of them changes any doctrine. (1) *John 10:8.* The Textus Receptus reads 'All that ever came before me'; a few early witnesses, including P75 and the first hand of Codex Sinaiticus, lack 'before me' (verify the witnesses). Both readings point to false shepherds. (2) *John 10:14.* The Textus Receptus reads 'am known of mine'; many early manuscripts read *and mine know me*, with an active verb (verify). The sense is the same: the knowing runs both ways. (3) *John 10:29.* The Textus Receptus, with the great majority of manuscripts, reads 'My Father, which gave them me, is greater than all'. Some early witnesses, including Codex Vaticanus (verify the witnesses), have a neuter form that can be read *What my Father has given me is greater than all*, and some modern editions print it (verify). The first reading grounds the sheep's safety in the Father's greatness; the second in the greatness of the Father's gift. Both end in the same sentence: 'no man is able to pluck them out of my Father's hand'. (4) *John 10:38.* The Textus Receptus reads 'that ye may know, and believe'; many early manuscripts read a second form of the verb 'know', which modern versions render *know and understand* (verify). The KJV's reading joins knowledge to faith, as John does throughout (John 6:69; 17:8).

### 🚷 Preaching Mistakes to Avoid
- **Information without life:** a sermon on sheepfolds, Palestinian shepherding customs, the Maccabees, and the Greek of John 10:30 that never brings the hearers to the voice of the Shepherd calling them by name, or to the hand that holds them.
- **Heat without light:** a sermon that spends its force denouncing the thieves, robbers, and hirelings of other churches and movements, and never lets the preacher ask whether he is a hireling himself (John 10:13).
- **Abundance as prosperity:** preaching John 10:10 as God's promise of health, wealth, and success, when the verse is followed at once by the shepherd's death (John 10:11) and the chapter ends in stones.
- **Security as licence, or security as terror:** using John 10:28 to assure people who have never followed Christ that they are safe, or preaching the warnings of Scripture in a way that leaves the true sheep afraid of the Shepherd's grip failing. The text joins the voice and the hand; so must the sermon.
- **Little gods:** using 'Ye are gods' (John 10:34) to teach that believers share God's nature or can speak things into being, when Jesus quotes a psalm that tells such 'gods' they will die like men (Psalm 82:7).
- **Allegory run wild:** assigning a separate meaning to every detail (the porter, the wall, the time of day) beyond what Jesus himself explains. He explains two things: he is the door, and he is the good shepherd.
- **The shepherd without the Lord:** preaching a gentle Jesus who is a model of care, without the claim that made them take up stones: 'I and my Father are one' (John 10:30).

## 🟣 Tier 4 — Christocentric, Humility, Life & Light
### 👑 Every Answer in Jesus
John 10 demolishes every ground of self-glory. Religious position is no ground: the leaders who claimed the right to judge the flock were thieves and hirelings (John 10:1, 8, 12). Self-reliance is no ground: we are sheep, who go astray and cannot find pasture or fight wolves by ourselves (John 10:9, 12; Isaiah 53:6). Our grip on God is no ground: the safety of the sheep is in the Shepherd's hand and the Father's, and the sheep contribute nothing to it but to hear and follow (John 10:27–29). Even our faith is no ground for boasting: Jesus says the sheep believe because they are his, given to him by the Father (John 10:26, 29). And religious certainty is no ground: the men who demanded that he tell them plainly were the ones who could not bear to hear it (John 10:24, 31).

And every good thing the chapter holds is found in him. The way in is in him, the door (John 10:7, 9). Life, abundant and eternal, is in him, the one who came to give it (John 10:10, 28). Care is in him, the shepherd who knows his sheep by name and lays down his life for them (John 10:3, 11, 15). Power over death is in him, who takes his life again (John 10:18). Unity is in him, the one shepherd of one flock (John 10:16). Safety is in him and in his Father, whose hands are one (John 10:28–30). And truth is in him, the one whom the Father set apart and sent, whose word stands with Scripture that cannot be broken (John 10:35–36). This is what Paul means when he says of Christ, "In whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." (Colossians 2:3) The leaders thought that the treasures of Israel, the temple, the feast, and the law, were theirs to guard; they were standing in front of the one to whom all of them pointed. The chapter humbles us by calling us sheep who could not keep ourselves. And it makes Christ glorious by showing the LORD of Psalm 23 walking in Solomon's porch in winter, surrounded by men with stones, and going on toward the cross to lay down his life for sheep who did not yet know his voice.

## 📚 Doctrine
### Theology Proper · One God, the Father and the Son
**Proposition:** The Father and the Son are distinct persons and one God. They are distinct, for the Father loves, commands, gives, sanctifies, and sends the Son (John 10:17–18, 29, 36), and the Son knows the Father as the Father knows him (John 10:15). They are one, for the Son's hand is the Father's hand in keeping the sheep, his works are the Father's works, and each is in the other (John 10:28–30, 37–38). The leaders rightly understood this as a claim to be God, and it is true.

**Textual Anchor:** "I and my Father are one." (John 10:30), with *hen* (G1520, John 10:30, A-NSN) and the plural *esmen* (G1510, John 10:30, V-PAI-1P); the charge 'thou, being a man, makest thyself God' (John 10:33); and 'that ye may know, and believe, that the Father is in me, and I in him' (John 10:38).

**Cross-References:** "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1); "Believe me that I am in the Father, and the Father in me: or else believe me for the very works' sake." (John 14:11); "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD:" (Deuteronomy 6:4)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The Son is a creature, one with the Father only in will and purpose.* Arius in the fourth century and Jehovah's Witnesses today hold this, wanting to protect the uniqueness of the one God. But a creature could not say that his hand is the Father's hand in keeping the sheep for ever (John 10:28–29), nor that the Father is in him and he in the Father (John 10:38); the hearers understood a claim to deity, and Jesus did not correct them (John 10:33, 36). (2) *The Father and the Son are one person who appears in different modes.* Sabellius in the third century and Oneness Pentecostals today hold this, also wanting to protect the oneness of God. But Jesus says 'I and my Father', with a plural verb, and speaks of the Father loving, commanding, giving to, and sending him (John 10:17–18, 29, 36); a person does not send himself or know himself 'as' another knows him (John 10:15). (3) *Jesus was a great teacher who never claimed to be God.* Many modern people hold this, wanting to honour Jesus without his claims. John 10 records his opponents taking up stones precisely because of what he claimed (John 10:31–33).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Worship of the Son with the Father, without embarrassment or hesitation. Confidence that when Christ holds us, God holds us: there is no gap between his hand and the Father's. And reverence for the mystery of one God in three persons, received from Scripture rather than reduced to what we can picture.

### Christology · The Good Shepherd Who Lays Down His Life and Takes It Again
**Proposition:** Jesus Christ is the good shepherd promised by God, the LORD who shepherds his people and the son of David set over them. He laid down his life for his sheep, on their behalf and in their place, by his own will and in obedience to his Father's command; no one took it from him. And he took it up again by the authority the Father gave him, so that his death and resurrection are one saving work.

**Textual Anchor:** "I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep." (John 10:11), with *tithēsin* (G5087, John 10:11, V-PAI-3S) and *huper* (G5228, John 10:11, PREP); 'I lay down my life for the sheep' (John 10:15); and "No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again. This commandment have I received of my Father." (John 10:18), with *exousian* (G1849, John 10:18, N-ASF).

**Cross-References:** "And I will set up one shepherd over them, and he shall feed them, even my servant David; he shall feed them, and he shall be their shepherd." (Ezekiel 34:23); "Now the God of peace, that brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus, that great shepherd of the sheep, through the blood of the everlasting covenant," (Hebrews 13:20); "All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all." (Isaiah 53:6)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Jesus' death was a tragic accident or the martyrdom of a good man overtaken by events.* Many modern historians and some liberal theologians hold this, wanting a Jesus who fits ordinary history. But Jesus says his life is not taken but laid down, and that he has authority to take it again (John 10:18); John shows him in control at the arrest and on the cross (John 18:6; 19:11, 30). (2) *Jesus' death was only an example of self-giving love, not a death in the place of others.* Holders of the moral-influence view, from Peter Abelard (verify) to many modern writers, want to stress the power of love to change us. The shepherd's death is an example (1 John 3:16), but it is first a death 'for the sheep', on their behalf, that rescues them from the wolf; Scripture says the LORD laid on him 'the iniquity of us all' (Isaiah 53:6). (3) *Jesus did not rise bodily; his 'taking his life again' means his cause lived on.* Some critical scholars hold this, wanting a faith independent of miracle. But the life he lays down is the life he takes again, the same 'it' (John 10:17–18), and John will show the risen body with its wounds (John 20:27).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Adoration of a shepherd who chose to die for us. Assurance that his death was not a defeat but a finished work he meant to do. And a pattern for every under-shepherd and every believer: to lay down our lives for the brethren (1 John 3:16), not to save them, which he has done, but to serve them in his name.

### Christology · The Door, the Only Way into the Fold
**Proposition:** Jesus Christ is the only door into God's fold. Salvation, safety, and pasture are found by entering through him, and the door is open to anyone who will enter. Every claimant to lead God's people who comes some other way, apart from Christ or in his place, is a thief and a robber.

**Textual Anchor:** "Then said Jesus unto them again, Verily, verily, I say unto you, I am the door of the sheep." (John 10:7) "I am the door: by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved, and shall go in and out, and find pasture." (John 10:9), with *thura* (G2374, John 10:9, N-NSF); and 'He that entereth not by the door into the sheepfold, but climbeth up some other way, the same is a thief and a robber' (John 10:1).

**Cross-References:** "Jesus saith unto him, I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me." (John 14:6); "Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved." (Acts 4:12)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *There are many doors to God, and every sincere religion leads to the same place.* Religious pluralists hold this, wanting to honour sincere people of every faith. But Jesus says 'I am the door', not a door, and calls those who came claiming to be what he is 'thieves and robbers' (John 10:7–8). (2) *The door is open only to a select few by birth, merit, or ritual.* Some forms of ethnic, moralistic, or sacramental religion hold this, wanting to guard holiness. But the door is open to 'any man' who enters (John 10:9), and the shepherd has 'other sheep' outside Israel's fold (John 10:16). (3) *A church or a leader is itself the door.* Some systems place a human institution or leader between the believer and Christ as the necessary way in, wanting to protect order and truth. John 10 makes Christ himself the door for the sheep and for the shepherds; every true leader enters by him (John 10:2, 7).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Clarity in evangelism: we point people to a person, not a program. Freedom for the anxious: the only condition at the door is to enter by Christ. And humility for leaders, who are never the door but only those who come in through it.

### Anthropology & Sin · Sheep Without a Shepherd, and the Unbelief That Will Not Hear
**Proposition:** Human beings are like sheep: they cannot guide, feed, or defend themselves, and apart from Christ they are exposed to thieves, hirelings, and wolves who use and destroy them. The deepest form of their condition is an unbelief that will not hear Christ's voice even when his words and works are plain, because they are not his sheep; and they remain responsible for that unbelief.

**Textual Anchor:** 'The thief cometh not, but for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy' (John 10:10); 'the wolf catcheth them, and scattereth the sheep' (John 10:12); "Jesus answered them, I told you, and ye believed not: the works that I do in my Father's name, they bear witness of me." (John 10:25) "But ye believe not, because ye are not of my sheep, as I said unto you." (John 10:26)

**Cross-References:** "All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all." (Isaiah 53:6); "And Jesus, when he came out, saw much people, and was moved with compassion toward them, because they were as sheep not having a shepherd: and he began to teach them many things." (Mark 6:34)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *People are basically able to find their own way to God if they are shown it.* Pelagians in the fifth century and many modern people hold this, wanting to affirm human dignity and freedom. John 10 pictures people as sheep who must be called, led out, and brought in (John 10:3, 16), and shows men who had every evidence and would not believe (John 10:25–26). (2) *Unbelief is only a lack of information; with enough evidence, anyone would believe.* Many apologists and seekers assume this, wanting faith to be reasonable. The leaders had the words and the works, and still took up stones (John 10:25, 31); the problem was not evidence but belonging and willingness. (3) *If some do not believe because they are not his sheep, they are not to blame.* Some hold this, wanting to protect God's fairness. Jesus holds the leaders responsible for refusing the works they saw, and appeals to them to believe (John 10:37–38).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Compassion for the lost, who are sheep without a shepherd (Mark 6:34). Humility in believers, who were no wiser than anyone else. And urgency in prayer and witness, since only the Shepherd's voice can bring a sheep home.

### Soteriology & Grace · The Shepherd Who Knows, Calls, and Gives Life to His Own
**Proposition:** The Father has given the Son a people from Israel and from the nations. The Son knows them as his own, laid down his life for them, calls each of them by name through his word so that they hear his voice and follow him, and gives them eternal life as a free gift. Their believing is the fruit and evidence of their belonging, and the door is open to all who will enter.

**Textual Anchor:** 'he calleth his own sheep by name, and leadeth them out' (John 10:3), with *kalei* (G2564, John 10:3, V-PAI-3S); "And other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring, and they shall hear my voice; and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd." (John 10:16); 'ye believe not, because ye are not of my sheep' (John 10:26); and 'My Father, which gave them me' (John 10:29), with *dedōken* (G1325, John 10:29, V-RAI-3S).

**Cross-References:** "All that the Father giveth me shall come to me; and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out." (John 6:37); "I have manifested thy name unto the men which thou gavest me out of the world: thine they were, and thou gavest them me; and they have kept thy word." (John 17:6); "According as he hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and without blame before him in love:" (Ephesians 1:4)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *God chose those whom he foresaw would believe, so that belief comes first and belonging follows.* Arminian theology holds this, wanting to protect human responsibility and God's fairness. Jesus reverses the order: they do not believe because they are not his sheep (John 10:26), and he has other sheep who have not yet heard (John 10:16). (2) *Christ's call is only an invitation that may always fail.* Many hold this, wanting to protect the freedom of the will. The Shepherd's call is personal and effective: the sheep 'shall hear my voice' (John 10:16), and they follow (John 10:27). (3) *Since God has chosen his sheep, there is no need to preach to everyone.* Hyper-Calvinists in the eighteenth century held something like this (verify), wanting to guard God's sovereignty. But the door is for 'any man' (John 10:9), and the other sheep hear through the word of those Christ sends (John 17:20); election is why we preach with hope, not a reason to stop.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Deep gratitude, because we were known and given before we knew. Assurance, because belonging rests on the Father's gift. And confidence in evangelism, because the Shepherd has sheep we have not yet met, and they will hear his voice.

### Soteriology & Grace · The Security of the Sheep
**Proposition:** Those whom the Father has given to the Son and whom the Son has called will never perish. They are kept by the hand of the Son and the hand of the Father, which are one, and no power in heaven or earth can snatch them away. They persevere in hearing and following because they are kept; those who finally fall away show that they were never truly his.

**Textual Anchor:** "And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand." (John 10:28), with *ou* (G3756, John 10:28) and *mē* (G3361, John 10:28) joined to *apolōntai* (G622, John 10:28, V-2AMS-3P), and *harpasei* (G726, John 10:28, V-FAI-3S); "My Father, which gave them me, is greater than all; and no man is able to pluck them out of my Father's hand." (John 10:29)

**Cross-References:** "And this is the Father's will which hath sent me, that of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing, but should raise it up again at the last day." (John 6:39); "They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would no doubt have continued with us: but they went out, that they might be made manifest that they were not all of us." (1 John 2:19); "Being confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ:" (Philippians 1:6)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *A true believer can finally fall away and be lost.* Arminian and Wesleyan theology holds this, wanting to keep Scripture's warnings real. The warnings are real, but they are means by which God keeps his sheep following; the promise itself is that they 'shall never perish', because the Father is 'greater than all' (John 10:28–29). (2) *A person who once made a profession of faith is safe no matter how he lives or whether he goes on believing.* Holders of the strong free-grace view want to protect assurance from works. But the sheep who are kept are the sheep who hear and follow (John 10:27); a profession that never follows gives no evidence of belonging. (3) *Assurance is presumption; no one can know he is safe.* The Council of Trent taught that no one can know with the certainty of faith that he has obtained God's grace, apart from special revelation (session 6, paraphrased, verify), wanting to guard against complacency. Jesus spoke John 10:28–29 so that his sheep would know it and be comforted.

**Pastoral Fruit:** Rest for anxious believers, whose safety is in two hands, not in their own grip. Seriousness about holiness, since the kept sheep are the following sheep. And perseverance itself, because a sheep that knows it is held is freed to follow without fear.

### Ecclesiology · One Flock, One Shepherd, and Faithful Under-Shepherds
**Proposition:** Christ gathers one flock from Israel and the nations under himself as its one shepherd, and its unity is found in hearing his voice, not in any one enclosure. Those who lead his flock are under-shepherds who must enter by him, feed and protect his sheep at cost to themselves, and give account to him; leaders who use the flock for their own gain, or abandon it in danger, are thieves and hirelings.

**Textual Anchor:** 'there shall be one fold, and one shepherd' (John 10:16), with *poimnē* (G4167, John 10:16, N-NSF), 'flock'; "But he that is an hireling, and not the shepherd, whose own the sheep are not, seeth the wolf coming, and leaveth the sheep, and fleeth: and the wolf catcheth them, and scattereth the sheep." (John 10:12) "The hireling fleeth, because he is an hireling, and careth not for the sheep." (John 10:13)

**Cross-References:** "Take heed therefore unto yourselves, and to all the flock, over the which the Holy Ghost hath made you overseers, to feed the church of God, which he hath purchased with his own blood." (Acts 20:28); "Feed the flock of God which is among you, taking the oversight thereof, not by constraint, but willingly; not for filthy lucre, but of a ready mind;" (1 Peter 5:2); "And when the chief Shepherd shall appear, ye shall receive a crown of glory that fadeth not away." (1 Peter 5:4)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *Christian unity means one visible institution under one earthly head.* Some have read 'one fold' this way, wanting to protect the church's visible unity (verify particular uses of the verse). The Greek says one flock under one shepherd; the unity is Christ's, and it crosses the boundaries of every enclosure (John 10:16; Ephesians 2:14). (2) *Every church or group is a separate flock with its own shepherd.* Some forms of independency and individualism treat believers as belonging only to their own congregation or to themselves, wanting to protect freedom. Christ has one flock, and all his sheep belong to it (John 10:16; 17:21). (3) *Pastors are employees who provide religious services to paying members.* A consumer view of church holds this, wanting efficiency and accountability. John 10 makes every leader an under-shepherd who must care for the sheep as his own charge and give account to the chief Shepherd (John 10:13; Hebrews 13:17; 1 Peter 5:4).

**Pastoral Fruit:** Love for believers in other churches and nations as fellow sheep of one flock. Gratitude and prayer for faithful pastors. And for every leader, a searching question: do I care for the sheep, or for what they give me?

### Christian Walk · Hearing and Following the Shepherd's Voice
**Proposition:** The life of Christ's sheep is a life of listening and following. They know his voice from all others because they have heard it in his word, they refuse to follow strangers, and they go where he leads, trusting that he goes before them into every pasture and every danger.

**Textual Anchor:** 'the sheep follow him: for they know his voice' (John 10:4); "And a stranger will they not follow, but will flee from him: for they know not the voice of strangers." (John 10:5) "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me:" (John 10:27), with *akolouthousin* (G190, John 10:27, V-PAI-3P).

**Cross-References:** "If any man serve me, let him follow me; and where I am, there shall also my servant be: if any man serve me, him will my Father honour." (John 12:26); "For even hereunto were ye called: because Christ also suffered for us, leaving us an example, that ye should follow his steps:" (1 Peter 2:21)

**Errors Refuted:** (1) *The Shepherd's voice is mainly an inner impression, to be sought apart from Scripture.* Some forms of mysticism and popular spirituality hold this, wanting a personal and immediate relationship with God. But in John the Shepherd's voice is his word, spoken and written (John 10:25; 20:31), and it is by his word that the sheep test every other voice (John 10:5). (2) *Following Christ is optional for the saved, a second stage for the committed few.* Some teaching that separates salvation from discipleship holds this, wanting to protect grace. Jesus describes all his sheep as those who follow (John 10:27). (3) *Following means doing whatever my conscience or community tells me.* Moral relativists hold this, wanting freedom from outside authority. Sheep follow a shepherd, not themselves, and they 'know not the voice of strangers' (John 10:5).

**Pastoral Fruit:** A daily habit of listening to Christ in his word before listening to anyone else. Discernment that recognises false voices by their difference from his. And peace in following, because the Shepherd 'goeth before them' (John 10:4) into every place he leads.

### Assumed Here
- **Pneumatology:** John 10 does not mention the Holy Spirit, though the hearing of the Shepherd's voice and the sheep's being brought to follow are his work. Scripture teaches it plainly in John 3:5–8 (the new birth by the Spirit), John 14:26 and 16:13–14 (the Spirit who teaches and glorifies Christ), and Acts 20:28 (the Spirit who makes overseers of the flock).
- **Eschatology:** John 10 speaks of eternal life and of sheep who 'shall never perish' (John 10:28), but it does not teach the last things directly. Scripture teaches them plainly in John 5:28–29 and 6:39–40 (the resurrection at the last day), 1 Peter 5:4 (the appearing of the chief Shepherd), and Revelation 7:17 (the Lamb who shepherds his people by living fountains of waters).

## ⚖️ The Council's Hard Questions
### ❓ Question 1 · I and My Father Are One: What Kind of Oneness
**Moderator:** 'I and my Father are one' (John 10:30) has been read in three very different ways: as oneness of will and purpose only, as oneness of person, and as oneness of being between two distinct persons. Which does the text support, and how sure can we be?

**Philologist:** Let us start with what the words themselves say, and no more. The numeral is neuter, *hen* (G1520, John 10:30), not the masculine that would mean *one person*, and the verb is plural, 'are'. So the grammar rules out the reading that the Father and the Son are one person. But I should be honest about the other side too. The STEPBible lexicon lists this verse, with John 17:11, under 'union and concord', and in John 17:11 Jesus prays that the disciples 'may be one, as we are'. The word by itself does not prove unity of essence. If we want more, we must find it in the context.

**Literary Scholar:** And the context gives it. The sentence is not an isolated saying; it is the last step of an argument about the sheep's safety. 'No man is able to pluck them out of my Father's hand' follows 'neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand' (John 10:28–29). The Son's hand and the Father's hand do the same thing with the same power. Then Jesus sums it up: 'I and my Father are one'. The oneness in view is at least a oneness of power and work that no creature could share. And the Old Testament language behind it is the LORD's: 'neither is there any that can deliver out of my hand' (Deuteronomy 32:39).

**Historian:** Then look at how the hearers took it. They took up stones, and they said why: 'because that thou, being a man, makest thyself God' (John 10:33). These were men who knew the Shema and would never have stoned someone for claiming to agree with God's will. In John 5:18 they had heard the same kind of claim as 'making himself equal with God'. They were hostile witnesses, but they heard a claim to deity, and in John 10:34–38 Jesus does not say *you have misunderstood me*. He defends his right to call himself the Son of God and repeats that the Father is in him and he in the Father.

**Church Historian:** The church fought over this verse for centuries. The Arians read it as oneness of will, and Athanasius answered that saints and angels agree with God's will but never say that they and the Father are one (verify). Sabellius and his followers read it as one person, and the church answered with the plural verb. Augustine put both answers in one sentence, saying that 'one' refutes Arius and 'are' refutes Sabellius (verify the wording). And I should add that Calvin, who held the Nicene faith firmly, warned that the ancient writers pressed this verse too hard, and read it as oneness of power with the Father in keeping the sheep (verify). He did not deny the Son's deity; he wanted the argument to rest on the whole of Scripture, not on one word.

**Theologian:** Calvin's caution is healthy, and it does not weaken the doctrine; it puts it on firmer ground. John 10:30 alone does not define the Trinity, but it stands inside a Gospel that begins 'the Word was God' (John 1:1), that will say 'he that hath seen me hath seen the Father' (John 14:9), and that ends with Thomas's 'My Lord and my God' (John 20:28). Read with those texts, the oneness of John 10:30 is a oneness of power and work that flows from a oneness of being. Two persons, one God.

**Resolution:** The grammar rules out the idea that the Father and the Son are one person, and the context rules out a merely moral agreement, because the Son claims to do with his hand what only the LORD's hand can do, the hearers understood him to make himself God, and he explains the unity as mutual indwelling. The word 'one' by itself speaks of union; John's whole Gospel shows that the union is that of the one God, Father and Son. "But if I do, though ye believe not me, believe the works: that ye may know, and believe, that the Father is in me, and I in him." (John 10:38)

### ❓ Question 2 · Ye Are Gods: What Is Jesus Arguing
**Moderator:** In John 10:34 Jesus quotes 'I said, Ye are gods' from Psalm 82:6. Some say he was backing away from his claim to deity. Others build on it the teaching that believers are little gods. Who are the 'gods' of the psalm, and what is Jesus doing with them?

**Historian:** There are three main readings of the psalm (verify each). The first, held by many Jewish and Christian interpreters and by Calvin, is that the 'gods' are Israel's judges, called so because they judged in God's name; the KJV itself translates the same Hebrew word 'judges' in Exodus 21:6. The second, found in later rabbinic writings, is that they are Israel at Sinai, who received the law and were addressed by God (verify the sources). The third, which some modern scholars prefer, reads them as heavenly beings in God's council whom God judges, and a scroll from Qumran seems to read the psalm that way (verify). The phrase Jesus adds, 'unto whom the word of God came' (John 10:35), fits the first two readings best.

**Philologist:** Whichever reading we choose, the Hebrew word is *elohim* (H430), the ordinary word for God, used here of beings who are not God. And the psalm immediately puts them in their place: 'ye shall die like men' (Psalm 82:7). The title is borrowed authority, not shared nature.

**Literary Scholar:** Then notice what kind of argument Jesus makes. It is from the lesser to the greater. If Scripture, which 'cannot be broken', can call mere men 'gods' because God's word came to them, how much less can it be blasphemy for 'him, whom the Father hath sanctified, and sent into the world' to say 'I am the Son of God' (John 10:35–36)? He does not say *I am only a god in the way they were*. He says *they were called gods because the word came to them; I am the one the Father set apart and sent*. And then he goes on to say more, not less: 'the Father is in me, and I in him' (John 10:38). The hearers did not take it as a retreat; they tried again to seize him (John 10:39).

**Theologian:** That settles the misuses too. Some teachers in the Word of Faith movement have used this verse to say that believers are little gods, with God's own creative power in their words (verify particular teachers), and Latter-day Saint teaching holds that human beings may become gods (verify). But Jesus quotes a psalm that condemns the 'gods' for injustice and tells them they will die. The verse cannot teach that we share God's nature; it teaches that God can lend a title of authority to men, and that the Son is infinitely more than they.

**Teacher:** In a group, the most useful thing is to read Psalm 82 aloud in full before discussing John 10:34. Once people hear verses 2 and 7, the 'little gods' reading falls away on its own. Then ask what Jesus' use of one word in a psalm shows about how he read Scripture.

**Resolution:** Jesus quotes Psalm 82:6, where God gives men who received his word and his commission a title of authority, and then rebukes them for injustice and reminds them that they will die. He argues from the lesser to the greater: if Scripture, which cannot be broken, calls them 'gods', he whom the Father consecrated and sent does not blaspheme in calling himself the Son of God. The verse neither lowers his claim nor raises ours. "Jesus answered them, Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?" (John 10:34)

### ❓ Question 3 · Not of My Sheep: Election, Unbelief, and the Open Door
**Moderator:** Jesus says, 'ye believe not, because ye are not of my sheep' (John 10:26), and speaks of sheep the Father 'gave' him (John 10:29) and 'other sheep' he already has (John 10:16). Does this mean that some people cannot believe because God has not chosen them? And what do we say to someone who fears that they are not one of his sheep?

**Theologian:** The Reformed reading takes the words in their order. Belonging comes before believing: the Father gives the sheep to the Son, the Son calls them by name, and they hear and follow (John 10:3, 27, 29). This is election and effectual calling, and it fits John 6:37, 44, and 65. The Arminian reading, stated fairly, holds that God gave the Son those whom he foresaw would believe, so that 'not of my sheep' describes people who have already, freely, set themselves against him. Both readings agree that those who do not believe are responsible for it. The Reformed reading seems to me to fit the order of John 10:26 and the language of 'other sheep I have' (John 10:16), sheep who belong to him before they have heard.

**Literary Scholar:** And the chapter never uses election to shut the door. The same Jesus says, 'by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved' (John 10:9). He appeals to men holding stones: 'believe the works: that ye may know, and believe' (John 10:38). The leaders are not pictured as would-be sheep turned away at the door; they are men who have heard and seen and refuse to come.

**Philologist:** Notice also the tenses. 'Ye believe not' is present: it describes what they are doing now, in front of him (John 10:25–26). Nothing in the verse says that they could never believe, and John records that some leaders later did (John 12:42; 19:39).

**Pastor:** This is where the question becomes personal. There will be someone in the group who hears 'ye are not of my sheep' and thinks, *what if that's me?* I would say to them what the chapter says: the sheep are those who hear his voice. If you are troubled that you might not belong to him, and you want to belong, that very wanting is the voice at work. The door is open; go in (John 10:9). No one ever came to the door and was told, *sorry, you are not on the list* (John 6:37). The people Jesus spoke to were not afraid of being outside; they were sure they were inside.

**Teacher:** I would keep the group's attention on the two sentences that belong together: 'ye are not of my sheep' (John 10:26) and 'if any man enter in' (John 10:9). Ask them to explain how Jesus can say both. Most will find their way to the answer: God's choosing is why anyone comes, and the open door is why anyone may.

**Resolution:** John 10 teaches that the Father has given the Son a people, that the Son calls them effectually, and that unbelief shows that a person does not belong to him; it also holds unbelievers fully responsible and sets the door wide open to anyone who will enter. The doctrine of election is given to comfort and humble those who come, not to discourage anyone from coming. "All that the Father giveth me shall come to me; and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out." (John 6:37)

### 🔦 The Life & Light Audit
**Pastor:** If a hearer obeyed this lesson exactly, then tonight they would pray John 10:27–28 with their own name, and put the Shepherd's voice before every other voice tomorrow morning. Within a day they would read those verses to one frightened or wandering believer, contact one person hurt or dropped by a church or leader, learn and use the name of someone they had never really known, and gently test one false voice by Scripture. What would come alive in them is rest: the rest of a sheep that knows whose hand holds it. And in others, hope, as someone who was scattered hears that the Shepherd has not let go. The dark place lit would be the hearer's own ear first: the voices they have been following that are not his.

**Theologian:** The practices keep source discipline. None asks the believer to be anyone's shepherd in Christ's place; the believer reads Christ's words, prays in his name, and serves as an under-shepherd who points to him. The Life practices rest on what the Shepherd has done and promised (John 10:3, 11, 16, 28); the Light practices turn first on the hearer's own listening and leading (Q25, Q26; the Inward practices) and forbid the use of 'thief' and 'hireling' as labels for other people. The Abundance Guard is kept by tying John 10:10 to John 10:11 every time it is quoted. And the security promise is never given without the call to follow (John 10:27), which guards against licence as well as fear.

**Teacher:** A Level 1 participant can do the Inward Life practice tonight: read two verses and pray them with their name. The Outward practices name the kind of person to reach (someone hurt or dropped by a church or leader; someone seen often but not known; a member who has been absent; someone new or on the edge; a person passing on a false teaching; a person hurt by a hireling) and leave a blank for the name. The Doing objective is one verse read to one person in one day.

**Fix:** The Audit found three risks, and the unit addresses them. First, a lesson on the security of the sheep can comfort the careless and frighten the tender; so every place that quotes John 10:28 also quotes or names John 10:27, the Common Errors table answers the licence error, the Debate Corner's Pastoral Warning addresses both dangers, the Modern Example shows the promise used to bring a wanderer back, and the Shepherding Notes name members who fear they have fallen away. Second, the words 'thief' and 'hireling' can become weapons against other churches and leaders; so the Light practices, the Preaching Mistakes, and the Leader's Map turn them first on the leader and the participant. Third, members hurt by abusive or absent church leaders may hear the lesson as an accusation or as a demand to trust leaders again; so the Shepherding Notes and a Life practice speak to them, and the chapter's distinction between the one Shepherd and all under-shepherds is kept throughout. The naming round reads only the second row of the Light Planner, never the private first row.

### ✅ Answer Key
**Q1.** The shepherd enters by the door; the thief does not enter by the door but climbs up some other way (John 10:1–2). The porter opens to the shepherd; he calls his own sheep by name, leads them out, puts them forth, and goes before them (John 10:3–4). The sheep hear his voice, follow him because they know his voice, and will not follow a stranger but flee from him (John 10:3–5).

**Q2.** They did not understand what he was saying to them (John 10:6). Jesus says he is the door of the sheep and the door (John 10:7, 9); anyone who enters by him shall be saved, and shall go in and out and find pasture (John 10:9). (Some members may add that he says all who came before him are thieves and robbers, John 10:8.)

**Q3.** The thief comes to steal, to kill, and to destroy; Jesus came that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly (John 10:10).

**Q4.** The good shepherd gives his life for the sheep (John 10:11). The hireling sees the wolf coming, leaves the sheep, and flees, and the wolf catches and scatters them; he flees because he is a hireling, the sheep are not his own, and he does not care for the sheep (John 10:12–13).

**Q5.** Jesus says he lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:15), that the Father loves him because he lays down his life that he might take it again (John 10:17), and that no one takes it from him: he lays it down of himself, with power to lay it down and power to take it again, by the Father's commandment (John 10:18).

**Q6.** There was a division among the Jews (John 10:19). Many said he had a devil and was mad, and asked why anyone listened to him; others said these were not the words of someone with a devil, and asked whether a devil could open the eyes of the blind (John 10:20–21), pointing to the healing of the man born blind (John 9:1–7).

**Q7.** At Jerusalem, at the feast of the dedication, in winter, as he walked in the temple in Solomon's porch (John 10:22–23). They asked how long he would keep them in doubt, and told him to tell them plainly if he was the Christ (John 10:24). He said he had told them and they did not believe, that his works in his Father's name bore witness of him, and that they did not believe because they were not of his sheep (John 10:25–26).

**Q8.** His sheep hear his voice and follow him; he knows them, gives them eternal life, and says they shall never perish and no one shall pluck them out of his hand (John 10:27–28). They are held in his hand and in his Father's hand (John 10:28–29).

**Q9.** Together they teach that the LORD himself is the true shepherd of his people (Psalm 23:1; Isaiah 40:11); that false shepherds feed themselves and scatter the flock (Ezekiel 34:2–4); that God promised to seek his sheep himself and to set one shepherd, David, over them (Ezekiel 34:11–16, 23); and that the risen Jesus is that great shepherd, over under-shepherds who must feed the flock willingly (Hebrews 13:20; 1 Peter 5:2–4). In John 10:11 and 10:16 Jesus claims to be both the LORD who shepherds and the promised one shepherd (John 10:11, 16).

**Q10.** As the door, he is the only way into God's fold and salvation, open to anyone who enters (John 10:7, 9; John 14:6). As the shepherd, he is the one who knows, calls, leads, feeds, and dies for the sheep (John 10:3, 11, 14). He uses both because salvation is both an entrance through him and a life lived under his care (John 10:9–11).

**Q11.** His death was his own willing act of love and obedience, not a defeat or an accident; men sinned in crucifying him, but they did not take his life from him (John 10:17–18). It shows he was in control, that his death was a gift for the sheep, and that he had power to rise. Pilate had no power over him except what was given from above (John 19:10–11).

**Q12.** Unbelief is not caused by a lack of plain words or evidence; they had both (John 10:25). Jesus says they do not believe because they do not belong to him (John 10:26; compare John 6:37, 44). The leaders show they understood him by taking up stones and saying that he, being a man, made himself God (John 10:31–33).

**Q13.** Either view can be defended; the phrase 'unto whom the word of God came' (John 10:35) fits both, and View A has the support of the psalm's own rebuke of unjust judging (Psalm 82:2) and of the KJV's use of 'judges' for the same Hebrew word (Exodus 21:6). What matters most is that both views read the argument from the lesser to the greater, and that neither makes human beings divine (Psalm 82:7). Jesus rested his argument on a single word and said that Scripture cannot be broken (John 10:35).

**Q14.** Peter had to learn that the Shepherd keeps the sheep, not the sheep the Shepherd: his promise to die for Jesus failed, but Jesus' prayer for him did not (John 13:37–38; Luke 22:32). Jesus found him and restored him, and even made him a shepherd (John 21:15–17). Our failures do not loosen the Shepherd's hand, and his restoring grace calls us back to follow (John 10:27–28; 1 Peter 2:25).

**Q15.** Carla sought a wandering sheep, gave her the Shepherd's words rather than her own, and stayed with her (John 10:11–13; Ezekiel 34:16). She pointed to his hand, not Denise's grip (John 10:28–29), and to his voice calling her back (John 10:27), without excusing the drinking. Members may name someone who is afraid, wandering, or discouraged.

**Q16.** The sheep are his own (John 10:14), bought by his life (John 10:11), held by him and the Father (John 10:28–29). Belonging to ourselves leaves us to keep ourselves; belonging to Christ means he keeps us (1 Corinthians 6:19–20). The catechism joins comfort and a willing life because the sheep who are kept are the sheep who follow (John 10:27).

**Q17.** Salvation rests on Christ's keeping and the Father's power, not on the strength of our hold (John 10:28–29; Philippians 1:6). That gives rest, not fear. John 10:27 describes the kept sheep as those who hear and follow, and 1 John 2:19 shows that those who finally leave were never truly his, so the promise cannot be claimed by someone who has no desire to follow (John 10:27; 1 John 2:19).

**Q18.** Answers will vary. The Shepherd who died for us knows us personally and calls us by name (John 10:3, 11, 14–15); he does not use us or abandon us as the thief and the hireling do (John 10:10, 12). Members may name times they felt used or abandoned by leaders, family, or friends (Psalm 27:10).

**Q19.** Answers will vary: a parent too busy for a child, a friend who disappeared when things got hard, a leader who served for recognition (John 10:12–13). The Good Shepherd humbles us by showing true care, and heals us by forgiving and staying with us (John 10:11, 14; 1 John 1:9).

**Q20.** Answers will vary: news feeds, a critical parent's voice, a favourite teacher, anxiety. A concrete step might be to read a passage of Scripture before any screen tomorrow, praying to hear Christ's voice first (John 10:4–5, 27; Psalm 119:18). Humbling ourselves means admitting that we are sheep who need to be led (Psalm 100:3).

**Q21.** Model answer, not the only answer: we resist being led when we want control, being fed when we are proud of our knowledge, being corrected when we feel we know best, and being kept when we want to earn our place (John 10:4, 9, 28; Isaiah 53:6). It magnifies Christ that the hand holding us is the hand that was pierced for us: the one who is one with the Father became the shepherd who died (John 10:11, 15, 30).

**Q22.** Model answer, not the only answer: a friend who left a church after a leader's abuse or neglect; a coworker used and discarded by a manager; a relative drained by a controlling relationship; a young person scattered by a group that dropped them (John 10:10, 12). Encourage members to write a specific name.

**Q23.** Model answer, not the only answer: safety, trust in any leader, belonging, a sense of being known and valued, and sometimes hope itself (John 10:3, 10, 12).

**Q24.** Model answer, not the only answer: Christ gives entrance and pasture, abundant life, his own laid-down life, and eternal safety (John 10:9–11, 27–28). We withhold his care when helping would cost us time, comfort, or safety; the Shepherd paid with his life (John 10:15; 1 John 3:16).

**Q25.** Model answer, not the only answer: a voice of fear, of a past relationship, of a teacher we have never tested, of our own pride, or of a culture that tells us who we are (John 10:5; 2 Corinthians 11:14). The honest answer belongs in the private first row of the Light Planner.

**Q26.** Model answer, not the only answer: asking for guidance about a decision while ignoring a clear command; wanting certainty about God's existence while not obeying what we already know of Christ (John 10:24–25; John 7:17).

**Q27.** Model answer, not the only answer: someone drawn into a prosperity teacher's promises, a controlling group, or a spirituality that offers peace without Christ (John 10:1, 8, 10). Carry light by sharing what the Good Shepherd has done for you, reading his words with them, and praying for them, without mocking them or the teacher (John 10:11, 27; Ephesians 4:15).

### 🗺️ Life & Light Leader's Map
**Worked Life Planner Row:** *Who:* 'Marcus, who stopped coming to church two years ago after the pastor who discipled him left under a cloud and nobody ever called him'. *What the thief has stolen:* his trust in anyone who claims to be a shepherd, his belonging, and his sense that God noticed when he disappeared (John 10:10, 12). *What Christ gives instead:* the good shepherd who does not flee, who knows his own by name, and from whose hand no one can pluck his sheep (John 10:11, 14, 28). *What I will do:* on Thursday evening, call Marcus, tell him I have missed him and should have called long ago, and ask whether I can take him to lunch; listen to his story without defending anyone. *Done?* Tick it when done, and write one line about how it went.

**Worked Light Planner Row:** *Where is it dark (second row):* 'my brother's fascination with an online preacher who promises that God will make him rich if he gives enough'. *What is hidden there:* a thief's promise dressed as a shepherd's, and money taken from a family that needs it (John 10:1, 10). *What light Christ shines:* the good shepherd who gives his life for the sheep rather than taking from them (John 10:11), and his warning about strangers' voices (John 10:5). *How I carry it there:* on Saturday, ask my brother to read John 10:1–13 with me and tell me which of the three figures the preacher sounds like; do not mock the preacher or my brother; pray for him afterwards.

**When Someone Has No One to Name:** Walk through their circles with them: the person at church who has stopped coming; the colleague who was pushed out of a team; the neighbour whose marriage ended; the young adult who has drifted from faith; the friend who was hurt by a leader. Remind them that the good shepherd 'calleth his own sheep by name' (John 10:3) and that he has 'other sheep' not yet in the fold (John 10:16). If they still cannot name anyone, ask them to pray tonight, 'Lord, show me one of your sheep who is scattered', and to bring the name next week.

**When Someone Points the Light at Others:** When a member names another church, pastor, or group as the thief or the hireling on their Light Planner, bring them gently back to the first row and to John 10:13. Say something like: 'Jesus said this to the leaders of his day, but the question for us is whether we care for the people God has given us. Let's start there.' Remind them that the Love Guard forbids using 'thief' and 'hireling' as labels to wound, and that if someone is truly being harmed by a leader, the right step is to care for the harmed person and speak to the right people privately, not to broadcast accusations (Matthew 18:15). If the member persists, speak with them privately after the session.

### 🐑 Shepherding Notes
- **Members hurt by church leaders.** John 10 is a chapter about false shepherds, and some in the group have met them: pastors who abused, controlled, used, or abandoned them. The chapter may reopen wounds. Acknowledge that Jesus himself condemned such leaders (John 10:1, 12–13), do not rush to defend anyone, and point them to the one Shepherd who does not flee. Do not pressure them to trust leaders again quickly. If any disclosure involves abuse, follow your church's safeguarding procedures.
- **Members who fear they have fallen away.** Some will hear John 10:28 and feel it cannot be for them, because of a sin, a long absence, or doubts. Read John 10:27–29 to them slowly, point to whose hand it is, and remind them that their very concern is a sign of the Shepherd's voice. Encourage them to come back to following, not to earn their place but because they are held.
- **Members who presume on grace.** A few may take the promise as licence. Do not argue in the group; speak privately, and set John 10:27 beside John 10:28: the sheep who are kept are the sheep who follow.
- **Leaders in the group.** Anyone who leads anything, a small group, a family, a ministry, will feel the weight of the hireling passage. That is right. Let it lead them to repentance and to the chief Shepherd, not to despair (1 Peter 5:4).
- **Members from other faith backgrounds, or with Jewish family.** Be careful with 'the Jews' who took up stones (John 10:31); explain that it means the leaders and crowd of that moment, not the Jewish people. Members from traditions that teach that Jesus is not God, or that humans can become gods, may hear John 10:30 and 10:34 differently; answer questions gently and patiently, and offer to talk after the session.
- **Members who feel unknown.** Some have never felt known by anyone. The line 'he calleth his own sheep by name' (John 10:3) can be deeply moving; let it land, and make sure the group uses everyone's name.
- **The Light Planner's first row.** It is private. Never ask anyone to read it aloud or to share it. If someone chooses to speak about their own heart, receive it with gentleness and pray for them; do not probe for details.

### 🎯 Teaching Angles
**For a Tier 1 Group:** Bring a blindfold. Ask one volunteer to wear it while three or four people each call their name from different corners of the room, one of them a person the volunteer knows well. Ask the volunteer to walk toward the voice they trust. Afterwards, ask how they knew which voice to follow. Then read John 10:3–5 and 10:27. Draw a large hand on a sheet of paper, have everyone write their name inside it, and read John 10:28–29 together. End by asking: 'Whose hand is holding you?'

**For a Tier 2 Group:** Trace three lines through the chapter. The line of the sheep: 'sheep' occurs 17 times in the KJV of John 10; list what the sheep do and what is done to them, and ask who acts on them for harm and who for good. The line of the voice: 'voice' occurs 5 times in the KJV of John 10 (John 10:3, 4, 5, 16, 27); ask how the sheep learn to know it and how they recognise a stranger's. The line of belief: 'believe' and its forms occur 7 times in the KJV of John 10, all in John 10:25–42; ask why belief and unbelief become the chapter's theme at the feast of the dedication, and where the chapter ends (John 10:42). Then read Ezekiel 34:1–16 and 34:23 and ask the group to point to every promise Jesus fulfils in John 10.

**Tough Question 1:** *If Jesus says his sheep can never be lost, what about people who seemed to be strong Christians and then gave up the faith?* Scripture gives the hard answer: 'They went out from us, but they were not of us' (1 John 2:19). We cannot see hearts, and some who wander are sheep who will be brought back, as Peter was (Luke 22:32; John 21:15–17). So we do not pronounce on anyone; we pray for them and seek them. But the promise of John 10:28 stands, because it rests on the Shepherd's hand and the Father's, not on how strong someone looked (John 10:29). See the Debate Corner.

**Tough Question 2:** *Doesn't 'Ye are gods' mean that we are divine too?* No. Jesus quotes Psalm 82, where God rebukes men who judged in his name and tells them 'ye shall die like men' (Psalm 82:7). His point is that if Scripture could call such men 'gods' because God's word came to them, it is not blasphemy for the one the Father consecrated and sent to call himself the Son of God (John 10:35–36). The verse lifts him above them, not us to him. See the Council's second question.

**Tough Question 3:** *Jesus says the leaders did not believe because they were not his sheep. So is it God's fault if someone doesn't believe?* No. Jesus holds them fully responsible: they had his words and his works and would not believe (John 10:25, 37–38). God's choosing explains why anyone comes; it never forces anyone away, and the door is open to 'any man' (John 10:9). If you want to come, come, and you will not be cast out (John 6:37). See the Council's third question.
